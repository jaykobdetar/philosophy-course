# Course 1 Reading reasoning and philosophical writing

## Scope and preparation

120 focused hours. No prerequisite. The course begins philosophical work immediately and teaches academic reading and prose alongside it. Its small set of recurring problems concerns definitions, the examined life, knowledge, and obligation. Formal proof is introduced in Course 2; this course teaches the ordinary-language distinctions needed to begin it.

The first-unit default can begin with the existing Jowett, Veitch, Gettier republication, Singer scan, and free skills guides. Quote the edition actually used, with its actual locators; a preferred modern edition should improve interpretation rather than become an unexplained barrier to beginning.

By the end, independently reconstruct a short argument; distinguish validity from truth and necessity from sufficiency; use a counterexample; interpret the assigned passages accurately; write and revise a focused essay; and explain an objection and response without a script. The essay, fresh-passage task, and dialogue jointly assess these outcomes.

## First four study sessions

These four ninety-minute sessions make a six-hour opening week if convenient; they can be spread across any number of days.

Session 1: Read OpenStax 2.5 “Reading Philosophy” for fifteen minutes. Spend twenty minutes reading Euthyphro from 2a to the start of 5d in the [<u>marked Jowett copy</u>](https://persweb.wabash.edu/facstaff/helmang/phi110-1516F/phi110-handouts/Euthyphro.pdf) (PDF pages 1–4, up to but not including the 5d marker) and identifying who speaks and what question is emerging. In twenty minutes, write a 120-word paraphrase of Euthyphro’s reason for prosecuting his father. Spend fifteen minutes distinguishing the speaker’s claim from your reaction. Explain your paragraph aloud or in text for ten minutes. Use the last ten minutes to save the untouched attempt, note difficulties, and identify the next task. This is diagnostic work, not a scored test.

Session 2: Read OpenStax 5.3 “Arguments” for twenty minutes. For twenty minutes, identify reasons and conclusions in four short arguments. Use these original examples: “Every borrower agreed to return the book; Mira is a borrower; so Mira agreed to return it.” “The lights are on, so somebody must be home.” “A rule applies to everyone only if it applies to the mayor; this rule does not apply to the mayor; so it does not apply to everyone.” “Many people like this painting, so it is a good painting.” Spend twenty minutes explaining which conclusions follow deductively and which need additional assumptions. Use twenty minutes to revise the first session’s paragraph around a single claim and supporting reason. Finish with ten minutes of recall and correction.

Session 3: Read OpenStax 5.2 “Logical Statements” and the validity/soundness examples in 5.3 for twenty minutes. Work for twenty minutes on these distinctions: a valid argument with a false premise; an invalid argument with a true conclusion; being a square as sufficient but not necessary for being a rectangle; paying an entry fee as necessary but not sufficient for winning a stipulated race in which only paid entrants are eligible to win. In twenty minutes, invent and explain your own examples. Take twenty minutes to write two versions of “All good explanations are simple”: one charitable argument supporting it and one counterexample. Finish with ten minutes checking whether the counterexample targets the stated claim.

Session 4: Read Euthyphro from 5d to the start of 8b in the [<u>marked Jowett copy</u>](https://persweb.wabash.edu/facstaff/helmang/phi110-1516F/phi110-handouts/Euthyphro.pdf) (PDF pages 4–7, up to but not including the 8b marker) for twenty minutes. Spend twenty minutes listing the first definitions and objections, using exact locators. Spend twenty minutes drawing a small argument map in a document or on paper. Use twenty minutes to write a 150-word explanation of why an example of piety may fail as a definition of piety. Finish with ten minutes of questions for the next tutorial. A tutor should diagnose at most two problems, not rewrite the paragraph.

If these tasks take longer, extend the opening period. The first six hours count within the modules below; they are not additional homework.

## Credit the opening sessions once

The opening sessions deliberately interleave orientation, reasoning, writing, and an early Euthyphro preview. They are the first six hours of the course, not a separate preparatory course. After Session 4, finish the remaining parts of Modules 1–3, then continue Module 4. Do not repeat an output merely because it appears again in the module description.

## Ordered modules

**1. Starting evidence and study setup, 4 hours.** Complete the diagnostic, save a sample paragraph, set up a simple source list and work folder, and discuss one question that genuinely interests you. Output: a baseline paragraph and a one-page learning plan. No public repository, account creation, or dashboard is required.

**2. Arguments and definitions, 8 hours.** OpenStax 5.1–5.5; the original examples above; necessary/sufficient conditions, counterexamples, and the [two-hour practical-reasoning packet](../../instruction/course-01/practical-reasoning.md). This packet uses two hours of the module's existing unassigned practice time, with no change to its eight-hour total. Output: six argument reconstructions, four definition tests, and a 200-word explanation of validity versus soundness. PC activity: rearrange premises and inspect what the argument requires.

**3. Paragraphs and source ownership, 8 hours.** OpenStax 2.2–2.6 and Pryor’s guide. Output: a quotation with citation, an accurate paraphrase, and an attributed inference; three 150-word paragraphs revised once. A tutor models one different paragraph before support fades.

**4. Euthyphro and explanatory priority, 14 hours.** Read the full dialogue, then focus on 5d–11b and 13a–15c. Output: a 400-word reconstruction of the dilemma at 10a–11b and a 300-word response. Dialogue: defend each horn in turn and distinguish what causes something to be loved from what makes it pious. Avoid assuming that the dialogue directly settles every version of modern divine-command theory.

**5. Apology and the examined life, 8 hours.** Read the whole dialogue in the [<u>marked CHS version</u>](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/primary-source/plato-the-apology-of-socrates-sb/); reread the displayed 20c–23c, 28b–30c, and 40c–41c sections. Output: a 500-word explanation of Socrates’ conception of wisdom, followed by one objection to the death argument. PC activity: separate historical claims, dramatic presentation, and philosophical argument in a three-column note.

**6. Meno and inquiry, 10 hours.** In the [<u>marked MIT translation</u>](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/es-113-ancient-greek-philosophy-and-mathematics-spring-2016/dacf28712c74d94ae88a474376a61cf0_MITES_113S16_Meno.pdf), read the main text from page 1 through section 86c, which begins on page 34, stopping before 86d, then 97a on page 53 through the dialogue’s end on page 60 (100c). Do not include the extensive footnotes as required reading. Output: reconstruct the paradox at 80d–e and distinguish what the geometry episode shows from what Socrates infers. Write 400 words comparing knowledge and true opinion. Do not describe omitted passages as read.

**7. Descartes and doubt, 12 hours.** Meditations I–II. Output: separate maps of the dream argument, the deceiver hypothesis, and the cogito, followed by 500 words evaluating one inference. Dialogue: what survives each stage of doubt? Use exact textual evidence rather than a slogan about “I think, therefore I am.”

**8. Gettier and counterexamples, 10 hours.** Whole article. Output: explain both cases, create a structurally different case, and test a proposed “no false lemmas” repair. Write a 600-word objection-and-reply exercise. A successful case must explain justification, truth, belief, and the role of luck.

**9. Singer and obligation, 12 hours.** Whole article. Output: reconstruct the stronger and weaker principles in the paper, separate the pond example from the general argument, and write 600 words on demandingness, distance, or collective responsibility. Dialogue: change one morally relevant feature and explain its effect.

**10. First sustained essay, 18 hours.** Choose one prompt: “Does the Euthyphro dilemma defeat the response you consider strongest?”; “Does a no-false-lemmas condition repair the Gettier problem?”; or “Does Singer’s argument impose an objectionably demanding duty?” Produce a 1,000–1,200-word essay through proposal, outline, draft, feedback, revision, source check, and revision memo. Make one narrow argument, not a survey of all views.

**11. Independent demonstration, 10 hours.** With texts visible but no content assistance, spend up to ninety minutes interpreting a fresh short passage from the assigned works and sixty minutes answering a new objection to your essay. Hold a twenty-minute discussion or accessible text equivalent. Use remaining time to review evidence and remediate one weakness. Word limits, rather than speed, govern extended writing when accommodations are needed.

**12. Delayed consolidation, 6 hours.** During the opening modules of the next course, complete two short retrieval sessions and a new-case application, then inspect and correct them. These six hours are charged to Course 1. They occupy the calendar week’s shared study budget and slow the next course’s progress; they do not remove any required hours or outputs from the next course.

## Readiness and remediation

Progress to ordinary core reading when you can attribute a passage correctly, give reasons rather than assertions, and compose an intelligible claim–reason paragraph on fresh material. Progress to advanced essay work only after the substantial essay and independent task show competent interpretation, reasoning, attribution, and organization. If a weakness remains, use the targeted bridge described earlier. Continue discussing philosophy while developing prose; do not confuse difficulty with academic conventions with inability to reason.

Optional enrichment: a lecture on Plato or Descartes after the relevant first reading, or another dialogue. No enrichment is required for assessment, and it is outside the 120 hours.
