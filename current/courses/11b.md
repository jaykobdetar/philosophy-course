# Course 11B Africana philosophy

140 hours; prerequisites Courses 1 and 8. This is a focused course on selfhood, freedom, personhood, and philosophical method in selected African and African-diasporic texts. “Africana” identifies an interconnected field, not one unified doctrine or an undifferentiated continent.

Core: Du Bois, The Souls of Black Folk, chapters I and III; Frantz Fanon, Black Skin, White Masks, chapter 5, “The Lived Experience of the Black Man,” Richard Philcox translation, Grove 2008; Kwasi Wiredu, “How Not to Compare African Thought with Western Thought,” in Richard A. Wright, editor, African Philosophy An Introduction, third edition, University Press of America 1984, whole essay; Ifeanyi Menkiti, “Person and Community in African Traditional Thought,” in Richard Wright, editor, African Philosophy An Introduction, third edition, 1984, whole essay beginning p. 171; Kwame Gyekye, “Person and Community in African Thought,” in Person and Community Ghanaian Philosophical Studies I, 1992, pp. 101–122; Charles Mills, The Racial Contract, chapter 2, Cornell 1997. Required context is Du Bois’s short “The Forethought” to Souls, plus the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry “Frantz Fanon”: opening biographical paragraphs before the Contents, then section 1 “The Problem of Blackness” through the paragraph beginning “In the end, Fanon is a unique thinker,” inclusive ([<u>https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/frantz-fanon/</u>](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/frantz-fanon/)). Do not assign the remainder of the entry or whole collection introductions as hidden extra reading. Wiredu and Menkiti are available in the same 1984 anthology; borrowing that edition avoids mixing pagination from later reprints.

Outcomes: distinguish phenomenological, historical, conceptual, and normative claims; reconstruct the person/community debate; interpret racialization and double consciousness in context; assess methodological risks in cross-cultural comparison; defend a bounded argument without treating an author as spokesperson for all African thought.

**1. Context and method, 14 hours.** Read Wiredu’s essay, Du Bois’s “The Forethought,” and the bounded Fanon context reading named above. Write 600 words on a bad comparison and how to repair it. Identify the language, region, historical setting, and kind of claim relevant to each author.

**2. Du Bois on self and social life, 20 hours.** Souls I and III. Interpret double consciousness and the education/politics dispute. Map the roles of narrative, history, and argument. Output: a 700-word reading with exact textual support.

**3. Fanon on racialized experience, 24 hours.** Read chapter 5 in full, returning to the finite SEP context passage as needed. Distinguish the lived description from the claims about social structures it supports. Compare a narrow point with Du Bois while preserving historical differences.

**4. Personhood and community, 20 hours.** Menkiti and Gyekye in full. Reconstruct their positions and identify where personhood is descriptive, normative, acquired, or presupposed. PC activity: test a single case under each interpretation rather than attach blanket “individualist” and “collectivist” labels.

**5. Institutions and critique, 16 hours.** Mills chapter 2, with Course 8’s chapter 1 available for reference. Write 700 words on an explanatory or normative claim and a serious objection. Separate what the evidence establishes from what remains a philosophical inference.

**6. Comparative-method workshop, 14 hours.** Return to Wiredu. Audit one of your earlier comparisons for conceptual translation, selective evidence, and overgeneralization. Revise the argument rather than just adding a disclaimer.

**7. Essay and revision, 24 hours.** Write 2,000–2,400 words on the Menkiti–Gyekye debate, Du Bois and Fanon on a specified problem, or a precise Mills argument. Include a checked specialist source and a method note explaining the limits of your scope.

**8. Assessment and delayed check, 8 hours.** Interpret an unseen assigned passage and answer a new objection. Readiness requires contextual accuracy and a genuine argument, not recognition of author names. Remedy unsupported generalizations by narrowing the claim and locating direct evidence.

Optional extensions include Hountondji, African feminist philosophy, Akan philosophy, decolonial thought, and philosophy in other African languages. These are substantive further studies, not competencies acquired by association.
