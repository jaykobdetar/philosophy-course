# Course 13B Comparative moral cultivation

Prerequisite: Course 11A and Course 6. Core primary passages: Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics III.1–5 and X.6–9; Mengzi 2A2, 6A1–10, and 7A1–21; Xunzi chapters 19, 22, and 23. Chapter 22 (“Correct Naming,” Hutton pp. 236–247) is included because the modern agency debate relies on its account of mind, approval, and desire. Retain the complete translations already selected. Read Bryan W. Van Norden, “Mengzi and Xunzi: Two Views of Human Agency,” International Philosophical Quarterly 32.2 (1992), pp. 161–184, and David E. Soles, “The Nature and Grounds of Xunzi’s Disagreement with Mencius,” Asian Philosophy 9.2 (1999), pp. 123–133, DOI 10.1080/09552369908575494. These offer different diagnoses of the disagreement; Soles does not claim there is no substantive difference. Treat their disagreement as something to establish through reading, not an invented opposition.

Central question: what relation between dispositions, deliberate effort, habits, and social practices explains ethical cultivation? First establish what each text argues in its own setting. Then identify a precise common problem and a genuine limit of comparison. A three-author historical summary is not yet a seminar argument.

Default paper prompt: “Does my proposed interpretation make the Mengzi–Xunzi disagreement substantive rather than merely verbal?” Alternative: “Can Aristotle’s account clarify one feature of cultivation without distorting the Chinese text?” Include a short translation note and one counterexample that tests your actual conclusion.
