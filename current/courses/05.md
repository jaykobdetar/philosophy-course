# Course 5 Early modern philosophy

## Scope and core readings

120 hours; prerequisites Courses 1 and 3, with Course 4 recommended in sequence. Study arguments about knowledge, causation, substance, and mind in their seventeenth- and eighteenth-century contexts. “Rationalist” and “empiricist” are useful retrospective labels with limits.

Read Descartes, Meditations I–VI, in Cottingham/Stoothoff/Murdoch volume II; Elisabeth–Descartes correspondence dated 6 May, 21 May, 10 June, 28 June, and 1 July 1643, Lisa Shapiro, The Correspondence between Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia and René Descartes, Chicago 2007; Spinoza, Ethics I, definitions and axioms, propositions 1–15 with demonstrations, and Appendix, Edwin Curley, A Spinoza Reader, Princeton 1994; Leibniz, Monadology §§1–39 and §§78–90, Ariew and Garber, Philosophical Essays, Hackett 1989; Locke, Essay Concerning Human Understanding I.ii.1–9 and II.viii.1–26, Peter Nidditch edition, Oxford 1975; Berkeley, Principles of Human Knowledge, Introduction §§1–25 and Part I §§1–33, Dancy edition, Oxford 1998; Hume, Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding sections II–VII and XII, Tom Beauchamp edition, Oxford 1999. Use stable locators, not a modernized aid’s page numbers, when quoting the scholarly text.

Outcomes: reconstruct Descartes’ main transitions and the circle worry; explain Elisabeth’s interaction objection; compare rival substance and ideas accounts; evaluate Berkeley’s idealism and Hume’s induction argument; distinguish historical interpretation from a contemporary repair.

## Modules and assignments

**1. Doubt, the thinker, and representation, 12 hours.** Reread Meditations I–II and read III. Trace what each stage has established. Output: an annotated argument map and 500-word account of one contested inference.

**2. God, error, and the world, 16 hours.** Meditations IV–VI. Write 700 words explaining the strongest version of the circle objection and a possible response. A tutorial asks what depends on clear and distinct perception and what depends on divine reliability.

**3. Embodiment and interaction, 12 hours.** Read the five dated letters. Create a correspondence map showing objection, reply, reformulation, and unresolved issue. Output: 500 words on whether Descartes answers Elisabeth’s question rather than a different question.

**4. Substance and explanation, 16 hours.** Spinoza’s assigned passages and Leibniz’s Monadology. Reconstruct one argument for a substantive metaphysical claim from each. Compare explanatory aims before evaluating their conclusions.

**5. Ideas and qualities, 14 hours.** Locke and Berkeley. Use an ordinary object to distinguish primary/secondary qualities and ideas/objects. Write 600 words assessing a premise in Berkeley’s argument, with a charitable Locke comparison.

**6. Hume’s challenge, 20 hours.** Enquiry II–VII and XII. Separate the skeptical argument about induction, the “skeptical solution,” the account of necessary connection, and mitigated skepticism. PC activity: map where a proposed defense of induction risks circularity.

**7. Essay and revision, 22 hours.** Write 1,800–2,000 words on the Cartesian circle, Elisabeth’s objection, Berkeley’s anti-abstraction argument, or Hume’s account of induction. Use at least two exact passages and one checked scholarly interpretation, selected through the reference protocol below.

**8. Assessment and delayed check, 8 hours.** Independent passage interpretation plus a comparison not rehearsed in the essay, followed by a brief defense and delayed reconstruction. If comparison becomes a list of slogans, return to a shared question and state each author’s actual premise and conclusion.

Optional enrichment: the remainder of the Ethics or Monadology and lectures on the period. Do not let optional whole-book ambitions displace revision.
