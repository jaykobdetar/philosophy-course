# Course 11A Classical Chinese philosophy

140 hours; prerequisites Courses 1 and 6. The default complete-text route uses Slingerland, Confucius Analects With Selections from Traditional Commentaries (Hackett 2003); Van Norden, Mengzi With Selections from Traditional Commentaries (Hackett 2008); Ian Johnston, The Mozi A Complete Translation (Chinese University Press and Columbia University Press 2010); Ziporyn, Zhuangzi The Essential Writings (Hackett 2009); and Hutton, Xunzi The Complete Text (Princeton 2014). Borrow one or two at a time as the course proceeds. The 2023 Readings in Classical Chinese Philosophy anthology is a useful alternative only where its actual contents cover every assigned passage; its inclusion of an author does not guarantee the whole range.

Core passages: Analects Books 1–4, 7, 12, 13, and 15, Edward Slingerland translation; Mengzi 1A1, 1A7, 2A2, 2A6, 3A5, 4A17, 6A1–10, 6B2, 7A1, 7A15, and 7B24, Bryan Van Norden translation; Mozi chapter 16, “Universal Love III” in Johnston (also rendered “Impartial Caring III”), complete; Zhuangzi chapters 1–3 and 6, Brook Ziporyn translation in The Essential Writings, Hackett 2009, or another identified complete scholarly translation; Xunzi chapters 1 and 23, Eric Hutton translation, The Complete Text, Princeton 2014. The finite common context is SEP “Chinese Ethics,” section 1 “Characteristics of Chinese Ethics: Practical Focus and Closeness to Pre-theoretical Experience” ([<u>https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/</u>](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/)), plus SEP “Mencius,” section 1 “Life and Confucian Background,” only its opening paragraphs through the sentence listing five Analects themes and ending “ethical cultivation” ([<u>https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mencius/</u>](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mencius/)). Whole translator introductions are optional. Consult passage notes where needed while reading each primary text; these belong to that author’s module. Stable book/chapter locators control the assignment; transliterations and English renderings vary.

Outcomes: reconstruct debates over cultivation, partiality, order, and knowledge; explain key terms in context without equating them automatically with familiar English categories; compare thinkers within the tradition; evaluate the philosophical effect of a translation choice; produce a narrow, source-grounded interpretive argument. This English-language course does not establish Chinese philological competence. A translation-sensitive judgment must compare two identified renderings of a short passage or use a translator’s explicit discussion; it evaluates the philosophical consequences of those renderings, not independently certifies fidelity to the Chinese. Use a lawful short comparison or commentary rather than buying a second complete book only for the exercise.

**1. Context and reading practice, 10 hours.** Read the two bounded SEP context selections above. Create a provisional Warring States context map and begin a glossary of ren, li, yi, de, dao, and xing. Add a primary passage and alternative rendering as each term is encountered in Modules 2–6; the ten-hour opening module does not require mastering all six in advance. Do not treat a glossary entry as a final definition.

**2. Analects, 22 hours.** Read the specified books. Choose four connected sayings and reconstruct an account of cultivation or governing. Write 700 words explaining how an aphoristic text can support a philosophical interpretation without forcing it into an imaginary continuous treatise.

**3. Mengzi, 20 hours.** Read the assigned passages. Examine the sprouts, human nature, and the extension of concern. Write 700 words on one argument and a textual difficulty. Revisit Aristotle only after representing Mengzi in his own terms.

**4. Mohist challenge, 16 hours.** Read Mozi 16. Reconstruct the argument for impartial care and a likely Confucian objection. PC activity: distinguish impartial concern, identical treatment, and absence of personal relationships. Identify which claim the text actually requires.

**5. Zhuangzi, 24 hours.** Read chapters 1–3 and 6. Trace the role of story, perspective, skill, and transformation. Compare two interpretations of one passage and explain their evidence. Do not assume that a story is either a literal doctrine or philosophically empty literature.

**6. Xunzi, 18 hours.** Read chapters 1 and 23. Reconstruct a disagreement with Mengzi, including the work done by “nature,” deliberate effort, ritual, and learning. Write 800 words that distinguishes disagreement from a translation-generated appearance of disagreement.

**7. Essay and revision, 22 hours.** Write 2,000–2,400 words: “Is the Mengzi–Xunzi disagreement primarily about motivation, development, or the meaning of nature?”; “Does Mozi’s argument require abandoning special relationships?”; or “What does a selected Zhuangzi passage justify about knowledge or perspective?” Include one specialist secondary interpretation and a translation note.

**8. Assessment and delayed check, 8 hours.** Independently interpret a fresh assigned passage, compare two thinkers without using a European thinker as the default measure, and defend a translation-sensitive claim. A subsequent cross-tradition comparison must state both a productive connection and a limit.

Readiness depends on accurate intra-tradition interpretation, evidence, and awareness of comparison limits. If the course becomes a list of equivalents such as “ren equals virtue,” return to contrasting passages and revise the glossary. Optional extensions include the Laozi, Han Feizi, Neo-Confucianism, and modern Chinese thought; completion does not imply coverage of them.
