# Course 8 Political philosophy

## Scope and core readings

140 hours; prerequisites Courses 1 and 6. Study legitimacy, liberty, distribution, gender, race, and the limitations of idealized social contracts. The course includes conflicting traditions and distinguishes interpreting a theory from endorsing its politics.

Read Hobbes, Leviathan chapters 13–15 and 17, Richard Tuck edition, Cambridge 1996; Locke, Second Treatise chapter II §§4–15, chapter V §§25–51, and chapter VIII §§95–99, Peter Laslett edition, Cambridge 1988; Mill, On Liberty chapters I–II, any complete reliable English edition; Mary Wollstonecraft, A Vindication of the Rights of Woman chapter II, Miriam Brody edition, Penguin 1992, or a complete public-domain text; W. E. B. Du Bois, The Souls of Black Folk, chapter I “Of Our Spiritual Strivings,” 1903; Rawls, A Theory of Justice, revised edition, Harvard 1999, §§3–4, 11–13, and 24–26; Nozick, Anarchy, State, and Utopia, Basic Books 1974, pp. 150–164, from the entitlement principles through the Wilt Chamberlain discussion; Charles Mills, The Racial Contract, Cornell 1997, introduction and chapter 1, pp. 1–40. Read the works in context; a critique of a tradition is not just an extra objection after its “real” theory.

Outcomes: explain legitimacy and consent arguments; apply and criticize the harm principle; reconstruct the original position and entitlement objections; interpret gender and race critiques as philosophical arguments; distinguish ideal principles from historical exclusion and defend an institutional principle with limits.

## Modules and assignments

**1. Authority and method, 12 hours.** Hobbes 13–15. Separate prudential motivation, moral obligation, and institutional authority. Write 500 words on what the state-of-nature argument assumes.

**2. Consent and property, 18 hours.** Hobbes 17; Locke’s assigned passages. Map the labor/property argument and the consent claim. Compare a specific premise rather than summarizing “Hobbes versus Locke” in general.

**3. Liberty and expression, 16 hours.** Mill I–II. Apply the harm principle to two carefully described cases, then explain the argument for hearing contrary opinions. PC activity: distinguish a claim’s truth, the right to express it, and the justification for a particular restriction.

**4. Equal standing and education, 18 hours.** Wollstonecraft II and Du Bois I. Write 700 words reconstructing one author’s account of a barrier to equal agency or recognition. Discuss interpretive risks in treating autobiographical, literary, and historical forms as mere illustrations.

**5. Fairness and entitlement, 20 hours.** Rawls and Nozick. Reconstruct the original-position argument and the Wilt Chamberlain objection; identify where principles are selected and where institutions are evaluated. Build a small numerical distribution example, explaining why arithmetic alone cannot decide justice.

**6. Contract and exclusion, 20 hours.** Mills introduction and chapter 1. Compare a descriptive claim about actual institutions with a normative claim about legitimate ones. Write 700 words on whether Mills revises, rejects, or repurposes the social-contract framework, with evidence.

**7. Essay and revision, 28 hours.** Write 1,800–2,200 words on a bounded issue: a consent argument, the limits of Mill’s speech argument, Rawls versus Nozick on a specified distribution, or Mills’s challenge to ideal theory. Represent the strongest relevant opposing view before defending a conclusion.

**8. Assessment and delayed check, 8 hours.** Analyze a new political case, identify which factual questions need evidence, and defend one principle under a changed circumstance. Remediate a factual/normative conflation before reassessing. Political agreement with a tutor is never a readiness criterion.

Optional extension: Rousseau’s Social Contract I–II, feminist critiques of the family, democratic theory, migration, and global justice. The Social Philosophy area option develops several of these themes in depth.
