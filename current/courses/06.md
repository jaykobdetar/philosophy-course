# Course 6 Ethics

## Scope and core readings

120 hours; prerequisite Course 1. This course develops normative argument, a limited introduction to metaethics, and careful application. It does not claim to exhaust moral philosophy.

Read Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics I.1–10, II.1–9, and VI.1–5, Terence Irwin, third edition, Hackett 2019; Kant, Groundwork, sections I–II, Ak 4:393–445, Mary Gregor and Jens Timmermann, revised edition, Cambridge 2012; Mill, Utilitarianism chapters II and IV, George Sher edition, second edition, Hackett 2001; Hume, Treatise 3.1.1, Selby-Bigge/Nidditch edition, Oxford 1978; A. J. Ayer, Language, Truth and Logic, chapter VI, second edition 1946; Judith Jarvis Thomson, “A Defense of Abortion,” 1971, pp. 47–66, whole article; Mengzi 2A6 and 6A1–10, Bryan Van Norden translation, Mengzi With Selections from Traditional Commentaries, Hackett 2008. The 2023 Readings in Classical Chinese Philosophy anthology is an alternative only if its actual copy covers every assigned passage. The Mengzi encounter introduces a different account of moral cultivation before the sustained Chinese course; it is not offered as a representative of all Chinese thought.

Outcomes: apply and compare virtue, duty, and utility approaches; reconstruct one canonical argument in detail; explain a metaethical distinction without confusing it with a normative conclusion; test a principle through a carefully described case; assess a serious objection.

## Modules and assignments

**1. Moral questions and reasoning, 10 hours.** Revisit Singer’s argument and read OpenStax 8.1–8.3 for vocabulary. Separate a factual dispute, a normative claim, and a metaethical question. Write a 400-word case analysis with assumptions explicit.

**2. Character and flourishing, 18 hours.** Aristotle I.1–10, II.1–9, VI.1–5. Reconstruct the function argument and explain practical wisdom. Create an example in which applying the mean requires judgment rather than arithmetic.

**3. Moral cultivation across contexts, 12 hours.** Mengzi 2A6 and 6A1–10. Explain the sprouts analogy and one rival interpretation of “human nature.” Write 600 words comparing an aspect of moral development with Aristotle while stating a limit of the comparison.

**4. Duty and autonomy, 18 hours.** Groundwork I–II. Reconstruct a universal-law test and distinguish treating a person as a means from merely as a means. Test the argument on a fresh maxim before reading a commentator’s response.

**5. Welfare and justification, 16 hours.** Mill II and IV. Compare quality and quantity of pleasure, then reconstruct the proof. Output: a 600-word objection and reply on “desired” and “desirable,” with the inferential claim made precise.

**6. Moral language and applied argument, 16 hours.** Hume 3.1.1, Ayer VI, Thomson’s article. Explain the is/ought passage and emotivism without assuming Hume and Ayer have the same project. Analyze one Thomson thought experiment, distinguishing the conclusion it supports from a broader conclusion it does not establish.

**7. Essay and revision, 22 hours.** Write 1,800–2,000 words defending a bounded verdict on one core argument or applying two theories to a precisely specified case. Prompts: “Can Kant’s universal-law test identify the relevant wrong?”; “Does Mill’s proof justify utility as the ultimate end?”; “Does Thomson’s argument establish the conclusion commonly attributed to it?”

**8. Assessment and delayed check, 8 hours.** Independently distinguish three theory types, interpret a new passage, and assess a changed case. Disagreement with a moral conclusion is not a grading error; unsupported interpretation or inference is. Remedy a theory-confusion by reconstructing its premises before revisiting applications.

Optional extension: contractualism, care ethics, error theory, moral realism, animal ethics, or environmental ethics. Social Philosophy below offers a complete further route rather than a vague instruction to “read more.”
