# Course 3 Ancient philosophy

## Scope and core readings

120 hours; prerequisite Course 1. This is a focused study of inquiry, justice, explanation, and the good life in selected Greek traditions, not the whole ancient world. The Classical Chinese option in Course 11 supplies sustained study of another ancient tradition. Every route includes the bounded early Buddhist encounter in Course 7 and Islamic/Jewish work in Course 4; the Africana option is predominantly modern and is not described as an ancient survey.

Read Plato, Republic I 327a–354c, II 357a–367e, IV 427d–445e, VI 509d–511e, VII 514a–521b, and IX 577c–580c; Phaedo 64a–69e and 72e–84b. Use Grube/Reeve’s Republic, Hackett 1992, and Grube/Cooper’s Five Dialogues for Phaedo; cite Stephanus. The no-purchase marked routes are [<u>Republic in Gutenberg number 55201</u>](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/55201/55201-h/55201-h.htm), chiefly Jowett’s 1888 third edition with Stephanus numbering incorporated from the 1908 edition, and [<u>CHS Phaedo</u>](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/primary-source/plato-phaedo-sb/), Jowett adapted by Gregory Nagy, Miriam Carlisle, and Soo-Young Kim. The local Gutenberg Republic number 1497 lacks those markers; use number 55201 for these bounded assignments. Cite the named version actually read. Read Aristotle, Categories 1–5; Physics II.1–3 and II.8; De Anima II.1–3; use the relevant translations in Jonathan Barnes, editor, The Complete Works of Aristotle, revised Oxford translation, Princeton 1984, with Bekker numbers. Read Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus, complete, preserved in Diogenes Laertius X.122–135, using the collection’s C. D. Yonge translation; and Sextus Empiricus, Outlines of Pyrrhonism I.1–30, Julia Annas and Jonathan Barnes translation, Outlines of Scepticism, Cambridge 2000. The primary Sextus text must be borrowed before its module; the collection’s Patrick volume is mainly a secondary study and also includes Patrick’s own translation of Book I; it is not the assigned Annas/Barnes edition. A modern Hellenistic sourcebook is optional, and anthology fragments do not replace the assigned whole letter.

Outcomes: interpret a dialogue’s competing voices; reconstruct Plato’s answer to the challenge about justice; explain Aristotle’s kinds of explanation and soul/body account; compare an Epicurean argument and a skeptical practice; defend a historically responsible interpretation using evidence.

## Modules and assignments

**1. Historical orientation and method, 10 hours.** Revisit Apology 20c–23c and Meno 80d–86c. Consult OpenStax 4.2 “Classical Philosophy” for Greek context ([<u>https://openstax.org/books/introduction-philosophy/pages/4-2-classical-philosophy</u>](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-philosophy/pages/4-2-classical-philosophy)). Create a one-page chronology separating approximate author dates, dramatic settings, and composition where the sources actually establish them; mark unsupported dates uncertain. Use OpenStax for broad orientation, and verify exact quotations in a primary text. Its incidental description of the opening sentence of Aristotle’s Metaphysics is inaccurate; the primary opening is I.1, 980a21 ([<u>https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/metaphysics.1.i.html</u>](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/metaphysics.1.i.html)). This quotation check is only needed if you use that remark, not an extra assigned book. Write 400 words on why a dialogue character’s assertion is not automatically Plato’s doctrine.

**2. The challenge of justice, 16 hours.** Republic I and II through 367e. Map Thrasymachus’ and Glaucon’s challenges separately. Output: a 600-word reconstruction of the strongest challenge and a written explanation of what a successful answer would need to show.

**3. Plato’s answer and its limits, 18 hours.** Republic IV 427d–445e and IX 577c–580c. Reconstruct the city–soul comparison and the relation between psychic order and justice. PC activity: identify every premise needed to move from an account of justice to the claim that being just benefits its possessor. This supplies the answer omitted from the old reading list.

**4. Knowledge and the soul, 18 hours.** Republic VI 509d–511e, VII 514a–521b; Phaedo 64a–69e, 72e–84b. Distinguish images from arguments. Write 700 words evaluating either recollection or affinity, including what the selected argument is intended to establish.

**5. Aristotle on explanation, 16 hours.** Categories 1–5 and Physics II.1–3, II.8. Classify the explanatory questions asked about one natural object and one artifact. Write a 500-word comparison of substance and attribute, then assess one argument about teleology without importing modern biological assumptions silently.

**6. Aristotle and Hellenistic alternatives, 14 hours.** De Anima II.1–3; Epicurus 122–135; Sextus I.1–30. Explain why “soul” need not mean an immaterial ghost. Compare Epicurus on death with Socrates’ argument in Apology, and identify what suspension of judgment is meant to do. These are bounded encounters, not complete courses in Hellenistic philosophy.

**7. Essay and revision, 20 hours.** Write 1,500–1,800 words on one question: “Does Republic IV answer Glaucon’s challenge?”; “Does the affinity argument support immortality?”; or “Does Aristotle’s soul account avoid the problems of the model it opposes?” Include outline, draft, feedback, revision, and memo.

**8. Independent assessment and later recall, 8 hours.** Interpret an unseen short passage from the assigned corpus with the source visible; explain one comparison without notes; answer a new objection to the essay; complete a delayed reconstruction in the next course. Ready means accurate textual work and relevant reasoning on new material, not memorizing every author’s dates.

If the city–soul argument or a technical term remains unclear, annotate the exact passage and compare two interpretations before retesting. Optional extensions include Presocratics, Stoicism, and the complete Republic; they are not hidden essentials.
