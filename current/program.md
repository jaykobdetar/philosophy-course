# Program guide

## Purpose and recommendation

This program is for an adult beginning formal philosophical study, with no college coursework assumed, who can protect five to seven focused hours each week. Its aim is sustained philosophical competence: reading difficult arguments accurately, reasoning with formal and informal tools, writing defensible positions, understanding historical and cultural contexts, and conducting a bounded independent inquiry. It is designed around writing, with conversation, interactive computer exercises, and optional audio as supports.

This handbook is the revised study plan replacing the earlier fifteen-unit outline. Keep the original collection as a source library; use the sequence, reading boundaries, and assessment rules here for the new program.

The recommended route combines a common foundation, a sustained global-tradition course, a selected area, an advanced seminar, and a research capstone. It is a complete self-study plan, not an accredited degree, a substitute for a whole undergraduate education, or a validated claim of equality with a university graduate. Philosophy majors differ substantially. The design borrows defensible features from several programs rather than treating a single university as the universal standard.

The biggest practical conclusion is that this is a multi-year education. Do not try to imitate a full-time undergraduate timetable in a few evening hours. Work on one main course at a time, maintain a small cumulative-review practice, and take the time required to revise your writing. Finish useful stages along the way rather than regarding everything before the capstone as unfinished preparation.

The compatible Chinese plus Aesthetics route reduces the total to 1,870 hours; the compatibility map below identifies combinations requiring an extra course. Optional extra courses, remedial bridges, and a longer thesis add hours; audio does not reduce the required total. Every module’s hours sum to its course total. Essay drafting, feedback, revision, assessment, and delayed checks are already included. A delayed check done during the next course is charged to the prior course and occupies that week’s shared study budget. The next course advances more slowly; none of its required hours or outputs is deleted.

At 1,890 hours, five hours a week means about 378 study weeks; six means 315; seven means 270. With forty-six study weeks a year, that is approximately 8.2, 6.8, or 5.9 calendar years. These are arithmetic scenarios, not deadlines. At forty study weeks a year, the corresponding figures are 9.5, 7.9, and 6.8 years. Waiting for a library loan does not consume focused study hours but can extend elapsed time. Actual reading and writing pace may change the estimate substantially.

Useful milestones are foundations after Courses 1–2 (270 hours); initial historical and ethical competence after Course 6 (730 cumulative hours); the common breadth spine after Course 10 (1,270); depth and research preparation after Course 13 (1,710 on the default route); and the complete portfolio after Course 14 (1,890). Each milestone has useful outcomes in its own right. There is no need to claim full-program completion to value the work already done.

## Prerequisites and flexibility

The numbered sequence is a convenient default. The real dependencies are conceptual: argument analysis before most essays; ordinary-language logic before formalization; truth-functional and first-order semantics before technical language work; Descartes and Hume before the central Kant problems; ethical reasoning before advanced justice; contextual competence before cross-cultural comparison; and research practice before the capstone.

You may study history or ethics while still improving formal proofs. Full first-order proof proficiency is not a gate against all philosophical reading. Keep one main course at a time at this weekly budget; if you run logic alongside another subject, divide the same hours rather than doubling them. A learner may demonstrate prerequisites through fresh independent tasks instead of repeating already mastered material, but the assessment evidence must be recorded.

## What the benchmarks justify

The institutional and pedagogical evidence ledger at the end distinguishes three kinds of statement: a university’s actual requirement, a research finding, and a local design choice. The following are common patterns, not universal rules:

> • Breadth across history, value, and theoretical philosophy, with formal reasoning somewhere in the curriculum
>
> • A transition from introductory courses to demanding reading and advanced seminars
>
> • Repeated philosophical writing, criticism, and revision
>
> • Some opportunity for independent work; an extensive thesis is one route, not a requirement of every strong philosophy major
>
> • Significant differences between a US philosophy major within a broader degree and a UK whole-subject degree

University course counts alone are a poor comparison. An eight-course major with junior independent papers and a senior thesis can entail more work than the number eight suggests. Requirements also sit within institutions that provide faculty, peers, libraries, prerequisite courses, and assessment. This plan supplies explicit tasks and a coherent structure; it cannot quietly manufacture those institutional conditions.

## What completion means here

Keep a portfolio of work and an evidence record. Completion means that you have finished the required route, can meet its stated learning outcomes on independent work, have corrected serious misunderstandings, and can explain and defend the final project. It does not mean that an AI’s numerical score certifies university-level attainment.

Use two descriptions accurately:

> • Self-study completion: the work and readiness checks are complete, but assessment is principally self- and AI-assisted
>
> • Externally reviewed completion: a suitably qualified human has reviewed selected work against the stated criteria and documented strengths and remaining weaknesses

Neither description awards credit. A single human review cannot certify every topic, and several AI models agreeing does not create independent faculty assessment. The evidence record should say what was reviewed, by whom, and with what limits.

## Using the plan

Start with Course 1. Read its first-session instructions before buying books. Do not acquire the entire library in advance. Before each course, verify access to that course’s finite core reading set and record the editions you will use. Optional readings never become unstated prerequisites. If a core work is inaccessible, resolve the named fallback or borrowing route before the relevant module; do not silently replace it with an AI summary.

A module is a work package, not a calendar week. At six hours per week, a twelve-hour module normally spans two weeks. Courses specify outputs as well as readings, so you can stop and resume without designing the next assignment from scratch. Dates and deadlines are unnecessary. Reading ahead is welcome, but advanced assessment assumes the stated prerequisites.

## Workload and pace

Every estimate below includes focused reading and rereading, notes, exercises, tutorials or self-dialogue, drafting, feedback review, revision, assessment, and cumulative review. The totals are planning estimates, not credit conversions or guarantees of quality. The first two courses provide a calibration period. Record actual time in fifteen-minute increments for a few modules, then adjust later estimates without cutting essential learning outcomes.

Run a pacing check during the first four to six weeks and again at the first dense module in a new stage. Record pages understood rather than merely scanned, time spent reconstructing, draft words that survive revision, and help needed. After two contrasting sessions, project the remaining assigned pages and writing separately. For planning sensitivity, forty dense pages at four pages per hour require ten reading hours before exercises or revision; at two pages per hour they require twenty. These are illustrative rates, not claims about you or universal norms. The advanced seminar now gives forty-four hours to core analysis because a twenty-eight-hour bundle left too little room for its four reconstructions and maps. If the core cannot be read responsibly within an estimate, extend the module and update the overall projection. Optional enrichment is the first thing to remove; required arguments are not silently cut to protect a tidy total.

Use the following six-hour weekly rhythm as a starting point:

> • 120 minutes close reading and source notes, in two sessions
>
> • 60 minutes argument reconstruction, logic, or a computer-based exercise
>
> • 100 minutes drafting or revising the current written task
>
> • 40 minutes discussion, oral explanation, or a tutor session
>
> • 25 minutes retrieval from earlier modules and correction of errors
>
> • 15 minutes planning, source checks, and recording the next step

At five hours, reduce the size of that week’s reading/task package and carry the remaining hour forward. At seven hours, add an hour of reading or revision. Do not remove revision, postpone all writing until the end, or count the same activity twice. Logic-heavy weeks move time from prose writing to problems; essay weeks move it from new reading to drafting.

Optional listening during night work is enrichment. Use familiar material or a lecturer’s overview, and only where listening is safe and compatible with work. Do not operate machinery, drive, or undertake safety-critical work while doing demanding philosophical exercises. Distracted audio does not count toward the focused workload and does not replace primary-text reading. Afterwards, an optional five-minute note can record one argument you remember and one question to revisit when rested. There is no required audio quota.

## The study cycle

**1. Orient.** Read a short historical/context note and the module question. Identify unfamiliar terms. A beginner may see one brief worked example before attempting a different task.

**2. Read.** First locate the question and conclusion; then reread the relevant passage, noting reasons and qualifications. Avoid transcribing the whole text.

**3. Reconstruct.** Put the argument into your own words, mark implied premises, and distinguish the author’s claim from your charitable repair.

**4. Test.** Try a counterexample, an alternative interpretation, a conflicting case, or a rival argument. Identify exactly which premise or inference is challenged.

**5. Write.** Make a bounded claim, support it with reasons and textual evidence, and address the strongest relevant objection.

**6. Discuss.** Explain the argument without reading a script. Ask for an objection that targets your actual claim, then revise if necessary.

**7. Check.** Consult a scholarly source and verify quotations, locators, and attribution against the primary text. Record whether the secondary source changed your reading.

**8. Revise.** Make substantive changes before polishing style. Explain the two most important changes in a short revision memo.

**9. Retrieve.** Later, reconstruct the key idea without notes and apply it to a fresh case. Reopen the text afterwards to correct errors.

This is a flexible teaching sequence. It does not require unnecessary struggle with an unexplained technical term or forbid a useful lecture before reading. Support is gradually reduced as you gain skill.

## Academic tools for beginners

Course 1 teaches these practices in context. A lack of prior college credits is not a reason to delay philosophical work.

> • Distinguish an argument from a report, illustration, explanation, or rhetorical question
>
> • Paraphrase a short passage accurately and separate it from a quotation
>
> • Find a claim’s supporting reason and identify an unstated assumption
>
> • Use necessary and sufficient conditions, validity, soundness, counterexamples, and charity
>
> • Write a paragraph with a specific claim, evidence, and explanation
>
> • Cite a passage precisely and maintain a bibliographic record
>
> • Read a scholarly article’s introduction, argumentative structure, and conclusion without assuming every detail is equally important
>
> • Explain the difference between empirical evidence and a philosophical premise

When difficulty persists, add a targeted bridge of six to twelve focused hours. For reading, work through one paragraph at a time with vocabulary glosses and three paraphrases. For writing, develop three 150-word claim–reason–objection paragraphs with feedback. For basic quantitative reasoning, practise percentages, conditional probability, and interpreting a small table before the science course’s probability module. These are extra hours when needed, not hidden prerequisites or admissions tests.

## Writing that develops across the program

Course 1 moves from 150-word paraphrases to 300–500-word reconstructions, then to a 1,000–1,200-word essay. Early core courses use 600–800-word interpretive exercises and 1,500–1,800-word revised essays. Middle courses add comparison and source evaluation, generally culminating in 1,800–2,200 words. Advanced work uses a 2,500–3,500-word seminar paper. The capstone is a 5,000–7,000-word independent research paper with a bounded original contribution, such as a refined objection, a justified distinction, or a more accurate interpretation. A longer thesis is an optional extension, not evidence of quality by length alone.

For each substantial paper, retain the proposal, outline, first draft, feedback, revision, source list, and a 200-word revision memo. Do not submit several near-identical essays merely to increase the count. One paper revised seriously is often more educational than three unexamined drafts, but revision is paired with later independent work to show transfer.

The core paragraph template is temporary scaffolding: “I argue that … because … . A serious objection is … . This matters because … . My reply is … .” Mature work need not sound formulaic. Interpretive and comparative essays may need a more complex structure, but clarity about the question, evidence, and inferential steps remains essential.

## Feedback and readiness

The criteria are stable; the expected complexity rises. Evaluate six dimensions:

1\. Question and thesis: a precise answer to a genuine problem, with scope appropriate to the assignment

2\. Interpretation and context: an accurate, charitable account of the relevant position, supported by exact passages and without anachronism

3\. Reasoning: explicit reasons that support the conclusion; important assumptions, distinctions, and counterexamples handled responsibly

4\. Objection and reply: a substantial challenge to the actual argument and a responsive answer or justified revision

5\. Evidence and research: traceable citations, verified claims, suitable sources, and a clear distinction between evidence and inference

6\. Organization and expression: a reader can follow the argument; terms remain consistent; prose does not hide gaps in reasoning

Use the descriptors “not yet demonstrated,” “developing with support,” “independently competent,” and “strong and transferable.” “Independently competent” means the work succeeds on the assignment’s essential tasks without substantive coaching, though it may contain minor errors and need stylistic revision. Serious source fabrication, a central misreading, an unsupported central inference, or inability to explain one’s own paper prevents readiness even if the prose is excellent.

A normal course has three sources of evidence: a revised paper, an independent short task or problem set on a fresh passage/case, and an unscripted explanation or accessible equivalent. A delayed retrieval/transfer task in a later course checks retention. For each essential outcome group in the course assessment blueprint, retain one satisfactory module artifact and one independently competent fresh task. The module artifact may be supported practice, with the assistance recorded; the independent task must be completed without content assistance. A paper supplies depth but cannot stand in for unaddressed outcome groups. This evidence rule is a local design choice, not an empirically validated cutoff or university grading standard.

Written tasks are open-text when interpretation matters, but closed to AI assistance. Short recall checks can be closed-note. Formal examinations should say exactly which sources and aids are permitted. Oral work may be recorded or documented with consent; a live text conversation or additional timed written questions can provide an accessibility alternative, with its limits noted. Anxiety, accent, typing speed, or disability should not be mistaken for philosophical weakness.

If an outcome is not ready, name the problem, assign two or three targeted exercises, and repeat only the relevant component with a fresh prompt. Keep prior attempts. No penalty is needed. Do not force a full course restart because one proof technique or textual interpretation needs work.

## Cumulative review and returning after a break

After a module, make one pivotal retrieval prompt by default. Add at most two more only for fragile concepts: an argument, distinction, or application. Revisit it in the next study cycle, a few weeks later, and in a later course. Use the weekly review slot for the oldest or least secure prompt, not every accumulated card. Merge redundant prompts and retire a prompt after a successful later-course transfer, keeping only occasional cumulative sampling. A failed recall gets correction against the source and a focused later retest; it does not create a growing catch-up debt. Adjust intervals to actual recall. These are practical heuristics, not a scientific formula for philosophy.

After a long break, spend one session reconstructing the last course’s central questions and reread your last revision memo. Attempt two representative tasks. Resume from the first missing skill or unfinished module. A forgotten label does not require repeating work you can still explain and apply.

## Interactive work on a computer

Use ordinary documents or an offline diagram tool for argument maps. Each node should contain a complete proposition, with arrows showing support or objection. The map is valuable only if you can explain the relation in prose. Use a spreadsheet for small decision tables and probability examples; a local text file works equally well. For logic, use the selected proof system’s exercises and a compatible proof checker, while also producing unaided solutions on paper or in a plain document.

No custom server, API key, coding environment, or new software installation is a prerequisite. Optional tools are replaceable. An interactive activity always has a low-technology equivalent so that tool setup does not become an unplanned part of the curriculum.

For every interactive activity, save a small four-part record: the input passage or case; what you changed; the resulting map, table, or proof; and a two-sentence explanation of what the change shows and what it does not show. Then check the result against the source or formal rule. A paper outline, hand-drawn table, or written proof is the low-technology equivalent. The activity is complete when the reasoning is inspectable, not when an app awards a point.

For a solo five-minute explanation, spend one minute stating the question and claim, one giving the strongest reason, one stating the strongest objection, one answering or narrowing the claim, and one naming an uncertainty and next check. You may record it privately if useful. The accessible written alternative is five short paragraphs with the same functions; do not treat speaking speed or performance polish as philosophical evidence.

## AI coaching and assessment limits

The learner writes the assessed arguments. An AI may ask questions, define a term, show a different worked example, diagnose a draft, suggest a passage to reread, or generate a clearly labelled practice problem. It may not write or rewrite the learner’s assessed paragraphs, invent quotations, provide a gate answer, or grade confidently when it cannot inspect the source.

For a tutoring session, supply the course, module, exact edition/passage, your attempt, the time available, and the requested kind of help. Ask the tutor to identify one or two consequential issues first, point to evidence, and let you attempt a repair before giving more assistance. For historical or global traditions, require attention to translation, context, interpretive disagreement, and the limits of comparison.

Keep a brief assistance declaration with assessed work: tools used, kinds of help, and what was done independently. Use a fresh prompt and, where feasible, a separate assessment session for independent tasks. This reduces immediate answer leakage but does not make the model independent or reliable. Automated proof checking verifies formal steps under a defined calculus; it does not establish that the English argument was faithfully formalized.

Source verification means opening the cited edition and checking that it supports the claim. A second AI repeating the citation is not verification. When uncertain, mark the claim unresolved and consult the text, an authoritative reference, or a human specialist. Never treat a fluent invented quotation as a memory aid.

## Human dialogue and external feedback

Peer discussion supplies perspectives and exposes ambiguities; subject expertise improves interpretation and assessment. They perform different jobs. A practical low-cost option is a reading partner or small group that meets periodically to exchange short papers and discuss the assigned question. A stronger option is occasional paid or volunteer feedback from a philosophy instructor, doctoral researcher, or subject specialist, with scope, confidentiality, fees, and qualifications checked first. Audited courses, university extension, public philosophy groups, and library services may help, but access and offerings must be checked locally.

A useful review request includes the actual source passage, assignment, rubric, your draft, and two questions about it. Ask the reviewer to identify the most consequential error, the strongest objection, and a concrete revision task. Suggested checkpoints are the end of the methods course, the first middle-stage essay, the advanced seminar, and the capstone proposal and final paper. These are recommendations, not appointments, promised availability, or purchases.

If no human review is available, proceed with clearly labelled self-study evidence and a skeptical verification routine. Preserve work so that later external review is possible. Do not present unreviewed completion as independently validated expertise.
