## Teach the dispute

Read this as a dialogue. Demea's emphasis on misery supports a religious posture of dependence, while Philo can deploy similar observations against Cleanthes's inference to humanlike divine goodness. Agreement on an observation therefore need not be agreement on the conclusion. Do not equate every statement of Philo with an unqualified authorial declaration by Hume.

One challenge combines power, benevolence and the existence of suffering. If an agent could prevent a harm and goodness required preventing it, its occurrence appears to threaten that combination. The condition about what goodness requires does substantive work: a defender may argue that some goods are inseparable from risks or that preventing one evil permits a worse one. Merely naming “greater good” does not show that the actual evil is necessary for it. Keep logical possibility, an explanation of a particular evil, and evidence for a general attribution of goodness separate.

The end of Part X shifts the burden: even granting that evil is compatible with perfect goodness, can mixed effects establish pure, unlimited goodness? A compatibility defense responds to an allegation of contradiction. It does not by itself turn the observed mixture into evidence for the attributed perfection. This makes the challenge more demanding than a simple “evil exists, therefore no God” slogan, and more precise about what it targets.

The **serious response** is Cleanthes's finite-perfection proposal in D11.1. Reconstruct it charitably: a vastly superior yet limited benevolent intelligence may choose a lesser harm to avoid a greater one, or tolerate an inconvenience necessary for a desirable end. Its ability is constrained, so the expectation that all suffering could simply be removed no longer follows. This is a real change to the inference, not an evasive refusal to reason. Its cost is equally explicit: it abandons the infinite-attribute claim being defended earlier, and still owes evidence that the observed harms are necessary or proportionate under its proposed limitations. Assessing that cost does not require deciding anyone's religious commitment.

Philo's D11.2 response distinguishes two epistemic positions: someone independently assured of a benevolent source may interpret surprising effects in light of that assurance; someone trying to infer benevolence from those effects cannot treat ignorance of the larger plan as positive evidence for it. D11.12 preserves a compatibility concession while pressing this inferential limit. In the intervening discussion, pain, general laws, limited capacities, and poorly balanced natural arrangements supply different explanatory targets. A reply relevant to deliberate wrongdoing is not automatically a reply to disease or animal suffering. The packet evaluates relevance and adequacy without demanding that a learner recount personal suffering.

## Worked practice and output PA30

Invented, nonreligious model: a designer installs a warning alarm that sometimes produces discomfort. The designer argues that some warning is needed to avoid larger harms. This supplies a possible purpose for discomfort. It does not show that every intensity or duration is necessary, that the designer could not use another system, or that the design proves unlimited benevolence. A finite engineer's constraints may support a qualified defense that would be weaker for an unconstrained designer. The example explains the distinctions; it is not evidence about the universe.

Make a dialogue map with four rows: D10.24–25 challenge; D10.35 concession and remaining demand; D11.1 reply; D11.2/12 response to the reply. For each record speaker, premises, conclusion, target, and residual burden. Then write **600 words**: 150 on challenge; 200 on the strongest finite-perfection response; 150 on what it changes and leaves unresolved; 100 on why compatibility and confirmation differ. Cite four stable locators and clearly identify your own evaluation. Criteria: speaker fidelity, explicit challenge, serious reply, direct test of the reply, no evaluation of the learner's belief.

## Independent FA30 — 60 minutes

Use Parts X–XI only; no practice notes, AI, tutor hints or examiner notes. Invented scenario: a defender concedes that a powerful creator could not prevent every natural harm without losing some regularity. The critic answers, “Then the creator is certainly malicious”; the defender replies, “Since you cannot rule out hidden goods, these harms prove perfect goodness.” In 350–450 words reconstruct the strongest reasonable position on each side, test both stated conclusions, and compare the finite-response strategy with the compatibility concession. Cite D11.1 and D11.2 or 12. Criteria: valid scope, serious alternatives, evidential distinction and exact attribution. Declare exposure.

## Alternate BA30 — 60 minutes if FA30 exposed

Same conditions. Invented scenario: a proposed designer is known only through a habitat containing stable reproduction and avoidable-looking disease. A defender proposes limited powers and long-term compensation; a critic demands that every instance of disease be shown logically incompatible with goodness before it can count against the proposal. Explain what each side must establish. Distinguish defending consistency, explaining this harm, and inferring the designer's attributes; use D10.29–35 and D11.1–2/12. Write 350–450 words without importing unassigned theological doctrines.
