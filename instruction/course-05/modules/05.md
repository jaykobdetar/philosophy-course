# C05-M05 — Ideas and qualities (14 hours)

**Question:** What does an object's producing an idea tell us about the object? Prerequisites: representations in M01 and distinct explanatory roles in M04. Outcomes: distinguish idea/quality and primary/secondary quality; reconstruct Berkeley's anti-abstraction and immaterialist reasoning; assess a premise while giving Locke a charitable comparison.

**Sources:** Locke, *Essay Concerning Human Understanding* **I.ii.1–9 and II.viii.1–26**, Peter Nidditch edition (Oxford, 1975). Berkeley, *Principles of Human Knowledge*, **Introduction §§1–25 and Part I §§1–33**, Jonathan Dancy edition (Oxford, 1998). These edition details are inherited from the curriculum and remain unmatched. [Source audit](../source-audit.md) distinguishes Gutenberg original-language alternatives from Bennett's modernizations; neither is the prescribed critical edition. Preserve book/chapter/section or Introduction/Part I/section in every citation.

**Budget:** reading 4.5h; practice 3.5h; feedback 1h; independent I5 1h; revision 3h; delayed D5 1h = 14h.

## Teach the reasoning

Locke's argument against innate principles in I.ii.1–9 examines an alleged mark of innateness: universal assent. Lack of conscious assent among some people is a difficulty for that mark; interpreting innateness as a mere capacity to know threatens to make acquired truths innate too. Reconstruct what the selected paragraphs establish before declaring that every contemporary theory of innate cognitive structure is refuted. A modern capacity hypothesis may be a different claim.

In II.viii, an idea is what is immediately present to the mind in thinking; a quality is a body's power to produce an idea. Primary qualities such as solidity, extension, figure and motion/rest are presented as inseparable from bodies through their changes. Secondary qualities concern powers, grounded in the primary constitution, to produce sensations such as color and taste. “Secondary” does not mean unreal: the power can be real although the sensation does not resemble the microscopic arrangement producing it. The resemblance claim is the philosophically vulnerable bridge. Variation in a perceived quality can motivate distinguishing perception from the body's constitution, but does not alone prove that every primary-quality perception resembles its cause.

Berkeley's Introduction questions abstract ideas conceived apart from every determinate feature: a general triangle need not be an image simultaneously possessing incompatible shapes. A particular sign or image can function generally by representing many instances. This is an argument about how generality works, not a denial that general reasoning occurs. Distinguish selectively attending to an aspect from supposedly forming an impossible indeterminate image.

Part I challenges the independent material bearer of sensible qualities. If sensible objects are combinations of perceived ideas, what warrants a further material substance outside all minds? The resemblance argument presses whether an idea can resemble something wholly non-ideational; the attack on the primary/secondary distinction extends the dependence considerations to size and shape. In §§22–24 the conceivability challenge asks about conceiving sensible things unperceived. A critic can distinguish a thought's being entertained from what its content represents: thinking of an unattended tree need not represent oneself observing it. Berkeley's response must be assessed at that precise step. Spirits are active perceivers; they are not merely another kind of passive idea. §§25–33 explain the involuntary, regular character of sensory ideas and distinguish them from freely imagined ones. Avoid the caricature that idealism makes everything a voluntary dream.

## Worked practice and output P5

Invented case: a gray ceramic mug looks blue under a lamp and gray in daylight. For Locke distinguish (a) the mug's constitution, (b) its power under illumination, (c) the perceiver's idea. A changed idea need not mean the mug acquired blue paint. For Berkeley, describe the lawful sensory changes without adding an unperceived material substratum as an unexplained default. This establishes how the accounts describe the evidence; it does not decide which account is true.

Use an ordinary **lemon** for your own three-column object/power/idea analysis, including shape, taste and the changes caused by cutting it. Then write **600 words** assessing one premise of Berkeley's resemblance, abstraction, or unperceived-object argument. Include the strongest Locke-compatible alternative and two exact passages, one from each author. Add a 100-word explanation of why Locke's anti-innateness argument is not identical to Berkeley's anti-abstraction argument. Criteria: stable terms, charitable rival, clear target premise, and no unsupported inference from “perceived” to “imaginary.”

## Independent I5 (60 minutes)

Primary sources only; no notes, AI, hints or examiner guidance. Invented case: a circular coin appears elliptical from an angle. A student concludes: “Locke must classify shape as secondary because it varies in experience; Berkeley must conclude the coin is unreal because nobody perceives it from every angle.” In 350–450 words assess both claims, cite II.viii and two relevant Berkeley sections, and identify one remaining disagreement. Criteria: appearance/power/constitution distinctions, accurate Berkeley ontology, explicit premise, warranted conclusion.
