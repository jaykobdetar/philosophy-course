# C05-M06 — Hume's challenge (20 hours)

**Question:** What makes an inference beyond observed cases reasonable, and what do we mean by a necessary connection? Prerequisites: C01 inference and M05 ideas. Outcomes: reconstruct the induction dilemma; distinguish its skeptical solution from a deductive vindication; explain the inquiry into necessary connection; characterize mitigated skepticism.

**Source:** Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, **sections II–VII and XII**, Tom Beauchamp edition (Oxford, 1999). All parts within these sections are assigned. [Hume Texts Online](https://davidhume.org/texts/e/) supplies a separately edited accessible text with E section/paragraph and SBN locators. It is not a verified Beauchamp reproduction. ## Teach the reasoning

Section II distinguishes more forceful impressions from ideas and asks how complex thinking draws on experiential materials. The missing shade example complicates a simple universal copy rule; note the exception instead of suppressing it or claiming it dissolves the entire project. Section III examines association through resemblance, contiguity and cause/effect. A psychological association explains a transition; it does not automatically justify the resulting belief.

Section IV contrasts relations of ideas with matters of fact. The denial of a genuinely demonstrable proposition is contradictory; the denial of tomorrow's familiar event need not be. Reasoning from what is observed or remembered to an absent fact depends on causal expectations. Those expectations are learned through experience rather than read off an unfamiliar object a priori. But what justifies moving from experienced conjunction to an unobserved instance? Demonstration cannot exclude a change in nature, since such a change is conceivable without contradiction. An appeal to the past success of experience-based reasoning appears to employ the very transition it is meant to justify. Write that dependency explicitly. The problem concerns warrant for projection; a record of past reliability is not irrelevant, but its relevance to the future is the disputed bridge.

Section V calls custom or habit the source of our expectations. Repetition produces an inclination to expect the usual attendant. This explains why we reason inductively and why ordinary life cannot simply suspend all expectation. It does not turn custom into a deductive proof of uniformity. Whether Hume also endorses a kind of naturalized justification is an interpretive question; do not collapse the psychological explanation into either a proof or a ban on science. Section VI treats degrees of belief as experience varies. Fallible probabilistic inference still uses an inferential policy; saying “only probably” does not by itself answer IV's challenge to its warrant.

Section VII asks for the experiential origin of the idea of power or necessary connection. A single perceived sequence displays succession, not a visible tie that necessitates the effect; introspection of willing also leaves the operation obscure. Repeated conjunction changes the observer: the mind becomes determined to pass from one event to its usual companion. Distinguish the external regularity from this internal transition. The two causal definitions near the section's end bring out these perspectives. Do not infer without argument that Hume has proved that no powers exist anywhere; an account of the content or source of an idea is not automatically an exhaustive metaphysical denial.

Section XII distinguishes excessive skepticism from a moderated practice: attention to our fallibility can restrain overconfident inquiry while allowing ordinary life and appropriately bounded investigation. Read the actual limits he proposes rather than treating moderation as “anything goes.” His conclusions may themselves be criticized for their scope.

## Worked practice and output P6

Invented case: a station's old clock has chimed each noon for a month. “It will chime tomorrow because it has always worked” includes a bridge from past operation to future performance. Adding “and that bridge worked last week” supplies a past success of the same policy. A maintenance inspection adds useful causal information for ordinary prediction, but its projected reliability still raises the general question. This distinguishes improving a local forecast from answering a universal justificatory challenge.

Save four one-page maps: **P6a** impressions/ideas with the missing-shade complication; **P6b** induction dilemma with both rejected routes; **P6c** regularity versus experienced inferential transition in VII; **P6d** custom and mitigated skepticism. For P6b test three defenses: “Nature must remain uniform”; “induction has succeeded”; “we cannot live otherwise.” For each state whether it offers demonstration, empirical evidence, explanation or practical necessity, and write a 100-word Humean challenge. Add a 400-word comparison of IV and VII so that the origin of an idea is not confused with the warrant of a prediction. Criteria: distinct questions, explicit bridge, relevant passage, qualified criticism.

## Independent I6 (60 minutes)

Primary text open; no practice notes, AI, hints or examiner notes. Invented case: an engineer's pump detector predicted every previous breakdown. She says, “It is logically impossible for it to fail next time: its circuit diagram explains the correlation. Since I can see the diagram, I also see the necessary connection.” In 350–450 words test both claims using IV and VII. Explain one valuable ordinary use of the diagram that survives your criticism. Criteria: demonstrative/probable distinction, underlying projection, necessary-connection analysis, fair residual value. Declare exposure.
