# C05-M04 — Substance and explanation (16 hours)

**Question:** Does explaining dependent things require one substance or many? Prerequisites: C03 substance/explanation distinctions and M03 interaction. Outcomes: reconstruct a Spinozist demonstration; reconstruct Leibniz's reason for simple substances; compare explanatory aims and test one premise without equating their vocabularies.

**Sources:** Spinoza, *Ethics* I, **all definitions and axioms, propositions 1–15 with demonstrations, and the Appendix**, Edwin Curley, *A Spinoza Reader* (Princeton, 1994). Leibniz, *Monadology* **§§1–39 and 78–90**, Roger Ariew and Daniel Garber, *Philosophical Essays* (Hackett, 1989). **Budget:** reading 6h; reconstruction/comparison practice 4h; feedback 1h; independent I4 1h; revision 3h; delayed D4 1h = 16h.

## Teach the reasoning

Spinoza's definition of substance joins existence in itself with conception through itself. A mode exists in another and is conceived through that other; an attribute is what intellect perceives as constituting substance's essence. These are roles in an explanatory system, not merely labels for large and small objects. In I proposition 5, the issue is whether two substances can share an attribute: if the distinction lies in attributes they do not share the relevant attribute; if it lies only in modifications, treating substance as prior to its modifications removes the proposed basis of distinction. The argument depends on the stated resources for distinguishing things and on the priority claim. To reject it, target those assumptions rather than point to two visibly separate cups as if “substance” already meant cup.

The route to I.14 uses God's existence and the definition of God as having infinite attributes, together with the exclusion of two substances sharing an attribute. I.15 draws the consequence that whatever exists is in God and conceived through God. This is not the claim that every finite thing is independently omnipotent. Your reconstruction must include the dependence on I.11; I.5 alone does not establish that any particular substance exists. The Appendix examines how human pursuit of ends can encourage projecting purposes onto nature. An explanation of why people believe a teleological claim does not automatically refute it; identify the additional criticism of the claim's explanatory adequacy.

Leibniz starts from composites and argues for simple substances as their ultimate constituents. “Simple” means without parts; a monad is not a microscopic extended bead. Divisibility would defeat the intended simplicity. §§7–17 then complicate the account: a simple substance can have varied perceptions and internally governed change without spatial parts. The mill argument asks whether an inspection of mechanical parts would reveal perception; it challenges an explanatory reduction, not the empirical usefulness of studying mechanisms.

The principles of contradiction and sufficient reason organize different kinds of explanation in §§31–39. A chain of contingent reasons, however long, leaves the question of why this whole contingent order obtains. Leibniz moves to a necessary ground. One can challenge the demand for a further reason or the transition from each contingent event to the series; simply extending the series repeats the issue. §§78–81 present harmony between mental and bodily orders; causal coordination does not require causal influx between monads. §§82–90 connect rational spirits and divine government. These selected claims are not permission to import the whole *Theodicy*.

## Worked practice and output P4

Invented example: a bead necklace is explained as a collection of beads. This explains assembly but does not show that the beads are metaphysically simple; they may have parts. The example teaches how to distinguish a relative stopping point in an explanation from the ultimate simplicity Leibniz requires. It does not establish that there must be an ultimate stopping point.

Produce (a) a 350-word numbered reconstruction of Spinoza I.14 with its dependencies; (b) a 350-word reconstruction of Leibniz §§1–3 or §§36–39; (c) a **400-word comparison** answering “What makes an ordinary extended object dependent?” Include a shared question, each author's premise/conclusion, one explanatory success each seeks, and one precise objection. Add a four-row glossary showing why substance, mode, simple, and reason cannot be interchanged. Criteria: complete local dependency, distinct meanings, relevant comparison, limits of assigned excerpts. Feedback should test the weakest premise, not demand a preferred metaphysics.

## Independent I4 (60 minutes)

Assigned primary passages open; no coached notes or external help. Invented proposal: “Two perfectly synchronized observatory clocks need no explanation beyond their matching readings; both Spinoza and Leibniz would therefore count each clock as a self-sufficient substance.” In 350–450 words compare the proposal with Spinoza I definitions 3/5 and I.14–15, and Leibniz §§1–3, 7 and 78–81. State what the comparison can and cannot establish. Criteria: distinct senses of independence, actual inference, textual anchors, no reduction of monads to clock mechanisms.
