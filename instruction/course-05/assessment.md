# Course 5 — Learner assessment forms

These are held-out tasks, not practice models. The assessor selects a form after checking the essay topic and exposure log. Read only the selected items. Keep [examiner notes](examiner-notes.md) closed until submission. If you have already rehearsed an item or seen its anchors, declare that fact and use an unexposed alternate. If both forms are exposed, record the need for a new assessor-created changed case with the same source and criteria; completion remains unresolved until it is administered.

## Conditions and criteria

Written session: **90 minutes, three 30-minute tasks**, normally 250–350 words each. Primary texts at the named ranges and an edition-identification sheet are permitted. No lecture notes, essay, worked examples, search, AI, tutor hints or examiner notes. Text-to-speech and dictation are permitted as access tools; preserve the transcript and check citations. Record exact source, time, assistance, prior exposure and any interruption. The independent artifact is saved before feedback.

For each task: identify the question and author/speaker; explain the decisive premise and conclusion; cite a passage precisely in the actual edition; address the supplied changed case; distinguish textual interpretation from your own objection or repair. Criteria evaluate reasoning and attribution, not whether you agree with the author. Word counts guide scope and are not penalties for approved access arrangements.

Take one Cartesian/interaction variant, one substance/ideas variant, and the induction task. Select the first two away from the essay's main focus. If the essay is Cartesian, choose an Elisabeth variant; if on Elisabeth choose a Cartesian variant. If the essay is Berkeley choose substance; otherwise choose the less-secure uncoached second-row topic. Hume essays still receive the changed induction/connection case, not a repetition of their thesis. The assessment checks all three essential groups.

## Form A

**A1C — Cartesian transition.** Sources: Meditations III and V, especially the discussion of present clear perception and later recollection. Invented claim: “Once the meditator remembers a clear argument for God, every premise in it is secure because God guarantees clear thinking; the proof has therefore established its own reliability without further explanation.” Reconstruct the dependency, explain one possible response, and identify the issue that response must still settle. Do not merely attach the word “circular.”

**A1E — Interaction reply.** Sources: letters of 6 May and 21 May 1643. Invented claim: “Elisabeth assumes mental causation is impossible; Descartes refutes her just by pointing out that we move our bodies.” Distinguish the question asked, the assumptions relevant to motion, and the primitive-union reply. Decide whether the invented account represents the exchange fairly, explaining one limitation of your conclusion.

**A2S — Substance.** Sources: Spinoza I definitions 3/5 and propositions 14–15; Leibniz §§1–3 and 36–39. Invented scenario: an archivist explains a chain of copied manuscripts by naming each earlier copy and concludes, “A sufficiently long chain explains itself. This is what both philosophers mean by substance.” Compare their standards of dependence and explanation. State where the analogy helps and where it fails; do not infer either metaphysics just from the example.

**A2I — Ideas.** Sources: Locke II.viii.8–15 and 21–26; Berkeley Part I §§8–15 and 29–33. Invented scenario: a lamp changes a white tile's appearance, while rulers agree on its length. A student says both authors must treat color as imaginary and length as existing outside every mind. Compare the two accounts of the evidence and identify the disputed inference.

**A3 — Induction and connection.** Sources: Hume Enquiry IV, V and VII. Invented scenario: a forecasting method has outperformed all tested rivals. Its designer argues, “Its track record proves it will remain best, and repeated measurements reveal a necessary tie between the pattern and the outcome.” Analyze both claims, explain the skeptical solution's relevance, and state one legitimate practical conclusion left available.

## Form B — alternate fresh form

**B1C.** Sources: Meditations II and VI. Invented claim: “The thinker can doubt the body but not thinking, so Meditation II has already demonstrated that God can create this thinker without any body.” Reconstruct the stronger argument required in VI and identify the gap in the proposed shortcut. State one objection to the stronger argument without confusing it with the shortcut.

**B1E.** Sources: letters of 10 June, 28 June and 1 July 1643. Invented claim: “Once union is called primitive, requesting any further explanation is a contradiction in terms.” Explain why a defender might resist reducing union to extension, and why Elisabeth can still press a substantive question. Cite a feature of the actual exchange that helps adjudicate the claim.

**B2S.** Sources: Spinoza I.5, 11, 14–15; Leibniz §§1–3, 7 and 78–81. Invented claim: “If two instruments always agree, they are one substance for Spinoza and one monad for Leibniz.” Analyze why agreement alone does or does not establish either conclusion. Compare what each assigned argument uses in place of the proposed observation.

**B2I.** Sources: Locke I.ii.1–9 and Berkeley Introduction §§10–16. Invented claim: “A person who can prove a theorem using one drawn triangle must possess an innate abstract triangle; therefore Locke and Berkeley both deny mathematical generality.” Distinguish the two attacks, explain how general use differs from a particular image, and identify one question these passages leave open.

**B3.** Sources: Hume Enquiry IV–V, VII and XII. Invented scenario: a conservator predicts that a newly found ink will fade because chemically similar inks faded before. A critic says, “Your inference is not deductively certain, so you must stop all prediction. And because the connection is not visible in a single sample, no causal claim can have meaning.” Assess both claims and distinguish a local improvement to the forecast from a general vindication of projection.

## Defense — 20 minutes plus 10-minute record

Explain a premise from your actual submitted work for 5 minutes. The assessor selects one of these unprepared challenges, avoiding a rehearsed reply: (a) an opposing reader accepts your passage but rejects your bridge; specify the missing premise; (b) a conclusion is compatible with the evidence but does not follow from it; explain the difference in your own argument; (c) you changed a historical author's premise to strengthen the argument; identify the cost to attribution. Respond for 10 minutes, then spend 5 minutes stating what you would revise. A written exchange with a 20-minute response allowance is equivalent. The assessor identifies the actual target passage and records criterion, evidence and uncertainty.

## Delayed Form D — select the least secure group, 30 minutes after roughly four to six weeks or two later modules

First 10 minutes closed notes; final 20 minutes with the named primary text only. Mark changes made after consulting it. Write 200–300 words.

**D1 — Cartesian/interaction:** using Meditations VI and the 21 May letter, explain whether showing that two things are distinct shows that they cannot interact. State what further premise is needed and how the union reply addresses it.

**D2 — Substance/ideas:** using Locke II.viii.9–15 and Berkeley Part I §§8–15, analyze an invented case of a pepper that tastes mild to one person and strong to another. Explain whether differing ideas show that there is no real power in an object, and compare the two authors' commitments.

**D3 — Induction/connection:** using Enquiry IV–V and VII, analyze “All earlier storms were preceded by this pressure pattern; the pattern therefore compels the next storm.” State the projection and the additional necessity claim separately.

## Delayed Form E — alternate when D is exposed

Same timing and conditions; select the corresponding group.

**E1:** using Meditation IV and the 28 June letter, assess “Every failure to explain an embodied experience must be a freely chosen error of judgment.” Distinguish the explanation of error from the special way union is grasped.

**E2:** using Spinoza I definitions 3/5 and I.14–15, and Leibniz §§1–3, assess “Because a woven cloth depends on threads, both authors say each thread is metaphysically independent.” Compare the needed arguments.

**E3:** using Enquiry IV–V and XII, assess “Predicting by habit is unavoidable, so its reliability is logically necessary.” Distinguish what follows psychologically, practically and demonstratively; give a changed example of your own.

Final record: [outcome map](outcomes.md) completed with practice artifact, fresh sample, delayed result, source status, assistance, and any bounded repair. No overall score compensates for a missing essential row.
