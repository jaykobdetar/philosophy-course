# C08-M03 — Liberty and expression

**16 hours.** Question: when does preventing harm justify coercion, and why hear an opinion we believe false? Prerequisites: Modules 1–2. Outcomes: apply the harm principle with an explicit inference; reconstruct two distinct defenses of discussion; separate truth, expression and restriction.

## Explanation

Mill asks about civil and social liberty, including coercive public opinion. He offers prevention of harm to others as the purpose that can warrant interference with a mature person's liberty. One's own good is insufficient by itself. The principle does not automatically classify every unpleasant effect as harm, identify every effective intervention, or show every harmful act should be legally prohibited. Application needs an account of the threatened interest, causal evidence and the relationship between the proposed restriction and its purpose.

Chapter II provides more than the claim that any unpopular opinion is true. If the opinion is true, suppressors can deprive others of correction. If false, confronting it may improve understanding of the reasons for a true belief. Partial truths raise a further problem: opposing views may each contain something the other needs. These are different branches. “The claim is false, therefore none of Mill's reasons apply” misses the second branch. “Hearing any claim always improves understanding” overstates what has been established about conditions for fruitful discussion.

Keep three columns: truth of the claim; permission to express it; justification of this particular restriction. A claim may be false yet its suppression unjustified. A restriction can address a specified harmful use rather than settle the claim's truth. A neutral classroom case cannot decide actual causal risks without evidence. Also mark Mill's own exclusions and civilizational assumptions in chapter I: universalizing his stated scope without discussion conceals a problem which Modules 4 and 6 will revisit.

## Worked practice: invented harbor notice

A notice says the harbor council wastes money. Officials propose banning it because it is offensive. Neither falsity nor offense alone supplies a harm-prevention justification. Now suppose a notice knowingly substitutes a closed pier for the safe evacuation exit. The relevant additional premise concerns foreseeable danger from that use of information. The two notices require different causal analysis, even if officials call both “misinformation.” This does not settle whether a narrower correction would suffice.

**P3:** In 650–800 words analyze both cases: (a) a library lecture defending an unpopular tax proposal, with objections permitted; (b) a loudspeaker operator repeatedly announcing a false fire alarm during a crowded performance, with documented crowd injuries. For each give the three-column distinction, a proposed rule, one narrower alternative, and an empirical uncertainty. Then reconstruct Mill's false-opinion branch in four steps. Criteria: link the verdict to the principle, do not equate disagreement with harm, and do not assume facts absent from the case.

## Independent F3

A town meeting permits anyone five minutes, but a speaker uses a machine to occupy the microphone continuously. Her proposal itself concerns a harmless change to park opening hours. The council proposes either a viewpoint-neutral five-minute rule or a ban on criticism of park officials. In 350–450 words distinguish control of access from suppression of an opinion; evaluate both rules with Mill I–II; identify the evidence needed to show the first rule works without selectively silencing minorities. Sources/citation sheet permitted; no content help or rehearsed answer.
