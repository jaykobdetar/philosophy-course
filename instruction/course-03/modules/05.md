# C03-M05 — Aristotle on explanation

**16 hours. Guiding question:** What different questions can “Why is this so?” ask? Prerequisite: Module 3's premise/inference distinctions. Outcomes: distinguish primary substance, species/genus and attributes; give different explanations of one object; reconstruct an argument for natural ends without silently inserting a modern theory.

## Teaching: subjects and reasons

In *Categories* 1–5 the first distinctions concern how names and definitions apply and how things are said of or present in subjects. Being present in a subject is not simply being physically inside it: a part of a whole is not, for that reason alone, an attribute in this technical sense. An individual horse is a primary substance; the species and genus tell us what it is, whereas its color tells us a quality. A substance can remain numerically the same while acquiring contrary qualities over time. The argument therefore helps explain how change in an attribute need not be replacement of the individual. Do not treat every grammatical predicate as an accidental quality: “is an animal” and “is pale” have different roles in this classification.

*Physics* II.1 distinguishes natural things by an internal principle of change and rest, considered as the things they are. A wooden bed can rot because it is wood, not because bed-making is its internal source of development. The qualification “as such” matters. A manufactured object is made of natural material; that does not make its artifact form reproduce naturally. Matter and form are both relevant senses of nature, with form describing the attained organization and kind.

II.2 asks what the student of nature must study. Mathematical abstraction can attend to shape without attending to the object's moving, embodied nature; natural inquiry must consider form and appropriate matter together. A shape is not an adequate definition of an organism merely because one can draw it. This connects the categories of explanation with what kind of subject is being investigated.

II.3's causes are ways of answering why: material (what persists as the material), formal (what it is or the account of its organization), source of change (what brings about the relevant change), and final (what it is for). Several can explain one thing without competing as four mechanical pushes. The answer must specify the explanandum. The maker explains the making of a bowl in one way; its silver explains its material in another. “The bowl is silver” does not answer every question about its production.

II.8 confronts a rival: perhaps useful structures arise through necessity and survive when they happen to work, as rain can benefit crops without falling for their sake. Aristotle invokes regularity and organized sequences toward characteristic completion, compares art with nature and argues that failures do not eliminate ends. One vulnerable transition is from “not coincidence” to “for an end”: why are those exhaustive alternatives in the relevant case? This question evaluates the reasoning as stated. It differs from retrospectively declaring Aristotle a modern evolutionary theorist or refuting him by naming a theory whose mechanisms have not been supplied. A modern comparison must state its added assumptions and be labeled an external comparison.

## Original worked example and practice

**Invented artifact:** A ceramic seed tray is made from clay, has shallow compartments and drainage holes, is formed by a potter, and is used to start seedlings. These can answer four different questions. The compartments are part of the organized design; starting seedlings is its end; the potter's forming activity is a source of its production. A seedling growing inside has its own natural development; the tray does not become a living thing by supporting it. If a buyer uses the tray as a paint palette, distinguish the present use from the original production explanation instead of assuming “purpose” has only one sense.

Produce a two-object table for **an oak sapling and a woven basket**, with four explanatory questions and candidate answers for each. Mark empirical assumptions as assumptions; no botanical research is required. Explain how nature/art and matter/form apply differently. Then write **500 words** comparing substance and attribute using one individual plant through a change in color and height. Explain why the species is not simply another color-like property.

Reconstruct the II.8 argument in six numbered steps, including the rival's rain/teeth challenge and Aristotle's response. Add 250 words testing one inference. Feedback should check whether a purported formal cause is merely another material description and whether the teleology criticism targets a stated premise. Revise the table and one argumentative step.

## Independent check C03-M05-I (60 minutes)

**Invented changed case:** A living vine grows around a wooden trellis. A gardener made the trellis to support it. The vine's tendrils keep attaching even when the trellis is moved. A student says, “Both are natural because both are wood or plant matter; the gardener is the only cause of their shape.” Write 350 words distinguishing the objects and at least three explanatory questions; identify a missing natural-development explanation and explain what would be required to defend a final explanation. Source open, no notes or content help. Do not assert unprovided biological mechanisms. Criteria: correct as-such distinction, nonredundant causes, clear explanandum and explicit empirical limits. Conceptual performance can be judged provisionally from local texts; assigned-edition wording/Bekker judgments require the actual edition.

If categories are confused, replace the word “cause” by its question in each sentence. After Module 6, spend the retrieval hour reconstructing the four questions and checking one source locator.
