# C03-M02 — The challenge of justice

**16 hours. Guiding question:** What must a defense of justice show when reputation and punishment are removed? Prerequisite: Module 1's speaker/function method. Outcomes: distinguish Thrasymachus' account from Glaucon's renewed challenge; separate an explanation of a practice's origin from a reason to value it; reconstruct a demanding test of justice's benefit.

## Reading and time

R1 *Republic* **I 327a–354c and II 357a–367e**, Grube/Reeve 1992 or the explicitly named marked Jowett route. No Books III–IV reading is added here. See [readings](../readings.md). Budget: reading/annotation 6h; argument-map practice 3h; 600-word reconstruction 3h; feedback and revision 2h; independent task 1h; delayed retrieval/source log 1h. Total 16h.

## Teaching: distinguish the challenges

Book I begins with proposed accounts associated with paying debts, truth-telling, and benefiting friends while harming enemies. Counterexamples test whether an apparently familiar rule gives justice's nature. A rule may guide ordinary cases yet fail as an unrestricted definition. Follow the speaker changes rather than treating the whole book as Socrates announcing his own definition.

Thrasymachus connects justice with the advantage of the stronger: rulers make laws for their own advantage and demand obedience. This combines a claim about political power with a claim about what counts as just. Socrates asks about rulers who make mistakes. If obedience to a mistaken command harms the ruler, the two formulations can come apart. Thrasymachus' appeal to the ruler strictly understood responds by tying proper rule to expertise. Socrates then contests whose advantage a genuine craft serves. That exchange is not resolved merely by observing that some rulers are corrupt; both sides are now distinguishing a role performed correctly from its defective performance.

A second thread concerns whether injustice benefits its possessor more than justice. Defeating a proposed definition does not yet establish the value of justice. The closing uncertainty of Book I matters: a reader should not mistake a sequence of refutations for a completed positive account.

In Book II Glaucon renews the case for injustice because he wants a stronger answer. His threefold distinction separates things valued for themselves, for their effects, and for both. Socrates places justice in the valuable-for-both group. Glaucon stages a rival explanation: people agree to restraints because suffering injustice is worse than the advantage of committing it; those who could act unjustly with impunity would do so. The ring story makes impunity vivid. It is a thought experiment, not an observed survey proving what every person would choose.

The strongest test reverses appearances: the unjust person receives a reputation for justice, while the just person is misjudged. Adeimantus deepens the criticism of praise that advertises rewards rather than justice's effect on the soul. Thus “justice earns trust” is relevant to ordinary life but insufficient by itself to meet this controlled challenge. The response must identify what justice and injustice do to their possessors apart from the external rewards attached to appearing just. This does not require pretending external losses never occur. It requires explaining the kind and strength of the alleged benefit and why the offered argument supports it.

## Worked example and practice

**Invented case:** An anonymous scholarship evaluator can favor a friend with no chance of discovery. “Fairness will protect the evaluator's reputation” cannot answer the stipulated case because reputation is held fixed. “The evaluator will preserve an ordered pattern of commitments” addresses an internal effect, but it needs premises connecting that pattern with justice and with benefit. The example models identifying a burden of proof; it does not establish Plato's eventual answer.

Create two maps, each with claim, supporting steps, objection and reply. Map Thrasymachus' ruler/craft exchange separately from Glaucon's origin/impunity/reputation sequence. Under each map mark “speaker endorses,” “speaker tests,” or “speaker rehearses” with evidence. Then compare these three proposed defenses: fair acts earn praise; just people avoid all pain; justice improves the agent in a way worth having even without praise. State which meets the challenge's form and what would still require proof. Treat none as established merely by classification.

Write **600 words** reconstructing the strongest challenge, including the point of reversing reputations and one distinction from Thrasymachus. Append a 150-word success condition: what a successful defense must demonstrate. Feedback should test whether your reconstruction can survive the opponent saying “I stipulated that nobody will discover it.” Revise the central premise rather than adding an unrelated benefit.

## Independent check C03-M02-I (60 minutes)

**Invented changed case:** An archivist can secretly destroy a rival's accurate work. Keeping it will bring the archivist disgrace because everyone falsely believes the work is fraudulent. Destroying it brings praise. A defender says, “Preserve it because justice always receives recognition eventually.” Write 300 words: identify the defense's relation to the stipulated case; reconstruct the strongest challenge to preserving the work; state two things a successful answer would need to establish; distinguish this task from identifying whichever group makes the laws. Source open, no notes or live content help. Do not use Module 3's teaching or examiner notes. Criteria: faithful stipulations, distinct attribution, explicit inferential burden, no assumption that the final verdict follows from publicity alone.

Repair a conflation by using different colors for definition, origin and advantage claims and tracing each to its speaker. After Module 3, spend the reserved retrieval hour reconstructing the challenge without notes, checking it, and repairing one missed condition. Next: read Module 3 and test whether the proposed answer meets the success conditions you just wrote.
