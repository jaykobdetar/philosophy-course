# C03-M03 — Plato's answer and its limits

**18 hours. Guiding question:** Does an ordered soul establish that justice benefits its possessor? Prerequisite: the separate challenge maps in Module 2. Outcomes: reconstruct the city–soul comparison and partition argument; distinguish inner order from merely efficient planning; identify the bridges from psychological justice to ordinary conduct and benefit.

## Teaching: the analogy needs support

The inquiry in IV identifies wisdom, courage and moderation in the city, then locates justice in each class performing its proper work. Political specialization is the starting model, not yet a demonstration that an individual's soul has the same structure. The text explicitly turns to whether the individual exhibits corresponding kinds of motivation. Its method permits checking the city against the person, not simply projecting a civic map onto psychology.

A principle of opposition guides the partition: the same thing cannot undergo or do opposites in the same respect, at the same time, in relation to the same object. A thirsty person can also resist drinking. If the resistance and attraction are simultaneous and properly specified, Socrates distinguishes the appetitive source from the reasoning source. Different wishes at different times would not satisfy the test. Spirit is further distinguished through anger that can oppose appetite and through cases in which spirited responses precede developed reasoning. This is an argument about sources or kinds of motivation; it is not an anatomical discovery of three pieces of brain.

Justice in the person is each part doing its work under appropriate rule. Reason should govern with knowledge of the whole; spirit can support it; appetite has a legitimate place without ruling the whole. Thus a highly organized fraud is not automatically “rational” in the relevant sense. Calculation about means may serve appetite. To defend the theory, however, one must explain why governing for the whole excludes the supposed counterexample, rather than defining “proper rule” as whatever yields the desired verdict.

The text connects psychic justice with ordinary examples such as returning deposits and keeping faith, then compares virtue with health. Two bridges require scrutiny: **why inner order reliably yields the conduct ordinarily called just**, and **why that order makes its possessor better off even with damaged reputation**. An analogy with bodily health suggests a kind of constitutive good, but an analogy alone does not prove that the comparison holds. A careful answer identifies what is evidence, what is an added premise, and what remains disputable.

The bounded IX passage portrays the tyrannical person as enslaved to uncontrolled desires, fearful and unable to achieve the aims of the soul as a whole. It reinforces the contrast between apparent success and internal condition. Do not import the later numerical pleasure comparison into 577c–580c. Nor does the misery of an extreme tyrant alone show that every possible unjust act makes every agent worse off than every just alternative. The scope of the conclusion needs an argument. A learner can defend a qualified answer to Glaucon while accepting that some steps remain controversial.

## Worked example and practice

**Invented case:** A sailing crew has a navigator, lookout and rowers. Each role can contribute to the voyage. Rowers who coordinate perfectly to seize the cargo are organized, yet their success does not establish good navigation of the whole undertaking. The analogy helps distinguish coordination from proper rule. It cannot prove that a person's motivations form equivalent roles or that their proper ordering is moral justice; those are precisely premises to investigate.

Make a city–soul diagram with a labeled arrow for each claimed correspondence. Under it reconstruct the thirst argument in five steps, preserving the same-time/same-respect restriction. Test it against (a) wanting hot tea in the morning and cold water later and (b) simultaneously wanting a sweet drink while judging it harmful.

Build a **benefit-premise ledger**, with at least six rows: proposed premise; supporting passage; inference it enables; rival interpretation; confidence. Include the existence of distinguishable motivations, reason's governing role, the connection to ordinary conduct, the health comparison, the role of reputation and the scope of IX's tyrant case. Write 500–700 words answering the success conditions from Module 2. A good analysis does not count an asserted bridge as a demonstrated bridge.

Consult the annotated historical-writing progression in [tutor notes](../tutor-notes.md) after producing your own first paragraph. Request feedback on one contested bridge. Revise it by adding textual support or narrowing the thesis, and state what the revision still does not establish.

## Independent check C03-M03-I (60 minutes)

**Invented changed case:** A treasurer calmly plans to keep an unclaimed deposit. She feels no inner conflict, has consistent long-term priorities, and will never be discovered. A critic concludes, “Since she is internally orderly, Republic IV says she is just.” Write 350 words evaluating that interpretation. Identify a textual criterion of order, explain one inference the critic needs, and state what evidence would decide between two readings. Use assigned primary text only; no notes, models or live content help. Criteria: distinguish calmness from the account's governing relation; identify a real bridge; preserve the question about ordinary justice rather than assume the desired result.

If the partition argument is unclear, annotate the exact example and write two rival explanations of the conflict. If the health analogy seems decisive, supply the missing comparative premise and a possible counterexample. After Module 5, spend the reserved two hours on a no-notes map, source check and revised bridge paragraph. Next: Module 4 asks how knowledge would equip a soul to govern.
