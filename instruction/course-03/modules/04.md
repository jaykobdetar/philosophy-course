# C03-M04 — Knowledge and the soul

**18 hours. Guiding question:** What can an image clarify, and what must an argument establish? Prerequisites: source attribution and the distinction between a premise and an illustration. Outcomes: explain the line's epistemic distinctions; interpret the cave's educational and political functions; reconstruct recollection or affinity while limiting its conclusion.

## Reading and time

R1 *Republic* **VI 509d–511e; VII 514a–521b**. R2 *Phaedo* **64a–69e; 72e–84b**. Editions and legal no-purchase routes are in the [register](../readings.md). Budget: reading/annotation 7h; image/argument practice 3h; 700-word evaluation 3h; feedback/revision 2h; independent check 1h; delayed retrieval/citation checks 2h. Total 18h.

## Teaching: methods, objects and conclusions

The divided line relates different objects and modes of cognition. Images and the things they depict occupy the visible domain. Mathematical reasoning can use visible diagrams while reasoning about intelligible relations, proceeding from hypotheses it has not itself justified. Dialectical inquiry treats hypotheses as starting points to investigate rather than unquestioned foundations. The distinction is not simply “looking bad, thinking good”: a drawn square can support reasoning whose object is not the ink. The line's arrangement illustrates a hierarchy; assessing that hierarchy requires reasons for the relations it represents.

The cave dramatizes education as reorientation, difficulty and adjustment. A released prisoner does not immediately become a reliable judge of everything. The return introduces a political problem: those who would prefer contemplation are required to contribute to the city that educated them. The passage's argument concerns the good of the whole city and obligations of its educated members. It does not follow merely from the imagery of sunlight. Preserve Glaucon's objection about making their lives worse; it matters for the earlier challenge about justice and benefit.

In *Phaedo* 64a–69e the impending death gives philosophical inquiry a distinctive setting. Socrates associates philosophical purification with loosening bodily distractions and seeking understanding. This describes a conception of philosophical life; it is not by itself proof that the soul survives death. Likewise, the courage displayed by a character does not establish the metaphysical proposition he hopes is true.

The recollection argument uses the difference between sensible equals and equality itself. Judging that perceived instances fall short of a standard appears to require acquaintance with the standard. Socrates argues that this knowledge precedes sensory experience and hence birth. A reconstruction must specify why the standard could not be acquired or constructed from experience; merely possessing a concept is insufficient to establish prenatal possession. Even if pre-existence followed, continued existence after death would need more. The interlocutors themselves identify this gap around 77b–c. Do not silently use the earlier cyclical argument outside the assigned range to discharge it.

The affinity argument compares the soul with intelligible, stable, noncomposite things and the body with visible, changing, composite things. The soul's reflective activity and ruling role enter the comparison, which supports a claim about its resistance to dissolution. Similarity is not identity: a reader must explain which similarities matter to dissolution and whether the inference is demonstrative, probabilistic or part of a cumulative reassurance. The passage also connects the soul's condition with the way a person has lived. A “nothing invisible can perish” reconstruction is too blunt; it drops the structure and qualifications of the argument. The later final argument for immortality is outside the assignment and cannot repair an essay unnoticed.

## Worked example and practice

**Invented example:** A student recognizes that a hand-drawn circle is imperfect. That judgment uses a standard of circularity. It does not immediately tell us when or how she acquired that standard: teaching, abstraction and a prior grasp remain competing explanations until argued about. This models the gap between possessing a criterion and establishing its origin. It neither refutes nor proves Plato's recollection argument.

Draw the four divisions of the line and give a sentence on objects and method for each. Then make a two-column cave chart: image/event and philosophical claim it is used to illuminate. Circle any claim for which the illustration supplies no independent proof. Add the return-to-cave objection and answer in ordinary prose.

For *Phaedo*, choose recollection or affinity. Produce a six-step reconstruction with a clearly bounded conclusion, then write **700 words** evaluating it. Include the intended conclusion, the pivotal premise, a serious rival explanation and a reply or qualified judgment. Use at least two checked references and distinguish your reconstruction from the dialogue's own words. Feedback should name the strongest conclusion the stated premises support. Revise one overstatement before polishing prose.

## Independent check C03-M04-I (60 minutes)

**Invented changed case:** A reader writes, “Because a theorem is not visible and remains true, whatever can grasp it is also unchanging and indestructible.” Using *Phaedo* 78b–80c, write 300–350 words comparing this inference with the assigned argument. State one relevant resemblance, one missing premise and one possible defense that does not simply repeat the conclusion. Source open; no notes or live content help. Criteria: accurate argument form, discriminating use of resemblance, explicit scope, traceable passage. A disagreement with immortality is not itself an objection.

For remediation, write “knows about X,” “resembles X,” and “has all properties of X” on separate lines and justify any transition you use. After Module 6, use the reserved two hours for a no-notes argument map, text comparison and one corrected paragraph.
