## Teaching: life, absence and inquiry

Aristotle defines soul through form and actuality of a natural organized body potentially alive. First actuality differs from currently exercising a capacity: knowing a language differs from speaking it now. An organism asleep can still have its living capacities. The analogy prevents defining life only by its present overt activity. Nutrition, perception, appetite, movement and thinking distinguish kinds of living beings; “soul” consequently covers plants as well as human thought. The account asks what makes a body a living body of its kind, not how a second object happens to inhabit it.

The eye/sight and tool/function analogies help explain the relation but have limits: a tool is not a natural organism. Nor does the excerpt license the unqualified claim that Aristotle proves every psychic power inseparable. II.1–2 leaves qualifications and questions about intellect/separability. The requirement that capacities belong to suitable bodies gives a reason to reject arbitrary attachment of soul to any body; it does not settle all later debates about mind.

Epicurus' letter presents philosophy as practical care throughout life. Its death argument connects good and evil with sensation, death with the absence of sensation, and the absence of a subject at death with death's not harming that subject. The inference depends on a view of harm. A deprivation objection asks whether losing a possible good can be bad even when the person never experiences that loss. That is an objection to a premise, not a report that Epicurus forgot people fear dying. Distinguish death, a painful dying process, anticipation, and others' grief.

The letter also distinguishes desires and explains why a good pleasure need not always be chosen: consequences and stability matter. It connects prudence, virtue and pleasant living, discusses the gods and resists treating necessity or fortune as complete masters of action. Reading only the famous death passage would miss the practical framework that makes freedom from fear matter. The argument is not “pursue every intense sensation.”

In *Apology* 40c–41c, Socrates considers death as either absence of perception or a change of dwelling, offering reasons for hope under each possibility. Epicurus' letter uses a firmer account of the absence of sensation. Similar practical reassurance does not mean identical metaphysical premises or genre: a courtroom speech represented in a dialogue and a letter of ethical counsel do different work.

Sextus presents an ongoing practice of inquiry that sets opposed appearances and accounts against one another. When neither appears more persuasive, suspension follows; tranquility in matters of opinion is described as following without the expected doctrinal resolution. This differs from confidently asserting that nothing can be known. He also distinguishes acquiescing in how things appear from affirming how they are by nature, and ordinary guidance in action from a philosophical criterion of truth. Hunger, thirst and other unavoidable affections are not magically removed. **This conceptual orientation was checked against Patrick's separately identified translation, not against Annas/Barnes.** Before making an edition-specific claim, inspect I.1–30 in the assigned edition and compare its terms. ## Worked example and practice

**Invented example:** An experienced musician sleeping still possesses a skill she is not exercising. This distinguishes a standing capacity from present performance; it does not show that a musical skill is itself a soul or can exist without a musician. Now distinguish the capacity to play, playing, and the instrument's shape. The example's value lies in its restricted correspondence.
