# C03-M01 — Historical orientation and method

**10 hours. Guiding question:** How can a dialogue supply philosophical evidence without being a transcript of its author's beliefs? Prerequisite: Course 1 reconstruction, paraphrase and citation practice. Outcomes: distinguish author, narrator, speaker and interlocutor; separate a dramatic date from a composition date; reconstruct a question before judging a position.

## Teaching: a claim has a position in a conversation

In the *Apology*, Socrates reports an oracle and describes examining people reputed wise. His conclusion concerns a difference between possessing wisdom and believing one possesses it. The argument needs the examinations: the oracle's report alone does not demonstrate what each purported expert understands. A reader should distinguish the dramatic Socrates' account, Plato's construction of that defense and a historian's claims about the actual trial. A compelling speech can support an interpretation of a text without independently verifying every event it narrates.

*Meno* raises an obstacle to inquiry. If one already knows, searching seems unnecessary; if one does not know, how could one recognize the answer? This treats knowing and ignorance as exhaustive and apparently leaves no useful intermediate condition. The questioning of the enslaved boy stages movement from confidence in a wrong answer to perplexity and then better answers. That sequence can show that recognizing ignorance enables inquiry. A further claim—that the knowledge was possessed before birth—requires additional premises. Neither the presence of questions nor success in geometry proves that stronger claim by itself. Notice the asymmetry in the setting: Socrates controls questions and diagrams, and the boy's enslaved status is part of the dramatic scene; do not present it as a modern experiment with controlled teaching conditions.

Use four columns: **who speaks; what is claimed; why now; how others respond**. A character may propose a definition to test it, make a concession, rehearse another view, or voice an objection. Attribution requires following that function. Equally, “it is only a character” is not a reason to ignore an argument. You can evaluate the inference while qualifying the attribution: “Socrates argues here that …; the dialogue's later response supports/complicates this reading.”

A chronology has different sorts of entries. Author lifespan, date of an event depicted, and date of writing are not interchangeable. Put approximate Socrates, Plato and Aristotle lifespan dates from OpenStax into one column with attribution. Put the trial as dramatic context in another, and mark any exact composition date unsupported unless a checked source establishes it. Use “not established by this reading,” not a guessed year. Epicurus and Sextus belong to later settings; this course's order is a sequence of questions, not evidence that all its interlocutors met.

## Original worked example and practice

**Invented dialogue:** Mara says, “Every trained navigator knows where north is.” Ivo asks whether a trained navigator can be disoriented. Mara concedes the possibility and changes her claim to “Training normally improves orientation.” A weak report says “The author believes trained navigators never get lost.” A sound report attributes the first proposal to Mara, records the concession and identifies the revised claim. The concession defeats the universal first claim; it does not establish that training never helps. This is a model of attribution, not an interpretation of Plato.

1. Make a one-page chronology with rows for Socrates, Plato, Aristotle, Epicurus and Sextus, and columns for lifespan/source, dramatic context/source, composition/source and uncertainty. Leave unsupported cells explicitly unknown; do not spend this module researching exact dates outside the assigned sources.
2. Reconstruct the inquiry difficulty in four sentences; add one premise that the geometric episode would need to establish pre-existence. Mark the episode's observable result separately from Socrates' interpretation.
3. Write **400 words** explaining why a character's assertion is not automatically Plato's doctrine, using one move from each dialogue. Give two checked locators and identify a reasonable claim the text does support. Criteria: accurate speaker/function, a genuine inference, appropriate uncertainty, no fabricated historical facts.

For feedback, ask which sentence confuses textual evidence with historical inference. Revise that sentence and explain the change in fifty words.

## Independent check C03-M01-I (60 minutes)

**Invented exchange:** Dione asks whether knowing a danger is sufficient for acting courageously in facing it. Leon answers yes. Dione asks whether a frightened person can know the danger yet flee. Leon replies, “Then knowledge of danger alone is insufficient; perhaps knowledge of what should be feared is needed.” A commentator reports: “The playwright proves that courage is impossible.” With only this exchange and your assigned texts available, write 250 words auditing the report and comparing its attribution error with one risk in reading *Meno*. No tutor/AI content help, notes or examiner guidance during the attempt; access tools and dictation are allowed. Declare prior exposure. Criteria: distinguish assertion, revision and conclusion; state what remains undecided; use the comparison without identifying the invented people with ancient authors.

If attribution is insecure, reread the exact exchange, underline each speaker's words and list what changes. After two later study sessions, use this module's retrieval allocation to write from memory one difference between evidence about a dialogue and evidence about its composition; then verify.
