# Module 4 — Persons and continuity

**18 hours.** Guiding question: what survives through change, and must survival be numerical identity? Prerequisites: distinction between necessary/sufficient conditions and questions of evidence versus constitution. Outcomes: distinguish organism, memory/consciousness, psychological continuity and numerical identity; reconstruct a branching problem; state limits of hypothetical cases.

## Teaching explanation

Numerical identity means being one and the same entity. Qualitative similarity means sharing properties. Two identical-looking keys are two objects; one key can change color while remaining one key. Identity is transitive: if A is B and A is C, then B is C. That formal property creates pressure when a proposed survival relation can branch toward two distinct successors.

Locke asks what sort of thing is being traced. The continued life of an organism, sameness of material particles, sameness of an immaterial substance and sameness of a person need not receive the same criterion. Consciousness connects the person with past action and concern. Do not replace this with the loose slogan “a person is a list of memories”: apparent memory might be inaccurate, and ordinary forgetting raises difficulties for simple criteria. Interpret the author's argumentative role of consciousness before proposing a modern repair.

Psychological connectedness concerns particular links such as memories, intentions or character; continuity can involve overlapping chains of such links. A branching thought experiment asks whether two future individuals could each bear the relevant relation to an earlier person. If both do, and are distinct from one another, both cannot be numerically identical to the earlier person under ordinary identity logic. One may revise the relation, introduce a non-branching condition, or question whether identity is what chiefly matters in survival. Parfit's article must be consulted to attribute his argument and qualifications accurately; the logical problem alone does not prove his whole view.

An imagined operation is a stipulated model. Conceivability is not automatically biological feasibility or metaphysical possibility. State what the case assumes and whether the conclusion is conditional on that possibility. Likewise, the practical importance of memory does not by itself show that memory constitutes identity rather than providing evidence of it.

## Worked practice and output P4

Original example: Ari's archive is copied to Bea and Cal. Both have equally vivid apparent memories of Ari's childhood; Bea and Cal later disagree and occupy different rooms. The copying provides qualitative similarity and stipulated psychological links. It does not establish two numerical identities. If Ari = Bea and Ari = Cal, transitivity would require Bea = Cal, contradicting the case's distinct-successor stipulation. A theorist might say neither is Ari, or that the case shows the value of continuity despite lack of identity. The diagram alone does not select a theory.

Draw a four-layer map for a fictional patient before and after gradual neural change: organism, experiences/consciousness, direct memory links and overlapping psychological chains. Mark every arrow with whether it supplies evidence or a proposed constitutive condition. Add a branching version. Write **500 words** on why a non-branching clause could solve one logical problem while leaving a question about what matters to the patient. Reconstruct one Locke passage and one Parfit passage in separate 150-word notes, with checked locators before feedback.

## Independent changed case I4

**35 minutes, 350–450 words**, source-open with no help. Suri survives an accident in the same living body but loses all direct memories of school. A second person, Toma, receives fabricated memories closely resembling Suri's school memories, but has no causal continuity from Suri's actual experiences. Suri retains long-term projects and connected memories of later life. Compare a simple apparent-memory rule with one more careful account from the readings. Explain whether the supplied facts establish identity, merely evidence for it, or neither. State one limit of the stipulation. Criteria: sortal question, memory/continuity distinction, explicit inference and fair textual reconstruction.
