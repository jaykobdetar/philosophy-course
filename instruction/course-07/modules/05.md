# Module 5 — A different inquiry into self

**16 hours.** Guiding question: why might examining what we call “self” be a practical inquiry into suffering? Prerequisites: M4 distinctions and careful source attribution. Outcomes: identify the five aggregates; reconstruct one discourse argument; distinguish textual assertion from a guide's stronger interpretation; compare a bounded question with Locke without merging their projects.

## Reading and access

Read C07-R07, SN 22.59 complete in **Bhikkhu Sujato's translation**, plus C07-R11, IEP “Buddha,” **§§1.b, 3.b, 3.c and 3.d only**, complete sections. [Exact links](../readings.md). Sujato's official segmented text was inspected. The compressed repetitions are intentional; do not treat an empty segment as a missing new doctrine. The guide's section boundaries are the assignment, not an invitation to read the entire entry.

## Teaching explanation

The discourse considers form, feeling, perception, choices and consciousness. Sujato's “choices” and the guide's “mental formations” are translation choices associated with the same aggregate; record rather than erase that difference. One line of reasoning connects the aggregates' susceptibility to affliction and lack of command with their unsuitability as self. Another tests impermanence, suffering and the appropriateness of identification. The closing movement concerns disillusionment, fading desire and liberation. These features make the passage more than a criterion for deciding which future organism is numerically identical to a present one. Cite SN 22.59 segments 2, 5, 6 and 11 for these different roles. [Primary translation](https://suttacentral.net/sn22.59/en/sujato).

Reconstruction requires a bridge premise: why would the relevant kind of self be expected to have the properties under examination? Make that premise explicit, and distinguish what the discourse says from your analytic formulation. Do not infer “nothing whatsoever exists” or “moral responsibility is impossible” solely from a rejection of identifying the aggregates as self. Such conclusions require additional premises and texts.

The IEP guide discusses the historical transmission of sources and situates suffering, aggregates and non-self within a larger interpretation. It does not identify surviving texts with a stenographic transcript. Its broader account, including claims that range across the Nikāyas, exceeds what a single discourse alone establishes. A learner may question an interpretation while accurately reporting it. Cite the guide when using that interpretation; do not cite SN 22.59 as though every premise in the guide occurred there. [IEP](https://iep.utm.edu/buddha/).

A useful comparison first fixes a shared narrow question, then shows the different work done by each answer. “What role does consciousness play in claims concerning self?” permits comparison; “Which author gives the correct modern theory of personal identity?” may impose a question neither passage answers in that form. One early discourse in a Theravāda/Pāli collection cannot stand for all Buddhist or Indian philosophy. Reading it alongside Locke does not establish historical influence.

## Worked practice and output P5

Original comparison error: “The patient cannot control which memories appear, so Locke and the discourse both prove that the patient no longer exists.” This moves from lack of command to nonexistence, then assigns that conclusion to two authors without showing their different premises. A repair asks separately what consciousness contributes to Locke's account of a person and what role lack of control plays in this discourse's challenge to identification. The repaired comparison can identify tension without declaring the projects identical.

Prepare an evidence ledger with four rows: direct discourse claim; your reconstructed bridge premise; guide's stronger interpretive claim; question left unsettled. Give a segment/section locator for each sourced row. Then write **700 words** comparing one precise question with Locke. Include an account of practical purpose, one translation-sensitive distinction, one similarity and one non-equivalence. Tutor feedback must distinguish misquotation, unsupported attribution and a defensible disagreement with the guide.

## Independent changed case I5

**35 minutes, 350–450 words**, assigned sources open; no content help. An imagined therapy gives Lale more stable memories but does not give her command over pain, aging or involuntary thought. A commentator concludes: “Perfectly stable memory would satisfy both Locke and SN 22.59, so the two inquiries have the same goal.” Evaluate that inference using one located claim from each text. Do not infer that the therapy is technically possible. State a stronger thesis the supplied passages cannot establish. Criteria: accurate premise/conclusion, project difference, evidence boundary and explicit translation.
