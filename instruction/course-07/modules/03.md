# Module 3 — Epistemic injustice

**14 hours.** Guiding question: when does a credibility judgment wrong someone as a knower? Prerequisites: testimony, evidential support, distinction between epistemic and ethical evaluation. Outcomes: reconstruct testimonial injustice; distinguish it from deception and ordinary error; test an overbroad definition with a counterexample.

## Reading and access

C07-R05 Fricker, *Epistemic Injustice* (OUP 2007), chapter 1 complete. [Publisher/access record](../readings.md). Only contents/abstract were inspected during authoring. The account below is an instructional reconstruction requiring chapter verification before textual judgments. Hermeneutical injustice is not inferred from or assessed as though chapter 7 had been read.

## Teaching explanation

A credibility judgment estimates whether a speaker is a good source on a particular matter. A credibility deficit gives less credibility than is warranted. In the central testimonial-injustice pattern, an identity prejudice causes that deficit and the speaker is wronged in their capacity as a knower. These are connected conditions: it is not enough that a hearer disagrees, makes a mistake, or belongs to another group. Specify what credibility was warranted, what was actually given, the prejudicial mechanism and the epistemic wrong. Social power can shape whose words receive uptake even when nobody announces a discriminatory rule.

Ordinary error may be epistemically defective without meeting this specific pattern. Discounting a witness because an authenticated record proves that witness repeatedly falsified this kind of report has an evidential basis; discounting the same report because of a stereotype about the witness's group has a different explanation. Some cases combine justified caution with prejudice. The question then becomes whether the prejudice contributes to an unwarranted additional discount, not whether any adverse evidence exists.

Truth of the report does not settle the classification. A careful hearer can reasonably reject a true but poorly supported report; a prejudiced hearer can accidentally accept a true report. Conversely, a speaker can be wrong and still suffer an unjust prejudicial reduction in credibility. Keep truth, warrant and treatment of the person on separate lines. The philosophical diagnosis does not require pretending to know an actual person's motives: in real cases one must identify evidence for the mechanism and mark uncertainty.

## Worked practice and output P3

Original example: Noor and Eli independently report a broken sensor with equally clear photographs and the same training. A supervisor accepts Eli's account but dismisses Noor because “people from that community exaggerate faults.” The supplied comparison supports a prejudicial credibility deficit. Change the case so that Noor's photograph is demonstrably from an older device and the supervisor applies the same timestamp rule to everyone. A credibility reduction now has an evidential explanation; Noor being correct by coincidence would not restore the original mechanism. Neither example licenses a verdict about all unequal outcomes.

Write **600 words** analyzing a case you construct with four fixed facts: equal relevant expertise, a stated group stereotype, a measurable response to the testimony, and a practical consequence. Label any inferred fact. Then test the overbroad definition “testimonial injustice occurs whenever someone's true report is doubted” with a **150-word counterexample**. Revise the definition and explain one mixed case that remains difficult. Feedback asks which fact supports each condition; it does not supply a moral verdict for you to repeat.

## Independent changed case I3

**35 minutes, 350–450 words**, sources open, no content assistance. An accessibility tester reports that a kiosk fails with screen-reader software. The manager requests a repeatable log from everyone and receives none from this tester. The manager also says that disabled testers are “usually oversensitive” and discards this report while retaining equally undocumented reports from others. Later investigation shows the alleged software fault did not occur on this kiosk. Analyze the credibility assessment and alleged injustice separately from the eventual truth result. Include one rival causal explanation and what evidence would distinguish it. Criteria: necessary conditions, mixed evidence, precise attribution and no invented motives beyond stipulated facts.
