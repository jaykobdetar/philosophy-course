# Module 2 — Testimony and trust

**16 hours.** Guiding question: what grounds reasonable trust in another person's word? Prerequisites: M1 distinctions, Hume's general problem of experiential inference. Outcomes: reconstruct the Hume/Reid disagreement; vary reliability, witness dependence and background evidence separately; explain one limit of the comparison.

## Reading and access

C07-R03 Hume, *Enquiry* X Part I complete, and C07-R04 Reid, Brookes 1997, VI.24 complete. See [register](../readings.md). Hume §§87–89 were inspected; Brookes text was not. Do not attribute a precise formulation to Reid without a checked passage. This chapter of Hume discusses testimony in preparation for evaluating miracles; testimony itself includes ordinary reports.

## Teaching explanation

For Hume, testimonial confidence draws on experienced conformity between reports and events. What counts as support includes the witness's record, circumstances and competing evidence. An unusual report brings evidence from the testimony into comparison with experience concerning the event. Rare, unfamiliar and contrary to a law of nature are different descriptions: moving from one to another requires an argument. The text's frost example helps prevent the mistake that whatever exceeds one person's experience is impossible.

The contrast to investigate in Reid is between trust founded entirely on acquired evidence and an original disposition toward veracity and credulity, modified by experience. “Natural” does not mean every report must be accepted, every speaker is honest, or defeaters are irrelevant. Distinguish a psychological account of how trust develops from an epistemic argument about why it is justified. A fair comparison locates whether the authors disagree over the initial entitlement to trust, the role of accumulated experience, or both. Quote neither as simply saying “trust everyone” or “trust no one.”

Multiple witnesses help only insofar as their reports supply additional grounds. Twenty people repeating one unverified announcement are not twenty independent observations. Independence also differs from reliability: two highly reliable sources may share one instrument; two independent observers may each be poor at the task. Background evidence concerns the reported event, not only the reporters. In a philosophical model we may stipulate these facts; in an actual decision they would require evidence.

## Worked practice and output P2

Original example: a museum custodian says a gallery is closed. Three visitors repeat the custodian's notice. Treating this as four independent confirmations multiplies one informational source. A second custodian who directly checked the locked entrance supplies a distinct observation, though both may still be responding to the same mistaken schedule. This is a limitation to investigate, not an automatic reason to dismiss either speaker.

Build an **eight-cell matrix** for “the north bridge is closed”: high/low observed source accuracy × independent/shared report origin × compatible/strongly conflicting background evidence. In the low-accuracy condition stipulate 6 correct reports in 10 comparable trials; in the high condition 99 in 100. Independent means two separate direct observations; shared means one copied notice. Compatible background: storm closures happen locally. Conflicting: a current authenticated maintenance report says all bridges are open. For each cell write 80–100 words: what changes, a Hume-style evaluation, a Reid-style evaluation conditional on your checked reading, and what further fact would matter. Finish with a 250-word disagreement map. The matrix does not settle either theory solely by polling intuitions or multiplying percentages without an independence model.

## Independent changed case I2

**35 minutes, 350 words**, source-open; no live content help. Two school archivists report that a letter was written in 1810. Both have excellent records, but both used the same misdated catalogue; a trainee who inspected the letter reports 1818. A verified photograph shows a final digit compatible with either 0 or 8. Explain how report dependence and background ambiguity affect justified trust. Reconstruct how Hume and Reid could assess this without asserting they must disagree about the final verdict. State what the case cannot establish. Criteria: three variables kept distinct, fair reasons for trust, explicit remaining uncertainty.
