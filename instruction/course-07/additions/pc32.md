# PC-32 — A bounded Nyāya testimony encounter

**6 added hours; 0 replaced; net +6.** Take after M2. This is one primary encounter, not a complete Indian philosophy course. Outcomes: locate testimony among knowledge sources; reconstruct a trust condition; compare it with Hume/Reid while distinguishing text from translator commentary.

## Exact reading, context and source gate

Gotama/Gautama, *The Nyāya Sūtras of Gotama*, trans. **Satis Chandra Vidyabhusana**, *Sacred Books of the Hindus*, VIII, Allahabad, Pāṇini Office, **1913**: **Book I, chapter I, sūtras 1–8, including attached explanatory notes**, complete finite selection. Do not substitute a general summary or claim primary-source completion. No purchase or external contact is required by this packet.

Nyāya is a distinct Indian philosophical tradition, not a synonym for Indian thought or a version of the Buddhist discourse in M5. The initial sūtras situate inquiry in a broader concern with knowledge and liberation. The precise relation between testimony and a reliable speaker is the focus here. Treat the 1913 translator's explanatory prose as interpretation, not automatically as Gotama's own wording. A short encounter can display a substantive question while leaving dating, later developments and other schools outside scope.

## Teaching explanation

Testimony can be considered a source of knowledge rather than only a psychological cause of belief. A truth-conducive speaker condition asks about competence regarding the matter and faithful communication. Neither virtue in general nor social prestige guarantees competence about every topic. Conversely, a hearer may have good reason to trust a local expert who has little institutional status. These are conceptual distinctions for reading the selected definition, not claims that every nuance already appears explicitly in each sūtra.

Compare grounds of warranted acceptance, not only verdicts. Hume's experienced report/event conformity, Reid's initial dispositions and a Nyāya speaker-based condition might support the same everyday belief for different reasons. A disagreement about whether testimony is reducible to another source is stronger than agreement that unreliable reports should be doubted. Do not infer the stronger disagreement unless the bounded passage and a checked contextual account support it. The reading ledger must preserve that limit.

## Worked practice A32

Original case: a respected astronomer says that a bridge's timber is sound without inspecting it; a carpenter reports internal rot after testing a beam. Prestige and relevant competence diverge. A hearer should identify what each person actually knows and how the claim was conveyed before treating either title as a warrant. This case teaches a distinction; it is not a historical claim about Nyāya's answer.

Build a ledger with **eight rows, one per assigned sūtra**: translated statement in your own words, attached explanatory note, relevance to the testimony question, uncertainty. Then write **350 words** comparing one checked claim from sūtras 7–8 with Hume X.I and Reid VI.24. Reconstruct the reasoning, supply one objection and identify something the tiny selection cannot decide. If source access is still missing, leave these rows pending; do not invent their contents.

## Independent Q32-A / alternate Q32-B

After the source gate, release one only, **25 minutes, 250–350 words**, readings open and no help. **A:** A navigator accurately knows a harbor route but knowingly omits a dangerous turn when advising a rival. A stranger conveys the same route accurately after reading a reliable chart but cannot answer follow-up questions. Compare the basis of acceptance under one checked Nyāya condition and one Hume/Reid condition; state what is undecided about the stranger's competence. **B:** A normally reliable botanist makes an honest claim about an unfamiliar plant based on a mislabeled sample. A farmer without formal credentials correctly reports its local use from repeated observation. Distinguish competence, sincerity and warranted trust; use two located textual claims without assuming all the schools have the same theory. Neutral criteria: actual passages, conditions applied to facts, contextual care and limited inference.
