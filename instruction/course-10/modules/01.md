## Explanation

A valid deduction preserves truth from its premises to its conclusion: if all properly sealed chambers retain gas, and this chamber is properly sealed, it retains gas. The argument can be valid even if a premise is false. An induction goes beyond what its premises guarantee: the previous hundred seals worked, so the next probably will. Confirmation concerns how evidence bears on a hypothesis; it need not make that hypothesis certain. Do not treat these as mutually exclusive labels for whole sciences. A test may deductively derive a prediction, then use the result in an ampliative judgment about the hypothesis.

Hume's section IV asks what justifies the step from experienced regularities to unobserved cases. A change in nature is conceivable without contradiction, so demonstration cannot guarantee uniformity. An appeal to experience's previous success uses the very transition under examination. Merely adding more observations does not remove that justificatory dependence. The challenge is not that every prediction is false or that all habits are equally reliable. In section V, custom explains our expectations: repeated conjunction disposes us to expect one event upon encountering another. An account of how the expectation arises must be distinguished from a noncircular proof that it will be true. A defensible interpretation may discuss whether Hume is replacing an unrealistic demand for justification; anchor that further claim in the text rather than pretending the two questions were identical.

A hypothetico-deductive test usually has the form: hypothesis H, auxiliary assumptions A and initial conditions I jointly imply observation O. Observation O does not entail H: a rival may also imply O. Observation not-O deductively conflicts with the conjunction H-and-A-and-I if the observation report is accepted, but does not identify which conjunct failed. Confirmation is therefore comparative and sensitive to what a rival would predict. High probability of O given H is not automatically high probability of H given O.

## Worked invented example

A light flashes on 90 of 100 faulty batteries and on 90 of 900 sound batteries. Among faulty batteries the flash rate is 90/100 = 90%; among flashing batteries the faulty rate is 90/180 = 50%. The same 90 batteries appear in both numerators; the denominators answer different questions. A flash raises the faulty proportion from the initial 10% to 50%, so the flash is informative without being proof. These are stipulated population counts, not measured reliability claims.

## Independent changed case I1

A factory tests only batteries returned by customers. Of 100 returns, 80 are faulty; the flash appears in 72 faulty and 2 sound returns. A manager says, “A flash on any new retail battery means a 72/74 chance of fault, and successful prediction proves our fault theory.” In 250–350 words, identify what the data warrant, whether the retail conclusion follows, and how Hume's issue differs from a sampling defect.
