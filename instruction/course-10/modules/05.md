# C10-M05 — Realism and empirical adequacy

**16 hours. Guiding question:** What must one believe when accepting a successful scientific theory? Prerequisites: M01–04. ## Explanation

A scientific realist can interpret a theory literally and regard its successful account of observable and unobservable features as at least approximately true. Different realist positions qualify this commitment differently; do not infer that realism entails certainty in every detail of today's science. For this course, identify what the particular realist argument commits to rather than multiplying variants.

Constructive empiricism also takes scientific language literally: a claim about an unobservable entity says what it appears to say. Its distinctive position concerns the aim of science and the belief required for acceptance. Acceptance requires belief in empirical adequacy, roughly that the theory gets the observable phenomena right, while permitting agnosticism about the unobservable account. Acceptance also has practical dimensions: using the theory, pursuing its research program and speaking within its framework. Agnosticism about an entity is neither denial of that entity nor the claim that scientific sentences about it are meaningless.

The observable is not identical to the already observed. A theory that fits all data gathered so far may still misrepresent other observable phenomena. Thus empirical adequacy is a substantive commitment, not the trivial claim that “the records match themselves.” Observation also involves human capacities and circumstances; this course does not settle every instrument-mediated boundary dispute. If a case stipulates an entity is unobservable, accept the stipulation for the exercise rather than importing contested physics.

An inference to the best explanation proposes that a hypothesis's explanatory success supports its truth. A realist may argue that striking success across independent tests would be hard to account for if the theory radically misdescribed its subject. An empiricist can accept the success while questioning the move from explanatory virtue to truth about unobservables, pointing to empirically adequate rivals or to selection among theories. This is an argument to evaluate, not a deduction from successful prediction. Nor does one successful prediction automatically vindicate an entire ontology.

## Worked invented example

Model M says unobservable “lumes” carry energy between sealed chambers. It predicts all available meter readings. Model N, with a different unobservable mechanism, predicts the same readings. A realist may regard M's unification of several effects as evidence for lumes; an empiricist may use M while believing only that its account is empirically adequate. Neither view licenses pretending the models have been distinguished experimentally. If a later meter result contradicts M but fits N, the premise of empirical equivalence changes. Both views must respond to that evidence; the disagreement is not “realists test, empiricists do not.”

## Independent I5

Theory R posits unobservable “links” and predicts a previously unmeasured but observable glow pattern. Rival S predicts the opposite pattern. The new observation supports R. A research director concludes that accepting R now requires believing that links exist exactly as described and that constructive empiricism must reject R's use. Assess both claims in 300–400 words, distinguishing the changed evidence from the philosophical commitments. One hour, assigned text open, no coached comparison or examiner notes. Criteria: account for the new discriminatory evidence, state each acceptance commitment, and address the extent of warranted belief. [Examiner](../examiner-notes.md).
