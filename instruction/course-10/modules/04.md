# C10-M04 — Change in science

**18 hours. Guiding question:** When does an anomaly become a challenge to a scientific framework? Prerequisites: M02–03. Outcomes: distinguish normal science, anomaly, crisis and revolution; reconstruct an assigned Kuhnian claim; avoid treating historical change as an inevitable cycle.

**Reading:** Thomas S. Kuhn, *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions*, fourth edition, University of Chicago Press 2012, chapters II, VI, VII and IX complete (C10-R04). ## Explanation

Normal science works within achievements that a community takes as exemplars of successful research. Those achievements guide which questions are tractable, what methods count, and what solutions look like. Normal science is productive partly because researchers need not reopen every foundational assumption for each experiment. A paradigm in this introductory use is richer than a single hypothesis; resist calling any popular opinion a paradigm.

An anomaly is an expectation's failure within a framework. Anomalies require recognition and interpretation: what looks insignificant under one set of expectations may be urgent under another. A difficult puzzle does not by itself imply crisis. Researchers can rationally suspect technique, refine a calculation, or set a problem aside while other successes continue. A crisis involves sustained disturbance of confidence in the framework's ability to handle significant problems. The supplied text must support any claim about how this happens; do not infer it simply from a count of surprising observations.

A revolution changes important commitments, exemplars or standards of a field. This is not the mere addition of one more accurate measurement. Neither is it an assertion that all evidence becomes irrelevant or that one community's preference is as good as another's. Differences in standards can complicate comparison without eliminating every shared reason. Within the assigned chapters, distinguish Kuhn's descriptive historical claims from a normative claim that researchers ought always to resist novelty. Historical reconstructions are answerable to evidence about actual episodes; the invented episodes below only test conceptual distinctions.

A sequence of normal work, anomaly, crisis and replacement is a possible pattern, not a timetable. An anomaly may be resolved within the framework, a crisis may remain unsettled, and not every change in terminology overturns a field. When reading chapter IX, ask what incompatibility between old and new approaches makes change revolutionary; when reading II, ask how stable exemplars enable inquiry in the first place. A fair criticism should target one claim, rather than the slogan “science is subjective.”

## Worked invented example

A mapping community's standard instrument predicts rock density within a small margin. One region lies outside it. For a decade investigators improve calibration and map other regions successfully: the region is an anomaly, but those facts alone do not establish a crisis. Later an alternative maps the region, changes what counts as a basic measurement and reorganizes the field's accepted problems. This supplies grounds to investigate revolutionary change. It does not prove replacement was inevitable, nor that every old measurement was false.

## Independent I4

A fictional field adopts a new detector. Measurements become twice as precise, but its accepted entities, explanation standards and exemplary problems remain the same. In a neighboring field, no new detector appears, yet researchers reorganize old records around a different kind of cause and reject the previous exemplary solutions. A commentator calls the first a revolution and the second “mere wordplay.” Evaluate both judgments in 300–400 words. Specify an uncertainty about each classification rather than invent historical facts. One hour, sources open, no practice notes or hints. Criteria: consequential conceptual comparison and source-grounded limits. [Examiner](../examiner-notes.md).
