# C09-M02 — Space and experience

**18 hours. Guiding question:** How can a form of intuition constrain objects we have not yet encountered?

**Prerequisites:** M01's two classification axes. **Outcomes:** reconstruct a bounded argument from §§7–10, distinguish intuition from a hunch, and test an objection without substituting a psychological claim.

## Assigned reading

Kant, *Prolegomena*, Hatfield, Cambridge revised edition 2004, **§§6–13**, including the remarks attached to §13 in the assigned edition. Retain section labels in notes. The older Carus wording “visualisation” can narrow what a beginner hears in “intuition”; it is not an authorized equivalence of every use. [Source/access audit](../source-audit.md).

## Teaching

An intuition in this discussion is a singular, immediate representation, contrasted with a general concept. “Immediate” does not mean infallible, and “pure” does not mean exceptionally clear. Pure intuition abstracts from the sensory matter supplied by particular encounters. Space is treated as a form under which outer objects can appear to us; mathematical construction helps Kant explain how knowledge can extend beyond conceptual analysis without waiting for a survey of objects.

Here is a restrained reconstruction of §§7–10. First, mathematical cognition, as Kant understands it, requires construction in intuition rather than extraction from definitions alone. Second, the necessity claimed by such cognition cannot rest merely on observed examples. Third, a representation that concerns an independently given thing's intrinsic features would require that thing to be given; it would not explain anticipation a priori. Fourth, if the intuition concerns the form through which objects can be sensibly given to us, its application to appearances becomes intelligible. The proposed explanation therefore ties a priori cognition to a restriction: the result concerns objects under our conditions of sensibility, not their constitution independently of those conditions.

The third-to-fourth step is a good place for criticism. Has Kant excluded every alternative explanation, or shown only that his explanation is available? The learner must specify whether an objection attacks a premise, the sufficiency of the explanation, or the claim that it is the only possible explanation. Saying “space feels external” does not by itself answer a transcendental argument. Kant's proposal concerns how external experience is possible, not a report that people feel space inside their heads.

The incongruent-counterpart discussion in §13 asks why matching descriptions of parts and their internal relations may fail to determine substitutability in space. A right glove and its mirror image provide orientation. This is not a complete proof that any modern mathematical treatment of orientation is impossible. Its place in the text is to challenge a conception of space fully captured by the relevant intellectual description and to motivate the role of sensible intuition. Keep that historical aim distinct from what later geometry might permit.

**Worked original example:** A square is drawn badly on a sheet. Its measured sides differ. The imperfection tells us something about this inscription, but does not show that mathematical construction requires every physical drawing to instantiate its concept perfectly. The drawing can guide attention to a construction; the pencil marks do not supply mathematical necessity. Conversely, one cannot establish the dimensions of an actual room by considering the concept of a rectangle alone. Distinguish the pure construction, the physical exemplar, and the empirical application.

## Budget and practice

Reading 6h; teaching/reconstruction 3h; practice and 600-word objection 3h; feedback 2h; independent task 1.5h; revision/retrieval 2.5h = **18h**.

**P02:** Draw or describe in numbered prose a five-step reconstruction of §§7–10, assigning one locator to each step. Write **600 words** testing this objection: “If we can imagine a malformed triangle, geometrical necessity is only a habit.” Give the objection its strongest relevant form, distinguish imagining an inscription from constructing under a rule, and identify one remaining question the reply does not settle. Add a four-line ledger separating Kant's assertion, your inference, possible rival reading and evidence needed.

## Independent task I02

Source-open, 75 minutes plus 15 minutes citation check. A proposed alternative says: “We can prove theorems within two stipulated geometries. That shows spatial necessity derives entirely from definitions; no pure intuition is needed. Either geometry can be used to measure a physical field without observations.” Write 450–550 words identifying the distinct mathematical and empirical claims; reconstruct the Kantian move this challenges; say which part the example bears on and which requires more argument. Do not assume modern alternatives either automatically refute or confirm all of Kant. Criteria: exact target, construction/application distinction, bounded historical attribution and a genuine objection. This changes the explanatory premise from faulty imagination to competing formal structures.

## Repair, retrieval and next step

If “intuition” means a guess, replace it with the singular-representation contrast and retest the glove example. If the objection is purely psychological, explicitly write “For objective experience to be possible …” and ask whether your evidence addresses that claim. Three days later reconstruct the third-to-fourth step in 15 minutes, then correct it within the revision budget. Take the unanswered necessity question to [Module 3](03.md).
