# C09-M03 — Objectivity and causation

**26 hours. Guiding question:** What distinguishes perceiving one thing after another from perceiving that one event objectively follows another?

**Prerequisites:** M01–02; Course 5's Hume discussion. **Outcomes:** reconstruct a bounded Second Analogy argument, identify its rule-governance premise, distinguish causal principle from particular empirical law, and state an objection to the transition.

## Teaching

Apprehension is successive: attention takes in a manifold over time. But a sequence in my apprehension does not by itself show a sequence in the object. I can inspect a building from roof to ground or ground to roof without the building's parts coming into existence in that order. Kant contrasts this with an event, such as the ship's downstream motion, whose states belong in an objective order. His example is about that particular movement, not a claim that ships can never travel upstream.

The argument must do more than note that some experiences are hard to reverse. A slideshow can constrain the order in which I see stationary objects. A recording can display an event in reverse. The relevant distinction concerns what makes the judgment answerable to an order of the object, rather than the accidental order in which this viewer receives information. Nor can one simply consult time itself as an independently perceived object and read every event's temporal position off it.

A bounded reconstruction is: (1) to experience an event, I represent states as objectively ordered; (2) mere successive apprehension leaves that objective order undetermined; (3) determining an objective succession requires a rule under which the later state follows from a preceding condition; (4) Kant identifies the relevant necessary connection with causality; therefore (5) experience of events presupposes causal rule-governance. Step 3 and the identification in step 4 bear the philosophical weight. An objection that some regular sequences are accidental challenges the sufficiency of regularity; it does not defeat a claim Kant made only about frequency, because he is not offering frequency alone as the source of necessity.

Keep two levels apart. The causal principle concerns conditions of possible experience. Discovering that this particular disturbance has this particular cause needs empirical inquiry. The argument does not tell the learner whether a sensor failed because of heat, moisture or faulty wiring. A defensible criticism asks whether objective temporal determination really requires the stronger causal relation Kant invokes, and whether the account risks presupposing the lawfulness it seeks to establish. A defensible reply must show the work done by the rule in constituting an event judgment. Merely renaming sequence “causation” is insufficient.

In the *Prolegomena*, judgments of perception and judgments of experience help make the objective-validity issue visible. Do not reduce objectivity to an opinion poll: agreement among observers may itself require explanation. And do not conclude that thinking causally makes every causal guess true. A condition on cognition and the success of one attempted cognition are different matters.

## Budget and worked practice

Reading 8h; teaching and argument map 4h; paired-case practice 4h (including 1h causal/modal bridge); feedback 3h; independent task 2h; revision/retrieval 5h = **26h**.

**Worked original pair:** An archivist examines intact bowls from oldest catalogue label to newest. Next she witnesses one bowl fall and break. Both experiences contain successive perceptions. The labels determine her inspection order in the first case without establishing a succession of states in a bowl. In the second case, she judges one object's transition; that judgment commits her to an objective relation between states. The broken fragments are not proved to have been caused by the archivist's glance. The causal principle leaves the particular mechanism to inquiry.

## Independent task I03

Source-open, 90 minutes plus 30 minutes checks. Two remote cameras send images in arrival order. One shows an intact vase, then fragments; another shows a clock at 10:02 before an image marked 10:01 because the network delayed a packet. An analyst argues that all ordering is subjective because arrival order can be reversed. Write 650–800 words and a text timeline: separate the relevant successions, reconstruct Kant's response, identify what evidence the analyst would still need about the vase, and present one serious objection to the necessary-rule premise. Criteria: no inference from delivery order alone to event order; distinguish general principle/empirical identification; locate and evaluate the pivotal step. Network mediation changes the epistemic premise; a correct answer cannot simply repeat the bowl labels.
