# C09-M01 — Kant's question and vocabulary

**12 hours. Guiding question:** How could a judgment extend knowledge while being justified independently of experience?

**Prerequisites:** Course 5's distinction between a claim about ideas and a claim about what exists; elementary premise/conclusion reconstruction. **Outcomes:** classify a judgment with a reason, distinguish a priori justification from psychological origin, and state what the B Preface's proposed reversal is meant to explain.

## Assigned reading

Kant, *Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics*, Gary Hatfield translation, revised edition, Cambridge 2004, Preamble §§1–5; *Critique of Pure Reason*, Paul Guyer and Allen Wood translation, Cambridge 1998, B Preface **Bxvi–Bxxii**. Read both endpoints. These editions are access-dependent: [audit](../source-audit.md). Carus and Meiklejohn are orientation copies with different wording; the latter's unpaginated text does not establish A/B boundaries.

## Teaching

Kant distinguishes two questions that everyday speech often merges. What does a judgment say in relation to its concepts? What could justify it? In his subject–predicate account, an analytic judgment makes explicit what is already contained in the subject concept. A synthetic judgment adds something not contained there. By contrast, a priori and a posteriori concern the kind of warrant: independent of experience, or dependent on it. Acquiring the vocabulary through experience does not by itself make every judgment involving that vocabulary a posteriori.

“Every four-sided figure has four sides” is an original analytic example under the stated concept. “This four-sided tile weighs more than a kilogram” needs measurement and is synthetic a posteriori. “Every change has a cause” is Kant's kind of candidate for synthetic a priori status, not a fact the course assumes without argument. It purports to tell us something about events beyond unpacking the concept of change, yet claims necessity beyond a record of observed cases. The classification identifies a philosophical burden; it does not discharge it.

An innate belief could be false. An obvious belief could depend on repeated experience. A difficult proof could be a priori. Keep psychological acquisition, subjective certainty and justification in different columns. Likewise, “before experience” is not simply a date in childhood. It concerns what experience must presuppose or what warrants a judgment, rather than a claim that infants consciously possess philosophical propositions.

The critical question asks how synthetic a priori cognition is possible and how far it reaches. The B Preface proposes testing whether objects of experience must conform to conditions of our cognition. This is not a license to make reality whatever we wish. A condition applying to possible experience is meant to constrain every cognizer of the relevant kind, including mistaken individuals. The distinction between appearances and things considered in themselves will limit the result. Do not slide from “experience is conditioned” to “objects are imaginary.”

Two readings of a sentence may classify it differently when its concepts change. If “licensed square” is defined to mean a square registered in a ledger, registration follows analytically from that stipulated concept. It does not follow that every geometric square is registered. This is a scope mistake, not evidence that arbitrary definitions discover new objects.

## Budget and worked practice

Reading 4h; teaching and concept sheet 2h; worked/practice cases 2h; feedback 1h; independent task 1h; revision and retrieval 2h = **12h**.

**P01 concept sheet:** Make six rows: analytic, synthetic, a priori, a posteriori, appearance, condition of possible experience. Each row needs a definition, original example, nonexample and source locator. Then classify: (a) every registered member is registered; (b) all registered members attended yesterday; (c) the next event must have a cause. The worked distinction above supplies the model: name what a concept contains, identify the proposed warrant, then separate classification from truth. For (c), state why merely saying “Kant says so” is not a justification. Finish with a 150-word account of the B Preface's proposed reversal, using one checked locator.

## Independent task I01

Source-open, 45 minutes plus 15 minutes citation check; follow the [independence rules](../assessment.md). A club defines a “valid entry” as an entry approved by its committee. The committee also claims, “Every valid entry is aesthetically excellent, because approval is by definition a guarantee of excellence.” Write 300–400 words distinguishing what follows from the definition, what needs an additional premise, and whether learning the definition yesterday settles the a priori question. Compare the club's move with Kant's problem without calling Kant's argument a stipulative definition. Criteria: separate content/warrant, identify the disputed bridge, attribute the Kantian question accurately.
