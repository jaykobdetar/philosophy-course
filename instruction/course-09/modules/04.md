# C09-M04 — Limits of metaphysics

**12 hours. Guiding question:** Can reason recognize a boundary without knowing the objects beyond it?

**Prerequisites:** M03's distinction between the causal principle and an empirical causal judgment. **Outcomes:** distinguish legitimate use from overextension, explain why limiting knowledge is not proving nonexistence, and reconstruct one modest role for metaphysics after critique.

## Assigned reading

Kant, *Prolegomena*, Gary Hatfield translation, Cambridge revised edition 2004, **§§57–60**, complete. Read the section's distinctions about limits/bounds and analogy in this edition rather than treating an English word as self-explanatory. Older Carus samples were inspected; modern wording and full endpoint coverage remain [access-dependent](../source-audit.md).

## Teaching

Kant's restriction on cognition has two directions. We cannot take the conditions under which objects can be experienced and apply them without qualification to things as they are independently of those conditions. But neither can we turn our way of knowing into a proof that nothing could exist beyond its reach. “This procedure cannot establish X” and “X does not exist” are different claims. The latter needs an argument the former does not provide.

The *Prolegomena* presents metaphysics as arising from reason's own demands, not simply from a few careless thinkers. Reason seeks completeness and explanation; the critical task asks which of its claims have legitimate objective use. Therefore “Kant rejects every metaphysical question” misdescribes the project. Critique investigates the possibility and boundaries of metaphysical cognition, including the principles under which experience is possible. It also disciplines the temptation to treat ideas extending beyond experience as if they supplied determinate knowledge of supersensible objects.

An important difficulty is whether speaking about a boundary already claims knowledge beyond it. A careful answer distinguishes a negative limitation of our cognition from a positive description of another domain. The distinction is philosophically contestable, but the contest must concern the actual claim. Criticizing a detailed account of a supersensible object's properties will miss a position that refuses such an account.

In §58, analogy concerns a relation rather than straightforward similarity of unknown intrinsic natures. This permits a more restrained form of thinking than attributing human psychological features to a supersensible being. It does not simply restore, under the word “analogy,” the unrestricted theoretical knowledge critique denied. The learner should identify which relation is being asserted and which intrinsic claim is withheld. One can object that even the relational assertion exceeds what is justified; that is a relevant, specific objection.

**Worked original example:** A measurement procedure detects only charged particles under specified conditions. Failure to register something does not prove that all reality consists of charged particles. Nor does describing the device's detection limit tell us the intrinsic nature of every undetected entity. This illustrates the difference between a restriction and an unrestricted negative conclusion. It is only an analogy: Kant's conditions of possible experience are not an instrument that we can replace tomorrow with a better detector. That disanalogy matters because it prevents a misleading empirical reading of a transcendental claim.

## Independent task I04

Source-open, 45 minutes plus 15 minutes citation check. A critic argues: “If Kant cannot know the intrinsic nature of a supersensible ground, he cannot even claim that nature stands in any relation to such a ground. Relational analogy is just disguised dogmatism.” Write 350–450 words explaining the objection, reconstructing the strongest bounded reply available in §§57–60, and identifying what the reply must not claim. Criteria: distinguish relational/intrinsic claims, acknowledge the burden of justification, and avoid treating critique as a proof either of God's existence or nonexistence. This task challenges the permission for analogy; it does not merely repeat the practice's inference from absence of measurement.
