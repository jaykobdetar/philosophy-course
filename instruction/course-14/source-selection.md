# Selecting and bounding topic-dependent reading

Required fixed reading: [Reference and research protocol](../../current/reference.md), from its opening through “Reusable assignment forms.” Read the local [access guidance](../../current/access.md), opening and “How to acquire only what you need,” plus the capstone paragraph at the end of “Advanced seminars and capstone,” before acquiring any new source. The capstone inherits the question and verified reading from Course 13; it does not require buying new books. Essential source access is a checkable dependency, not a promise made by these materials.

## Execute this procedure in M1–M2

1. Write the question and two or three search concepts. Search your seminar dossier first. Identify one primary argument that the answer will interpret, improve or challenge; record its actual edition and exact section. “Kant on freedom” is not a locator.
2. Locate one scholarly orientation entry through the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy or an equivalent named academic reference already available. Select the subsection corresponding to the question, record heading and URL/date, and read that subsection plus enough surrounding context to identify its target. Its bibliography supplies leads, not evidence that you have read the cited texts.
3. Discover one serious rival through the reference bibliography or PhilPapers. Search a neutral formulation as well as your favored thesis. Verify author, title, publication venue/date and publication version at a publisher, journal, institutional repository, library catalogue or the item itself. An abstract establishes the stated aim; it rarely establishes the entire argument.
4. Open the actual source through an authorized available route. Record readable page numbers, translator/edition, notes/appendices relevant to the inference and any access limits. Distinguish published version from accepted manuscript. Do not assume their pagination matches. If inaccessible, retain a discovery note, seek an already authorized alternative or narrow the question. Do not cite unseen paragraphs as support or buy/contact anyone automatically.
5. Trace one backward citation and one later citing or recent related item. Explain whether each changes your map. For a contemporary debate, include a dated search at the time of study; a 2026 authoring snapshot is not a current-literature guarantee. “No relevant update found using these queries on this date” is appropriately bounded.
6. For each candidate ask: What exact premise or interpretation does it bear on? Is it independent evidence or a repetition of the same source? Does it challenge the thesis? Is the relevant range readable within the budget? Keep sources doing a distinct job; drop merely prestigious names. A likely working set is eight to twelve items, with no forced count.
7. Freeze a provisional reading contract using T2. Assign actual pages/sections before close reading. Allocate roughly 40–70 pages of additional close reading in M2 plus rechecking seminar notes. Keep orientation, skimmed introductions and close reading distinguishable in the log. Dense formal or translated passages may require narrowing the range.
8. If the contract cannot support the central claim, change that claim now. When new evidence arrives later, update the question, ranges and hours explicitly. Source selection is a reasoned decision that remains revisable, not a claim to exhaust a field.

## Choose a method that fits the evidence

Interpretive question: compare two readings of a bounded passage using wording, argumentative role, genre and relevant context. A useful observation discriminates between the readings; popularity alone does not. If a translation matters, compare identified translations or a reliable translator's note and explain the consequence. Do not infer original-language competence from an English comparison.

Argumentative question: reconstruct the strongest actual argument, distinguish validity from the acceptability of premises, and target one premise or inference. A counterexample must satisfy the stated conditions while defeating the conclusion. If it changes an assumption, say which assumption and why that reveals a limit.

Comparative question: fix the question and unit of comparison before pairing passages. Explain each position in its own terms, identify one defensible common issue and one non-equivalence. A shared English word is insufficient evidence that two traditions share a concept. Use source-specific genre and translation records.

Applied question: separate the normative principle, conceptual bridge and empirical premises. Invented facts can isolate an inference but cannot establish what actual institutions do. If real-world data are essential, select and assess a legitimate source for that fact; otherwise state a conditional conclusion.

Phenomenological or genealogical question: state the descriptive or explanatory target and the evidence your method needs. A description of experience alone does not establish universal psychology; an account of a belief's origin alone does not refute its truth. Identify the additional bridge argument if making either move.

These are local decision procedures. Mixed methods are permitted when each step has a distinct job and the entire question remains feasible. A topic that requires a new language, unavailable archive, large empirical survey or full new specialist literature needs an adjacent bounded question or an explicitly separate project.

## Access and citation checkpoint

Before M3, every essential source has an actual readable copy and pinned range. Before submission, every quotation is checked character by character against the edition used, with ellipses and alterations disclosed. Verify every significant attribution in context. A modernized adaptation is attributed as an adaptation. If the exact source cannot be checked, remove the quotation and unsupported attribution or leave the relevant criterion unresolved. Keep rights/access notes per item: public availability alone does not establish redistribution permission. No full copyrighted text is supplied by this course.
