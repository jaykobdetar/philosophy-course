# Milestone 3 — Argument plan and trial section

**20 hours.** Guiding question: Does the paper's central inference survive being stated plainly? Prerequisites: P1, P2 and access to the selected primary and rival ranges. Outcomes: build a dependency outline, write a 1,000-word central section, distinguish exposition from defense, and revise a failed inference before expanding the draft.

## Teaching: test the bridge

An outline lists dependencies, not only topics. “Author A; Author B; my opinion” gives no reason the conclusion follows. Write the pivotal inference in ordinary language and label what is supplied by the source and what you add. A valid pattern can still rely on a false premise; an attractive conclusion can still outrun its reasons.

Suppose the invented thesis is that two agreeing reports provide two independent grounds. Premise one says the reports have different writers. Premise two says different writers imply independent evidence. The conclusion follows only if premise two is true, and a common copied observation defeats it. A repair distinguishes distinct speakers from distinct evidential origins. That is substantive progress, but it leaves open whether partially dependent reports add some evidence. Restricting the conclusion to “agreement alone is insufficient to establish independence” avoids claiming that dependent testimony is worthless.

For an interpretation, the bridge may be a claim about argumentative role: a sentence is best read as a restriction because the subsequent argument would otherwise contradict it. Show the textual dependency and compare a plausible alternative. For a comparative paper, explain why the two passages address the selected common issue before evaluating their disagreement. You can write a careful descriptive or genealogical argument without forcing it into formal logic, but the evidence-to-conclusion connection must remain visible.

## Work sequence

1. Complete T3. Give each planned section an argumentative job and provisional word share totaling 5,000–7,000. Flag the one section whose failure would most damage the paper. Make a dependency map of claims rather than authors.
2. In a 300-word practice exercise, reconstruct the strongest rival with its motivating reason. Write a counterexample to one premise; explain why it meets the premise's conditions. If it does not, revise it or acknowledge it as a changed-scope case.
3. Write P3, a 1,000-word trial section from the center of the argument. Suggested allocation: target claim and terms 150; accurate reconstruction 250; inference and support 300; strongest objection/reply 250; consequence and limit 50. Cite actual source ranges; invented facts are labelled. Do not spend the section warming up with general history.
4. Ask a reviewer to name the weakest arrow and the most plausible rival interpretation, with actual textual evidence. Revise the map before polishing prose. If the main move fails, record why and narrow or change the question. A defensible negative result can be the revised contribution.
5. Complete F3 independently in 60 minutes: the reviewer supplies the unseen method-sensitive constraint specified in [assessment](../assessment.md). Save the original and changed argument. Explain what was preserved and what had to change.

Criteria: explicit pivotal inference; accurate attribution; distinction between source claim and improvement; objection targets the real premise; conclusion no stronger than the support. The trial is not approved merely because it reaches 1,000 words.
