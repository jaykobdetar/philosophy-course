# Milestone 4 — Full first draft

**40 hours.** Guiding question: Can a reader follow and assess one sustained contribution from question to conclusion? Prerequisite: tested P3 and resolved access to essential sources. Outcomes: write a complete 5,000–7,000-word paper in your own words, integrate citations during drafting, sustain the argument and expose its strongest vulnerability.

## Assigned reading

Read [shared rubric](../../../current/assessment.md), “Question and claim,” “Organization and expression,” and “Objection and reply”; [reference protocol](../../../current/reference.md), source-note paragraph; T3–T4 in [templates](../templates.md); the worked draft/revision example. ## Teaching: exposition earns its space

Exposition equips the reader to evaluate your move. It should specify the claim you target, the reasons for it and relevant terms or context. A survey of everything an author says obscures the question. Defense begins when you explain why a claim should be accepted or how a proposed interpretation handles evidence better than its rival. Make this transition explicit so readers can distinguish accurate reporting from your contribution.

A sustained argument needs stable terms and controlled scope. If “independent” means separate writers in one section and separate evidential origins in another, a persuasive-looking conclusion can depend on equivocation. Keep a term sheet with definitions, disputed alternatives and examples. Where an ambiguity is the subject of the paper, label both senses and explain the stakes.

Treat objections as tests of dependencies. An objection to the truth of a central premise is different from a complaint that the paper fails to show it. A reply might defend the premise, provide missing evidence, distinguish cases, or weaken the conclusion. Saying the opponent also has problems does not answer the objection to your inference. A limitation tells the reader the boundary of the result; it should be reflected in the actual thesis and conclusion, not hidden in a final disclaimer.

## Work sequence

1. Draft the central argument first from P3, then the minimum exposition needed to understand it. Write the introduction and conclusion after the argument is visible. Reserve about 26 hours of independent drafting in several sessions. All assessed prose must be yours; use feedback to locate problems rather than obtain replacement paragraphs.
2. Use a provisional 6,000-word architecture: question/contribution 500; necessary background/interpretation 1,200; central argument 1,800; serious objection and reply 1,500; implications/limits/conclusion 1,000. Change these shares for your method while staying within 5,000–7,000 words. No section exists solely to reach length.
3. Attach T4 claims to sources as each section is written. A citation placeholder may identify a problem in a working draft, but it cannot survive into the final paper. Mark empirical claims needing evidence and remove unsupported generalizations.
4. Practice a reverse outline: one sentence giving each paragraph's job, one arrow to the claim it supports. Find a paragraph that could be removed without changing the argument and either justify its role or cut it. Check that the conclusion answers the opening question with the same scope.
5. Complete F4 independently, 45 minutes, using the new constraint provided in assessment. This is a small fresh reasoning sample inside drafting time, not a second paper. Save its implication for the manuscript.
6. Read the full P4 draft once as a skeptical reader. Submit the entire draft, bibliography, T4 ledger, term sheet and a 250-word vulnerability note identifying the weakest inference and possible repair. Feedback here diagnoses organization and argument; M5 conducts the focused critical review.

Criteria: complete line of reasoning; visible modest contribution; fair reconstruction; citations at the point of use; real objection and responsive reply; scope maintained throughout. Completion of a first draft is a milestone, not a completion verdict on the capstone.
