# Milestone 2 — Literature and source dossier

**26 hours.** Guiding question: Which sources actually bear on my question, and what can I responsibly claim from them? Prerequisite: P1 and readable essential sources. Outcomes: build an annotated bibliography with exact read scopes; map a foundational argument and serious rival; distinguish discovery, metadata verification and substantive inspection; check quotation and attribution integrity.

## Teaching: sources have jobs, not votes

A literature map is organized around a disagreement. For example, one argument treats reliability of production as sufficient for a recipient's justified belief; another requires accessible reasons; a third disputes that they are answering the same question. The map should show which premise each source defends and which alternative it actually engages. Three summaries in chronological order do not yet explain a debate. Do not attribute this invented example to any historical author without checking the actual text.

A citation can do different jobs. A primary passage supports an attribution; a commentary supports a reading of that passage; an empirical study may support a premise about behavior; none automatically settles a normative inference. Track the passage's speaker and dialectical role. A source may accurately report an opponent's claim while rejecting it. Quoting that sentence as the author's conclusion reverses the argument.

Reading the relevant range is legitimate when disclosed. Context is sufficient only when you can identify the question, local argument and qualifications that affect your use. If a relevant qualification appears outside the planned range, expand and log it. Bibliographic verification answers “Does this item exist in this version?” Substantive verification answers “Does this passage support this use?” Rights/access verification is a third question. Keep their statuses separate.

## Work sequence

1. Retrieve P1's question, then execute the selection procedure with a bounded search session. Use one backward and one later/recent connection. Save queries, dates, reasons to include/exclude and unavailable leads. Stop adding sources when each argumentative job is adequately supported and serious dissent is represented; record remaining gaps.
2. Build P2: usually eight to twelve source cards of 120–180 words each plus metadata/ranges. Relevance governs the count. For each card write the argument, its job in your paper and one limitation. Mark whole item versus selected portion. Do not write “reviewed” when you skimmed only the abstract.
3. Make a one-page debate map with at least two genuinely competing answers. Draw an arrow only when you can write the target premise and locator. Identify whether a source uses the same primary text as another; dependence can limit the apparent breadth of support.
4. Practice a 300-word source-use paragraph on your own project. Label each sentence A (attribution), I (your inference), C (criticism) or B (background). For every A sentence supply a checked locator; for I give the warrant. Remove ornamental citations.
5. Have a reviewer inspect two cards against the actual source passages, including the strongest rival. Resolve one interpretive ambiguity by distinguishing two readings and the evidence that favors one. Complete independent F2, 45 minutes, after practice. F2 tests source-use reasoning; the final M8 audit separately tests the learner's actual paper.

Concrete exercise: write an annotation for an unavailable article whose abstract promises a critique. Your output should be a discovery record with the actual abstract locator and a statement of what is not established. Then annotate an accessible source actually read. Compare the two records. Assessment rewards the accurate limit, not an invented full argument.
