## Teaching and worked example

Smart’s proposal concerns what the reported process is, rather than a synonymy between mental and neurological vocabulary. A person can report an experience without knowing its physical nature. Thus the epistemic difference between knowing a sensation and knowing neuroscience does not by itself refute identity. Identity is also stronger than correlation: two regularly correlated events could still be distinct; an identity says there is one event or process described in different ways.

The paper asks whether objections force the addition of nonphysical properties or processes and invokes parsimony when competing accounts fit the facts. Parsimony is a reason within an argument, not a demonstration that science has already identified every sensation. Putnam’s multiple-realization challenge then needs careful targeting: different realizers pressure a universal neural-type identity, but do not automatically show that particular mental occurrences are nonphysical. Do not treat identity, realization and dependence as synonyms. Realization specifies how a role is implemented; dependence alone need not be identity.

Invented example: a witness knows “the warning flash occurred” without knowing the exact electrical discharge. Different routes to knowledge of one event do not entail two events. The analogy removes a bad inference; it does not prove that sensations are flashes or that Smart is correct.

## Tasks, criteria and timing

Budget: 2 h reading; 1 h explanation/practice; 0.5 h feedback; 0.5 h independent task; 0.75 h revision; 0.25 h seven-day retrieval = **5 h**.

**P-SM:** Write a 500-word reconstruction with separate identity, meaning and explanatory-economy claims. Compare one human and one hypothetical differently constituted system that occupy the same functional role. Identify which identity claim the example pressures and which it does not. Give two actual Smart locators.

**I-SM (30 min):** A critic argues: “An after-image is orange and oval; no neural process is orange and oval; therefore a sensation cannot be a neural process.” Reconstruct the argument, identify precisely what bears the color and shape predicates on each side, and assess a Smart-style reply. State a remaining objection that would survive simply correcting a confusion between the represented object and the experiential process. Use 350 words, with actual Smart locators, and no live content help.

**A-SM:** A critic accepts that mental and neural descriptions need not be synonymous but argues that a sensation is experienced as a seamless unity, whereas a neural process has spatially distinguishable parts; identity is therefore impossible. Assess whether the experienced unity establishes absence of physical parts in the process. Distinguish a claim about how the process is presented from an asserted property that could genuinely conflict with identity; do not rely on different vocabulary alone.

**D-SM (15 min):** Explain why “always correlated” is weaker than “identical,” then give an objection to an identity claim that does not rely solely on different vocabulary.

Criteria: a fair primary reconstruction, correct target of objection, explicit inferential limit and actual locators. Repair by separating “means,” “is,” “realizes” and “depends on” in four sentences, then test them on a new example. Continue to A-M04; keep the primary record for the final mind/understanding row.
