## Teaching and worked example

Lewis accepts that experience can supply knowledge of what it is like while disputing that the gain must consist in new phenomenal information. Remembering, imagining and recognizing are abilities that factual lessons need not supply. The hypothesis is stronger than the observation that people gain skills: it proposes an account of the relevant knowing-what-it-is-like. A critic can accept the abilities while maintaining that new phenomenal facts explain them. Lewis’s reply distinguishes a causal story about traces left by experience from the metaphysical interpretation that those traces represent an otherwise inaccessible kind of fact.

Knowing an experience under a description can mix information and ability. Someone may recognize a taste without knowing its name, then learn the name later. That additional identification does not establish the special nonphysical information at issue. Likewise abilities and information often arrive together, so noticing that “knowledge” has several uses cannot by itself settle the argument. The primary task is to show which premise in Jackson Lewis denies and whether the denial accounts for the whole gain.

Invented example: after hearing an unusual chord, Leena can imagine and recognize it but does not know the instrument. Later she learns that it was played by a reed instrument. The later descriptive fact differs from the earlier recognitional capacity. The distinction helps specify the dispute but does not establish that no factual learning accompanied the first experience.
