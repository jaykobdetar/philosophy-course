## Teaching and worked example

What a sentence conventionally means need not determine a complete assertion without reference and context: “she is ready” requires identifying someone and what she is ready for. An implicature is a further communicated content that can be worked out through assumptions about the exchange, background knowledge and the speaker’s contribution. It is not simply anything an observer happens to infer. The speaker must be accountable for the inference in the relevant communicative setting.

The cooperative principle organizes expectations about conversational purpose. Quality, quantity, relation and manner concern truth/evidence, information, relevance and clarity. They are not a moral checklist requiring every conversation to be maximally informative. A conspicuous apparent failure can invite an inference that preserves a presumption of cooperation; an unnoticed misleading violation does not function in the same way. Opting out and conflicting demands are also distinct.

Invented example: A asks whether a report is ready to publish. B says, “The title is beautifully formatted.” In a shared context where B has read the report and can judge its argument, the weak relevant praise can communicate a reservation. The sentence does not entail that the argument is bad. If B adds “and the argument is excellent; I was answering your question about layout,” the implication may be cancelled by clarified purpose. If B has seen only the title, the same inference may never arise. The missing context matters.

## Tasks and budget

Use 1.5 h reading and context notes, 0.75 h teaching/practice, 0.5 h feedback, 0.5 h independent work, 0.5 h revision and 0.25 h delayed retrieval: **4 h**.

**P-GR:** For the worked exchange, make a 400-word table: conventional content; context required for what is said; proposed implicature; inferential steps; a context that blocks it. Then analyze a new practice case: asked whether all samples passed inspection, a technician says “Some passed.” State the assumption that makes “not all” a plausible implication and construct an explicit cancellation. Do not write it into the truth conditions without argument.

**I-GR, fresh (30 min):** A security officer knows exactly where a protected witness is staying. Asked for the address, the officer replies, “I cannot disclose that.” A journalist concludes that the officer has implicated ignorance by providing less information than requested. Assess the inference using Grice’s treatment of opting out, the purpose of the exchange and the difference between what is said and implicated. Contrast the case with a person who conceals ignorance while pretending to answer fully. Use 300–400 words, source-open, no live content help.

**A-GR, alternate:** A surveyor has evidence only that a depth is between four and six metres. Asked for the exact depth, the surveyor gives that interval and explains the measurement limit. An observer calls this a deliberate misleading violation of quantity. Diagnose whether conflicting informational and evidential demands can explain the answer; distinguish a clash of maxims from deception and identify what extra context would be needed to infer a further conversational message.

**D-GR (15 min, after at least seven days):** Someone answers “A few chairs remain” to “Can our group of twelve sit together?” Identify what the hearer must know to infer whether enough remain, and explain why the utterance alone does not entail a precise number. This is early packet retrieval; a later common-core delayed check can sample use/context again, charging its own allocated time once.

Criteria: distinguish levels of content, state shared assumptions, work out the inference, and test a cancellation/context change. Repair an entailment/implicature confusion by writing one situation where the assertion is true and the alleged implication false. Then proceed to the option’s next module and retain this packet’s evidence for route credit.
