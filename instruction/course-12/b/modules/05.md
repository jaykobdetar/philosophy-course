# C12B-M05 — Categories and perception

## Guiding question and outcomes

How can a category affect the aesthetic properties a work seems to have?

By the end: Distinguish standard, variable and contra-standard features; justify category selection; distinguish predicted and experienced change.

## Teaching explanation

For Walton, a feature’s aesthetic contribution can depend on the category under which a work is perceived. Standard features help make something recognizable as a work of that kind; variable features permit differences within it; contra-standard features tend to count against membership or make a work striking in that category. The same feature can play different roles in different categories. Category effects therefore need not be mere changes in verbal description after perception is finished.

Yet not any category chosen on a whim is equally correct. The work’s features, its origins, the artist’s intentions and historically available categories can bear on the appropriateness of a category. Keep two claims apart: perceiving under a category can alter experience, and a given category is the right one for this work. An introspective report can supply limited evidence for the first; it cannot by itself establish the second. Reproductions introduce another limit: texture, scale, sound and duration may be missing. A theory predicting an effect is not a report that you experienced it.

## Worked practice example

Invented example: a slow unchanging drone may seem static as a conventional melody but richly shifting as a study of timbre. Calling pitch stability “standard” in the second category changes what counts as a significant variation. The example illustrates a possible effect, not evidence that either category fits an actual composition.
