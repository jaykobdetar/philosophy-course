## Teaching explanation

Hume begins with diversity in taste but resists the conclusion that every verdict has equal authority. A standard depends on judges whose perception and judgment have been improved: delicacy, practice, comparison, good sense and freedom from prejudice matter to their assessment. These are conditions for reliable appreciation, not a demand that a person like whatever is popular. The relevant question is how to identify the qualifications without already presupposing the disputed verdict.

A circularity worry arises if the good works are simply those admired by good judges and good judges are simply those who admire the good works. The reply must identify some independently assessable practices or capacities. A cultural objection asks whether inherited training, access or prejudice shapes what is taken as delicacy and good sense. It is insufficient to answer that all evaluation is subjective; one must show how this problem affects the proposed standard. Hume also accommodates some differences connected with temperament and historical situation. Do not turn a qualified standard into a prediction that competent judges always converge on every preference.

## Worked practice example

Invented example: two listeners disagree about a quartet. One can identify inner voices, has heard comparable performances and can explain the balance; the other heard a compressed phone recording once. This difference can justify further inquiry into authority without proving that the first listener’s overall verdict must be right.
