# C12B-M03 — Kant on beauty

## Guiding question and outcomes

How can a judgment based on feeling claim the agreement of others?

By the end: Explain the four moments with different examples; distinguish disinterest from indifference; test a universality claim without converting it into a poll.

## Teaching explanation

The four moments describe judgments of taste under quality, quantity, relation and modality. Disinterested satisfaction concerns the relation between pleasure and desire for the object’s existence or possession. It is compatible with close attention and strong pleasure. Universal validity without a determining concept concerns a claim on others’ agreement even though no concept proves the object beautiful. Purposiveness without a determinate purpose concerns the form as experienced in the activity of our faculties, rather than success at a specified use. Exemplary necessity concerns the kind of assent demanded, not a causal prediction that everyone will actually agree.

These distinctions keep taste apart from the agreeable and the good. Wanting to eat a fruit, judging it nutritionally good and finding its arrangement beautiful involve different grounds. Kant’s account is not simply Hume’s qualified-judge procedure: it asks about the basis of a distinctive claim made in judging. A counterexample must first identify which kind of judgment the speaker is making. A dispute may concern whether a judgment is pure, whether interests entered its basis, or whether the claimed common ground is defensible. Different objections need different replies.

## Worked practice example

Invented example: liking a vase because it can be sold for a profit supplies an interest in its existence; enjoying the arrangement of its lines need not. Wanting to keep looking is not by itself proof that the aesthetic satisfaction is interested. The distinction concerns the ground of the liking, not the intensity of attention.
