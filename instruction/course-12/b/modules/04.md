# C12B-M04 — Defining art

Baseline allocation: **16 hours**. Prerequisites: Courses 1, 6 and Course 9 Kant vocabulary; prior modules in this option.

## Guiding question and outcomes

Can classification as art depend on theory and history?

By the end: Compare an open-concept approach and an artworld account; separate classification from evaluation; identify evidence a difficult case needs.

## Teaching explanation

Weitz challenges the demand for a closed definition expressed in necessary and sufficient conditions. An open concept allows new cases to call for decisions about extension; resemblance and continuity can matter without an essence fixed once for all. This does not mean every classification is arbitrary or that criticism loses its use. Evaluative theories may direct attention to important features even if they fail as definitions.

Danto asks how objects that are visually indiscernible can differ in artistic status. Seeing something as art can require a background of theory and history. The relevant “is” of artistic identification is not ordinary numerical identity: a painted area can be identified as Icarus without literally being a person. Do not reduce the paper to “a museum declares it art.” The argument assigns work to an interpretive context that makes the object intelligible as art. A comparison must therefore ask different questions of a case: is a concept being extended for intelligible reasons, and what theoretical background makes an artistic identification possible? Neither route alone establishes that the work is aesthetically good.

## Worked practice example

Invented example: a steel tray in a workshop and an indistinguishable tray deliberately presented as a response to industrial labor occupy different histories. Danto makes those histories relevant to seeing-as; Weitz asks how admitting the new case extends an open concept. Calling both trays dull evaluates them only after a classificatory question has been addressed.
