# C12C-M05 — Freedom and ambiguity

## Teaching explanation

Sartre’s lecture opposes the idea that a fixed human essence can relieve agents of responsibility for what they make of themselves. Choosing involves a commitment to value, and the lecture presses the significance of that commitment beyond private preference. The argument is not that people can achieve any outcome they wish. A choice can occur under severe constraint, and acknowledging responsibility does not establish unlimited power.

Beauvoir’s account of ambiguity begins from a human existence that is both factical and transcending: we are beings in circumstances who also take up projects. An ethics must work with this tension rather than escape into either pure mechanism or unconstrained sovereignty. Her Part I provides a basis for questioning how freedom becomes an ethical task; later detailed classifications should not be imported as if already assigned. Compare one shared issue, such as whether accepting a situation removes agency or how a project’s value is warranted. An objection must distinguish responsibility for a response from blame for conditions the agent did not create. Translation checks matter especially where “abandonment,” “despair,” “freedom” or “ambiguity” carry the comparison. Retained Mairet work supports conceptual overlap, not a quotation in Macomber’s words.

## Worked practice example

Invented example: a caregiver can accept a night shift or refuse it, but every option has serious costs because public support is absent. Saying there is a choice identifies a structure of agency; saying the caregiver is equally able to realize every project ignores the situation. A comparison can ask how each assigned argument treats responsibility without equating it with institutional blame.
