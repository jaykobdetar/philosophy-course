How does tracing practical consequences clarify a philosophical dispute?

## Teaching explanation

James’s pragmatic method asks what conceivable difference a disputed idea would make in experience and conduct. A dispute that generates no identifiable difference may turn out to concern words rather than competing expectations. This is a procedure for clarification; it is not the rule “believe whatever is profitable.” Consequences include long-run inquiry, consistency with other beliefs and resistance encountered in experience, not merely a pleasant feeling now.

The lecture on truth investigates how ideas become validated in experience and connected with other truths. It is tempting either to translate this into a conventional correspondence theory or to caricature it as arbitrary wish fulfillment. Both moves erase the disputed part of the account. Reconstruct what “working” must mean in the relevant passage and what constraints the surrounding argument places on it. A proposition can be comforting and fail inquiry; a temporarily inconvenient belief can survive tests. A serious objection asks whether successful verification explains truth or only our warrant for belief. This is a philosophical disagreement about the account’s explanatory target, not an accusation that James never cared about evidence.

## Worked practice example

Invented example: two mapmakers disagree whether a safe route is “direct” when both recommend exactly the same turns, predict the same journey time and acknowledge the same detour. Clarifying the term might dissolve their dispute. If they instead predict different travel times under rain, there is a practical difference that inquiry can investigate.
