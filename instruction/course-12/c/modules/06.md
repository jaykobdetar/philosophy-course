# C12C-M06 — Faith and the ethical

Baseline allocation: **16 hours**. Prerequisites: Courses 1, 5 and 9; prior modules in this option.

## Guiding question and outcomes

What would it mean for faith to suspend the ethical, and whose claim is this?

By the end: Explain pseudonymous presentation; reconstruct the teleological suspension problem; distinguish understanding a paradox from endorsing harmful conduct.

## Teaching explanation

The pseudonymous speaker Johannes de silentio poses a problem about Abraham that cannot be reduced to the rule “religion excuses wrongdoing.” The ethical, understood as the universal in this argument, demands publicly intelligible justification. The extraordinary relation claimed for faith cannot simply be expressed as another instance of that universal. A teleological suspension would concern a higher end in relation to which the ethical is suspended; it is not an abolition of all ethics by personal preference.

The contrast with a tragic hero matters because sacrifice for a publicly intelligible ethical end can be explained within the universal, whereas the Abraham case threatens that framework. Reconstruct the dilemma before resolving it: if the ethical is the highest, what follows for the purported act of faith; if the act is faith, what unusual relation must be possible? The pseudonymous form cautions against directly equating every utterance with Kierkegaard’s settled doctrine. Nor does describing the possibility establish that any actual person has such a relation. A criticism can target the intelligibility of the exception, its epistemic credentials or its compatibility with ethical obligation. Those are different objections.

## Worked practice example

Invented example: a commander sacrifices a strategic resource to protect a city and can publicly explain the end. A private claimant who invokes an inaccessible command cannot obtain the same justification merely by calling both acts “sacrifice.” This contrast helps locate the problem; it does not validate the private claim.
