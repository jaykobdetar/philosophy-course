## Guiding question and outcomes

Does gaining experiential knowledge establish a nonphysical fact?

By the end: Separate epistemic and metaphysical premises; reconstruct the knowledge argument; articulate an ability reply and an objection to it.

## Teaching explanation

Nagel directs attention to subjective character: an experience is an experience for a subject. Imagining how a human would behave with wings and sonar does not thereby reveal a bat’s point of view. The philosophical issue is how an objective account could accommodate that character, rather than a simple claim that scientific ignorance proves dualism.

Jackson’s knowledge argument requires an exceptionally strong stipulation: the confined scientist possesses all physical information relevant to the experience. After first experience she appears to learn something. To infer that physicalism is false, one must also maintain that this learning is factual knowledge of something not included in her prior physical knowledge. A failure to imagine complete physical knowledge and a dispute about what new knowledge amounts to attack different premises. An ability reply grants a gain while denying that it must be new propositional information: recognition, imagination or memory could improve. Its vulnerability is whether the whole gain can be exhausted by such abilities. A person might acquire an ability and learn a fact simultaneously. The baseline overview presents this debate; it cannot stand in for reading Lewis himself.

## Worked practice example

Invented example: Iona knows the complete stipulated physical theory of a new odor but has never smelled it. After exposure she can recognize it. The observation supports a gain in recognition. It does not alone decide between “a new fact enabled recognition” and “recognition itself is the relevant new knowledge.” To make the anti-physicalist inference, identify the additional factual-knowledge premise.
