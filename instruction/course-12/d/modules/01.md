# C12D-M01 — Family and the scope of justice

Baseline allocation: **18 hours**. Prerequisites: Courses 6–8; preferably Course 11B; prior modules in this option.

## Guiding question and outcomes

Can a theory of public justice assume the family is already just?

By the end: Reconstruct a public/private-boundary critique; expose one background assumption; separate a normative inference from an empirical claim.

## Teaching explanation

A theory that treats the family as a private unit can overlook how its internal organization shapes people’s opportunities outside it. Division of paid and unpaid labor, dependence and expectations about care affect the resources and bargaining position with which people enter public institutions. Okin’s challenge concerns the scope and background assumptions of justice, not merely whether particular family members are kind.

To reconstruct the argument, identify the theory’s unit of analysis and what it assumes about the people within that unit. If citizens are modeled as independent workers while care is supplied without explanation, a central condition of their independence has disappeared from the model. But no particular policy follows from exposing the omission alone. An argument for childcare provision, leave rights or a revised distribution rule needs further premises about fairness, agency and feasibility. Treat empirical assumptions explicitly: who performs care, what alternatives are available and whether a proposal changes dependence are matters for evidence. An imagined case can isolate the normative question provided it is clearly marked and does not masquerade as a description of actual households.

## Worked practice example

Invented example: a town grants equal training vouchers to adults, but courses run only when unpaid caregivers must be at home. Formal equality of vouchers does not establish equal access. The omitted care institution may explain a gap, while the judgment that the gap is unjust still needs a normative premise.
