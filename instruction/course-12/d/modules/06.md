# C12D-M06 — Method and idealization

Baseline allocation: **12 hours**. Prerequisites: Courses 6–8; preferably Course 11B; prior modules in this option.

## Guiding question and outcomes

When does an abstraction conceal the conditions needed to explain injustice?

By the end: Distinguish useful simplification from distorting idealization; audit a case; revise an inference without claiming all abstraction is illegitimate.

## Teaching explanation

An idealization selects or simplifies features for a purpose. Mills’s criticism concerns forms of ideal theory that make existing domination hard to see and can thereby function ideologically. A model may assume fully voluntary transactions, equal standing or general compliance precisely where unequal institutions shape available choices. If those assumptions remove the source of the problem, a conclusion drawn within the model may fail to address the actual injustice.

The criticism does not require rejecting every hypothetical or every normative ideal. Ask what explanatory or evaluative purpose the abstraction serves, what it leaves out and whether putting that feature back changes the argument. A transparent simplification can isolate a principle; a concealed idealization can treat an exclusion as already solved. The audit therefore needs a before-and-after inference. Name the omitted institution or historical condition, explain the mechanism through which it matters and qualify the resulting conclusion. Adding a paragraph about “context” without changing any premise does not demonstrate repair.

## Worked practice example

Invented example: an employment model calls every contract free because workers can legally refuse. If housing and access to essential services depend on the employer, the legal possibility of refusal may not establish the independence the argument needs. Restoring that institution changes the freedom premise, not merely the descriptive background.
