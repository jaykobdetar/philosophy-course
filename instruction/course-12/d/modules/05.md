# C12D-M05 — Beyond human institutions

## Guiding question and outcomes

What happens when moral concern extends beyond human citizens?

By the end: Compare units of concern; distinguish equal consideration from identical treatment; preserve a substantive animal/environment conflict.

## Teaching explanation

Singer’s equality argument concerns equal consideration of interests, not identical treatment of every being. The capacity for suffering and enjoyment gives a reason to consider interests that species membership alone cannot erase. Differences in capacities can affect which interests a being has without making its comparable suffering count for nothing. A reconstruction should identify the criterion and the argument against an arbitrary restriction, then ask how conflicting interests are weighed.

Leopold’s land ethic broadens the moral community to include soils, waters, plants and animals as an interconnected community. Its ecological perspective can make the integrity of a whole relevant in a way not reducible to each individual’s immediate welfare. Do not force agreement by stipulating that every environmental benefit makes every animal better off. A restoration project can harm some sentient individuals while protecting a community. The dispute concerns units of concern, value and justification as well as empirical predictions. Actual interventions require evidence about consequences and alternatives. In these teaching cases the facts are stipulated for philosophical comparison, not offered as conservation advice.

## Worked practice example

Invented example: removing introduced herbivores would reduce severe damage to an island habitat but harms the individuals removed. A Singer-style analysis must count those harms and compare alternatives; a land-ethic analysis can assign independent importance to the community. Both need evidence, yet perfect evidence would not necessarily remove the normative disagreement.
