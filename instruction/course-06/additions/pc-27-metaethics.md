## Moore: analysis and objection

Moore considers whether goodness is a complex analyzable notion, meaningless, or simple and indefinable. In §13 he tests a proposed analysis in terms of what we desire to desire: a competent questioner can still intelligibly ask whether that is good. The argument distinguishes sameness of meaning from a possibly true connection between properties. Even if everything satisfying a proposed description is good, it does not follow that the description defines “good.”

A reconstruction is: if the proposed analysis fully captures the notion, the relevant goodness question should not remain substantively open for someone who grasps the terms; it does remain open; therefore the proposed analysis fails. To challenge this, question the diagnostic premise: an informative identity or a difficult analysis might be true even when a competent speaker hesitates. This targets the inference from intelligible doubt to distinctness, not merely a proposed verdict about pleasure. A further argument is needed to move from a conceptual distinction to a claim about which properties exist. Do not conflate this issue with Hume's unexplained is/ought transition.

## Mackie: interpret the assigned sequence

Mackie separates practical commitments from a second-order denial of objective values. He distinguishes an ontological thesis from simple accounts of moral language. His error theory combines ordinary judgments' claim to objective prescriptivity with rejection of that claim. The relativity argument compares explanations of persistent differences; disagreement alone is insufficient. The queerness argument raises metaphysical and epistemological difficulties about intrinsically prescriptive features and our access to them. A critic can dispute the portrayal of objectivity, the explanatory comparison, or the inference from unfamiliarity to nonexistence. Ayer's noncognitivist account and Mackie's error theory therefore fail to coincide. Neither analysis alone supplies a new positive rule permitting cruelty. These claims should be checked against the assigned sections, particularly §§1–3, 7–9 and 12.

## Worked example — invented scoring system

A school defines “morally good action” as any act that increases its published points score. A student asks whether increasing the score by excluding less successful classmates is good. Two issues now separate: whether the definition captures the moral concept and whether the school's rule is justified. A definition stipulated for administrative bookkeeping may work as bookkeeping without answering either moral question. Replacing the definition with “everyone dislikes exclusion” adds a fact about attitudes; it still leaves the status and practical force of the evaluation to be explained.
