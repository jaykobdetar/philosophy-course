# C06-M02 — Character and flourishing

**18 hours.** Guiding question: why should excellence of character matter to a good human life? Prerequisite: M01. Outcomes: reconstruct the function argument; distinguish activity from a passing feeling; explain habituation, the mean, and practical wisdom; test a virtue judgment without numerical averaging.

## Teaching explanation

Book I proceeds from activities with ends to the question whether some end organizes choice. Eudaimonia names living well or flourishing, not simply feeling pleased. An ultimate end should be chosen for itself and make a life choiceworthy. These criteria constrain the answer; they do not yet establish that every human pursuit converges on one good.

The function argument asks whether the characteristic human activity can specify human excellence. In a charitable reconstruction: living and sensing are shared with other organisms; rational activity is characteristic of human life; performing a function well involves the relevant excellence; therefore the human good is excellent activity of the rational soul over a complete life. The important inferential hinge is the move from characteristic activity to what makes a human life good. A critic can accept the descriptive contrast with plants and still dispute that normative bridge. Do not turn the argument into “whatever is unique is good” without explaining why excellence is attached to the function. The assigned chapters also resist treating luck and external conditions as irrelevant to flourishing.

Habituation in Book II means that repeated action shapes stable dispositions of feeling and choice. Acting as a generous person would act once is different from acting knowingly, for its own sake, from a settled state. This creates a learning problem: learners need guidance and repeated practice before their motives are fully formed. It does not require fully virtuous agency before any good action can occur.

The mean is relative to the person and situation, determined through reason; it is not half the quantity between two extremes. Courage concerns fear and confidence toward appropriate dangers, for appropriate reasons. A moderate dose of injustice is not virtuous. Some action descriptions already name wrongs, so the mean cannot turn every action type into a permissible one. Practical wisdom concerns good deliberation about human living and particulars; technical skill concerns production. Cleverness at achieving a goal would not establish that the goal is worth pursuing.

## Worked example — invented rescue

A trained lifeguard enters a marked safe channel to rescue a swimmer while an untrained bystander throws a flotation device and calls for help. Both actions could express courage; equal danger-taking is not required. “The lifeguard spends six minutes in the water, the bystander zero, so three minutes is the mean” is a category error. The relevant factors include ability, foreseeable danger, available aid, and why the agent acts. Recklessness might arise if either ignores these factors to impress spectators. This is a philosophical invented case, not rescue instruction.

Practice: rewrite that explanation for generosity. A worker has £40 left after necessities; another has £4,000. Both are asked to give £20. State why the quantity alone cannot establish the virtue, then identify one constraint on arbitrary self-exemption. Return to II.6 for textual support.
