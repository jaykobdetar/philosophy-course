# C06-M05 — Welfare and justification

**16 hours.** Guiding question: what would justify treating happiness as the ultimate moral end? Prerequisite: M01 and ability to distinguish a criterion from a decision procedure. Outcomes: explain Mill's qualitative distinction; reconstruct the proof without collapsing its stages; assess an objection to an inference; apply welfare reasoning with explicit assumptions.

## Reading

Chapter II supplies the account of utility and replies to objections; IV supplies the proposed proof. ## Explanation

The moral standard concerns happiness impartially, not simply the agent's satisfaction. Pleasure and absence of pain matter, but Mill does not rank pleasures only by intensity or duration. His competent-judge test gives a role to preference among people acquainted with both kinds of experience and able to appreciate them. It raises questions about access, competence, disagreement, and whether the stated preference really tracks pleasure rather than dignity or another good. Those questions engage the account more directly than declaring that “pleasure is subjective.”

Do not confuse standard, motive, and deliberation. A welfare standard says what makes an action right; it need not require agents to calculate from scratch before every ordinary act or to act only from an explicit desire for the general good. Established practices can embody experience about consequences, while a difficult conflict may require returning to the standard. Our assigned chapters do not settle every dispute between later act and rule utilitarianism; qualify claims about Mill accordingly.

Chapter IV explicitly limits the sort of proof available for ultimate ends. Reconstruct at least three stages: actual desire is offered as evidence of desirability; each person's happiness is a good to that person and general happiness is presented as a good to the aggregate; apparently distinct ends such as virtue can become parts of happiness. Success at one stage does not settle the others. An equivocation objection asks whether “desirable” changes from capable of being desired to worthy of desire. A charitable evidential reading treats desire as our available evidence about ends rather than as a dictionary definition. That reading must still explain why actual desire is good evidence and why impartial aggregation follows.

The composition worry is also separate: from each person's valuing their own happiness, why should anyone adopt the general happiness as an authoritative standard? The objection is not merely that people sometimes act selfishly. Mill can distinguish what people pursue from the standard by which their conduct is judged. The evaluator must identify where the proof supports that distinction.

## Worked example — invented arts fund

A town chooses between a short amusement event enjoyed by 400 people and an accessible music program enjoyed more deeply by 80 people familiar with both experiences. Stipulate equal cost; do not stipulate that either enjoyment is “higher.” A poor argument simply counts 400 against 80. A better argument asks whether the quality claim is supported by competent comparison and whether longer effects alter the estimate. A critic may challenge the evidence without rejecting welfare as the standard. Conversely, a claim about exclusion or fairness may challenge the standard itself. Keep those objections separate.

Practice: draw a three-stage map of the proof and put these objections on the relevant arrow: (a) someone desires approval that harms them; (b) my happiness being good for me does not yet make yours my end; (c) valuing honesty even at personal cost seems to introduce another ultimate good. State a plausible Millian reply to each, then name what remains to be established.

P5: **600 words**, answering “Does the move from desired to desirable provide evidence for utility as an ultimate end?” Allocate roughly 150 words to reconstruction, 150 to an objection, 150 to the best reply, and 150 to your qualified judgment. Add a short note distinguishing that issue from aggregation and exclusivity of happiness. Quote sparingly and cite the exact chapter IV passage you inspect.

## Independent U5 — 30 minutes

An author argues: “Every member of a choir wants their own solo to be praised. Therefore praise for every solo is the sole good of the choir, and each member must maximize total praise.” In 300 words, distinguish two unsupported transitions and explain how one resembles a difficulty in Mill IV. Then state one charitable repair and what it still fails to establish. Source-open, no live content assistance. Criteria: separate desirability, aggregation, and exclusivity; reconstruct the actual argument rather than ridicule it.

Repair an equivocation diagnosis by replacing each use of “desirable” with its proposed meaning and checking whether the inference survives.
