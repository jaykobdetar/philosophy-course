# C06-M06 — Moral language and applied argument

**16 hours.** Guiding question: what changes when we ask about moral judgment's status, and what remains to be argued in a concrete case? Prerequisite: M01–05. Outcomes: explain Hume's inferential challenge and Ayer's ethical language account without conflation; reconstruct a Thomson argument; distinguish a right from a claim to another's aid; control the scope of a conclusion.

## Explanation

Hume's section belongs to an inquiry into moral distinctions, reason and motivation. One reconstruction pairs morality's influence on action with the alleged inability of reason alone to supply that influence, concluding that reason alone does not generate moral distinctions. Ask what “alone” excludes and whether the motivation premise is defensible. The closing is/ought passage challenges an unacknowledged transition in arguments. It is not a license to ignore evidence about consequences, nor a proof that every moral sentence is meaningless.

Ayer asks what ethical expressions contribute under his account of meaningful assertion. His account of a distinctively evaluative utterance treats it as expressing or evoking attitudes, rather than stating an additional evaluative fact. This differs from a report that the speaker feels disapproval: a psychological report can be true or false. Ethical disagreement can include factual disagreement and attempts to influence attitudes even where the evaluative element is not a factual proposition. The chapter also addresses theology; a complete reading includes that portion, but the assessed evidence concerns its ethical argument. Do not attribute this later linguistic project to Hume merely because both oppose a simple rationalist account.

Thomson provisionally grants fetal personhood to test the inference from a right to life to a right to use another person's body. The argumentative concession is not her independent conclusion about the beginning of personhood. A counterexample to that inference need not establish that every abortion is permissible. Distinguish having a right to life, having a specific entitlement to aid, acting unjustly, and behaving indecently. Cases involving consent, responsibility for dependence, threat, burden, and alternatives test different premises. Their relevance must be argued rather than bundled into “bodily autonomy.”

An analogy works by preserving the feature relevant to a principle. It can defeat a universal principle with one counterinstance without perfectly matching every real case. But applying its result to another case requires a further bridge: the relevant similarities outweigh any morally relevant difference. Thomson's later qualifications are therefore part of the assignment, not an optional afterthought.

## Worked example — invented equipment claim

A traveler urgently needs another person's privately owned radio to summon help. “The traveler has a right to life; therefore the traveler owns the radio” is invalid without an entitlement premise. That does not settle whether lending it is required, whether emergency use is permissible, or whether refusing is callous. Ownership and duties of aid need distinct arguments. This radio analogy illuminates inferential form; property is not assumed equivalent to bodily integrity.

Practice Hume: reconstruct “Residents oppose the shelter; therefore it must close” and supply two different normative premises that could connect its clauses. Test each. Practice Ayer: distinguish “I disapprove of the closure” as a psychological report from an evaluative condemnation of it; explain how a dispute about its actual effects differs. Practice Thomson: map the violinist argument and then locate a later passage that limits the conclusion. Preserve the precise force of “some,” “all,” and “not shown.”

P6a: 400 words comparing Hume's project and Ayer's, with one premise-level objection to each. P6b: a 500-word reconstruction of one Thomson thought experiment, its target inference, one relevant disanalogy, and one conclusion it does **not** establish. Keep both artifacts; PC-27 supplements these rather than replacing them.

## Independent U6 — 30 minutes

In an invented case, a traveler is connected without permission to a machine sustaining a stranger. Disconnecting immediately would end the stranger's life; waiting twenty minutes would allow transfer to an independent machine without bodily harm, but would make the traveler miss a social dinner. Analyze why personhood alone does or does not settle an entitlement to use the traveler's body. Distinguish a claim about rights from a claim about decent conduct. Then explain why Ayer's metaethics alone supplies no particular waiting-time verdict. 300 words, source-open, no live help or examiner notes. Criteria assess inference, theory distinction, burden, alternatives, and limited conclusion.

Repair by reconstructing the opponent's argument before offering your own verdict. After seven days, spend 20 minutes of this module's retrieval time separating Hume's is/ought challenge from Ayer's semantic claim; use the remaining time to check citations and an article qualification.
