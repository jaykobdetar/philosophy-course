## Learn the distinction

A descriptive claim reports how things stand: a fund can provide twenty meals. A normative claim evaluates or directs: the fund ought to provide those meals. A metaethical question asks about the status, meaning, or justification of such evaluations: does “ought” express a truth-apt claim? These are levels of inquiry, not mutually exclusive subjects. A conversation about a fund can contain all three. Nor does calling a sentence normative settle whether it can be true. Moral realists can accept that reasoning from purely descriptive premises requires scrutiny.

Use OpenStax for initial vocabulary, then qualify its compressed formulations. Moore's concern about defining goodness and Hume's concern about an unexplained inferential transition are distinguishable; Module 6 and PC-27 return to the primary texts. A valuable thing need not generate a duty to maximize it, and plural goods need not imply that every verdict is equally justified. Do not infer anti-realism merely from the presence of evaluation.

Singer's rescue argument moves from a serious preventable harm and a principle about prevention at acceptable moral cost to an obligation. Distance alone is challenged as a reason to exempt us. To object fairly, identify which condition fails: ability to prevent, comparative moral cost, information, or scope of the principle. “Charity is optional” restates the disputed conclusion. The stronger and weaker principles impose different comparison standards; cite the version you actually test.

A useful argument skeleton is: (1) action A would prevent harm H; (2) its costs meet criterion C; (3) whenever prevention meets C, one ought to act; therefore (4) one ought to do A. Premises 1–2 may mix factual and evaluative elements. If uncertain about effectiveness, conditionalize the conclusion instead of inventing evidence.

## Worked practice — invented case

A club can use £100 for decorative banners or for ten verified meal vouchers. Stipulate that the vouchers reach people otherwise missing dinner, no prior promise requires banners, and no other use is available. A developing answer says, “Meals help people, so buy them.” A competent reconstruction adds: preventing this serious avoidable harm at the loss of decoration is required. That normative premise explains why effectiveness matters. A strong answer tests whether the “no other use” assumption hides a competing duty and limits its verdict to the stipulated case. None of these descriptions makes the tutor's preferred spending policy the criterion.

Now change the banners to a promised accessibility sign needed for a public event. Write both arguments in numbered premises; identify why the changed cost might matter. Give one objection to the principle and one objection only to a factual premise. Explain why they require different responses.

## Do and save

P1 is a 400-word analysis of this invented case: a student has six discretionary hours this week; two hours of training allow effective participation in a verified food-distribution shift; an optional club rehearsal takes the same evening; a promised visit to an isolated friend also conflicts. The student can do only one activity. Do not assume the visit is trivial or that the shift saves lives. Specify one defensible priority, two missing facts, an explicit normative premise, and a limitation. Add a six-line classification of your own claims and a 100-word response to a plausible rival priority.
