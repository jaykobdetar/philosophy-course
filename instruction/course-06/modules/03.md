# C06-M03 — Moral cultivation across contexts

**12 hours.** Guiding question: what does an incipient moral response show about human nature, and what does it leave to cultivation? Prerequisite: M02. Outcomes: interpret Mengzi's sprouts analogy; distinguish a capacity from an achieved disposition; compare a delimited feature with Aristotle; identify a translation-sensitive claim.

## Reading and access

Mengzi **2A6 and 6A1–10**, trans. Bryan W. Van Norden, *Mengzi: With Selections from Traditional Commentaries* (Hackett, 2008), including the translation of each numbered passage; consult its attached notes for uncertain terms, but unassigned commentary is not another required reading. A 2023 *Readings in Classical Chinese Philosophy* copy is acceptable only after its contents are checked for **every** assigned passage. No complete Van Norden text was available for author inspection here. This module is fully authored but precise wording/translation judgments require the learner's lawful copy or a legitimate short source excerpt. Record that dependency before marking P3 or F8.2.

This is a Warring States argument about human development and ethical-political order, including disputes with Gaozi. It is not an account of every Chinese tradition or a synonym for Aristotle. Compare the authors' arguments on one question while preserving their different textual settings. The terms often rendered heart, mind, or heart-mind do not map neatly onto a modern division between cold reasoning and emotion.

## Teaching explanation

In 2A6, the imagined response to a child approaching a well helps isolate an uncalculated concern from motives such as obtaining praise. The claim is not that nobody ever fails to help. A spontaneous response can be a beginning whose cultivation and extension remain necessary. A sprout has a direction of development but can wither; the analogy therefore combines a claim about an initial endowment with a claim about care and conditions. Test both: even if the reaction is widespread, does it establish moral goodness, universality, or reliable future conduct?

The 6A exchanges ask how to characterize human nature without equating whatever happens with what belongs to a creature's development. In 6A1 the willow/cups comparison raises whether moral formation violently reshapes what we are or develops capacities already present. In 6A2 water's direction of flow contests an inference from artificial redirection to indifferent nature. In 6A8 the damaged landscape image draws attention to repeated loss and adverse conditions; a degraded state need not reveal the original potential. Keep the explanatory and normative claims separate: that deprivation damages a capacity does not, by itself, settle the duties of every institution.

Two interpretations deserve comparison. An endowment reading emphasizes beginnings of specifically moral concern; a developmental reading emphasizes what capacities become under appropriate nourishment. They may overlap. The issue is whether the claim “nature is good” describes fully achieved goodness, a tendency, or an evaluatively structured capacity. Defend a reading using two assigned passages. Do not replace the text with the slogan that all people are always nice.

Aristotle and Mengzi both make formation central, but their starting questions and accounts of initial moral orientation differ. Aristotle II.1 distinguishes possessing the capacity to acquire virtue from acquiring the state through habituation. The sprouts account gives the initial responses a more determinate moral role. A comparison can illuminate this difference without deciding that either word “nature” has the same meaning. The proper unit of comparison is a claim and its reasoning, not two civilizations.

## Worked example and practice

Invented example: Le notices a classmate being excluded and immediately feels concern, but remains silent because intervention risks social disapproval. “Le is fully virtuous because a sprout appeared” confuses a beginning with developed conduct. “Le's silence proves there was never any concern” infers too much from one outcome. A better analysis distinguishes the response, its cultivation, competing pressures, and action. Whether the response was uncalculated is stipulated for this exercise, not an empirical conclusion about all children.

Now consider an adult who initially feels no concern for unfamiliar neighbors but gradually learns to notice their needs through repeated service. Write two 150-word interpretations, one emphasizing dormant/depleted capacity, the other questioning whether a distinctly moral beginning must be assumed. Locate the textual pressure on each in 6A1–2 or 6A8. A tutor may ask where the analogy helps, but may not invent Van Norden wording.

P3: write **600 words** comparing the relation between initial capacity and cultivated action in Mengzi 2A6/6A8 and Aristotle II.1/II.4. Include a shared question; one reconstruction per author; one similarity; one consequential difference; a rival interpretation of human nature; and a 60-word limit of comparison. Add an edition note distinguishing quoted translation, paraphrase, and your inference.

Time: reading 4h; context and concept chart 1h; worked practice 1h; P3 draft 2h; feedback 1h; revision 1.5h; U3 0.5h; delayed retrieval/source audit 1h = **12h**.
