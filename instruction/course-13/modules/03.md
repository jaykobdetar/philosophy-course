# C13-M03 — Research methods and the current debate (22 hours)

**Question:** Which new source should change this paper, and what has actually been read? Prerequisite: R1–R4 and one unresolved disagreement. Outcomes: screen four scholarly candidates; verify bibliographic identity and reading extent; select two full readings totaling about 40–60 pages, including a serious challenge; distinguish recent discovery from verified current consensus.

## Teaching

Authority, relevance and access are separate. A peer-reviewed paper may address a different question. An unpublished paper may contain a relevant objection but lacks the same editorial scrutiny. A publisher title page establishes identity; an abstract establishes a declared aim; neither alone warrants “the paper proves P.” Citation chaining lets you locate the argument behind a repeated claim and see how it has been challenged.

Use a claim–evidence ladder. At the bottom, “the index lists this title” is a bibliographic statement. Next, “the abstract says it argues P” reports an author’s announced project. After reading the relevant argument, you may reconstruct its premises and conclusion. Only after checking the pivotal cited passage may you claim that the secondary interpretation accurately represents that primary passage. Mark the highest level actually reached.

Current-debate checking is bounded inquiry, not a race for the newest date. Follow one backward citation from a core source and one forward citation or later related source. At least one selected reading should be relatively recent when you take the course. Define your search window explicitly; if none is relevant and accessible, document databases, queries, dates and exclusion reasons. A defensible older challenge is better than inventing a current conclusion. Never infer consensus from a small or language-limited search.

**Worked example, invented research records:** Candidate Alpha has a verified 2025 journal record and abstract claiming to compare two theories, but no full text. Candidate Beta is a readable 2018 article with a detailed objection to your pivotal premise. For the paper, Beta can supply a reconstructed objection after reading; Alpha can supply only a note about an announced research direction. Selecting Alpha solely for recency would trade relevance and inspectability for a date. Do not invent authors or citations for these teaching records.

## Practice and outputs

Run four queries: author + disputed concept; exact core title + objection; alternate terminology + central premise; core title + later date window. Spend no more than 90 minutes searching before deciding whether access is the bottleneck. Screen four candidates; read each abstract and introduction when available. D has one row per candidate: citation; stable URL; discovery query/date; publication version; exact pages actually read; argument relevance; access/rights status; accept/reject reason; contribution; uncertainty. Select two whole articles or explicitly delimited complete arguments whose combined target is 40–60 pages; one must seriously challenge your thesis. If whole-source length differs, explain and log time; do not silently truncate a crucial reply.

After full reading, produce two 350-word source notes with premise, challenge, primary locator check and intended paper use. A source that supplies no inferential or interpretive work should leave the active bibliography.

**Independent changed task M3-I:** A verified index lists a 2026 paper. You have read its abstract, which promises a critique of “standard inference,” and a blog claims that it decisively refutes your thesis. In 200 words specify exactly what you may report now, what you must inspect before relying on the critique and how you would keep the search finite. No live help; source protocol visible. Criteria: calibrated attribution, source priority, feasible next action.
