# C13-M06 — Critical response and substantive revision (24 hours)

**Question:** Which criticism should change the paper, and why? Prerequisite: E1 with its source/assistance record. Outcomes: distinguish interpretive error from defensible disagreement; answer the strongest objection in 500 words; revise the argument substantively; justify accepted and rejected advice.

Reopen E1’s exact cited passages and the Module 3 challenge source, as listed in D. Review the route’s M6 objection. Read [active tutor rules](../../tutor.md) before requesting feedback. A reviewer must be able to inspect the pivotal source; otherwise label its textual judgment provisional. No reader is contacted automatically. If a substantial human response is feasible, obtain it through the learner’s own arrangements; otherwise use a source-bound AI critical read and disclose its limits.

## Teaching

Classify each criticism before acting: factual/source error; inferential gap; interpretive alternative; scope objection; expression problem. Repair in that order when the problem undermines the thesis. A source error cannot be outweighed by praise elsewhere. A rival reading needs comparison with the same textual evidence. A reviewer’s dislike of a conclusion is not yet a reason to abandon it.

A response can deny a premise with reasons, distinguish cases, concede and narrow, or abandon the claim. Merely restating the thesis is not an answer. A concession should identify its cost: if the original thesis was universal and now applies only under specified conditions, acknowledge the loss of generality. Revision is successful when it improves the argument even if the conclusion becomes less ambitious.

Use a change ledger: criticism; evidence; decision; changed paragraph; effect; remaining uncertainty. Rejecting advice is legitimate when it misreads the task or assumes an unsupported claim, but explain why. Do not average incompatible reviews; inspect the disputed premise or passage. Agreement among models does not verify a text.

**Worked example, invented:** A reviewer says a study of silence wrongly infers consent. The draft replies, “But we asked everyone.” This misses the objection because opportunity to speak does not establish absence of pressure. A substantive revision distinguishes invitations, usable opportunities, uptake and sanctions. It then conditions the inference to consent on evidence that refusal was safe and intelligible. The revision need not assert that every silent person dissented. It preserves uncertainty rather than reversing an unsupported universal claim.

## Practice and outputs

Prepare a review packet: P’s question; E1; exact primary passages or lawful short excerpts in private storage; D; help/exposure declaration; two questions (“Which inference is weakest?” and “What source observation challenges my reading?”). Seek one substantial review. If AI is used, ask for diagnosis and objections, not replacement paragraphs. Save the review and mark any unverified claim.

Write O, **500 words**: objection (150), reason it matters (75), reply or concession (200), remaining cost (75). Revise E1 into E2, still 2,500–3,500 words. Write a 400-word change memo citing at least two substantive changes and one advice decision. Compare a before/after paragraph; the changed reasoning, not new vocabulary, must be identifiable. See [practice exemplars](../exemplars.md) before revision, never as text to import.

**Independent changed task M6-I:** A reviewer objects that the paper’s key translation gloss comes from a commentator, not the primary text. The author has checked that the gloss is printed in the book, but has not checked who wrote it. In 250 words decide what must be verified, propose two possible revision paths depending on the result, and state what would remain of the original argument. No live help. Criteria: attribution, conditional response and consequential revision.
