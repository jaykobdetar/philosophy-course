## Teaching explanation

The course's flying-person problem asks us to distinguish awareness of oneself from awareness of bodily parts. A conceptual reconstruction for examination is: imagine a person newly created with capacities intact but without sensory contact with external things or bodily surfaces; ask whether that person would affirm their own existence while not affirming the body's dimensions or parts. This is a **provisional reconstruction of the course topic**, not verified wording or confirmed scope of the Khalidi excerpt. Match it against the passage before attributing any premise to Avicenna.

Three conclusions must be separated. First, a person might have self-awareness without attending to a bodily representation. Second, that awareness might have a distinctive object or manner of presentation. Third, the soul might be a substance capable of existing apart from the body. The third is stronger than the first two. To move from a difference in awareness to a metaphysical distinction, an argument needs a bridge: perhaps that the awareness discloses the self's nature, rather than merely revealing how the self is known. Which bridge the text supplies is an interpretive question for the source, not a premise to insert unnoticed.

Do not turn the scenario into an experiment on an actual sensory-deprived person. A philosophical stipulation about what would remain known does not become an empirical finding merely because it resembles a possible laboratory setup. Conversely, objecting that an imagined scenario is unusual does not identify the inference that fails. Ask whether the assumptions are coherent, whether the proposed awareness would remain, and what would follow if it did.

The comparison with Aristotle should specify a claim studied in Course 3 and its locator, such as a definition of soul in relation to a living body, rather than “Aristotle was materialist.” The comparison with Descartes is a limit statement at this stage: do not assume that methodological doubt, the cogito, and a separate-substance argument are all already present in this different project. A resemblance in conclusion neither proves influence nor makes the arguments identical. Broader contextual orientation can be checked in [Gutas's account of Ibn Sina, §4](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ibn-sina/#MetaRatiSoulPracPhil); it does not verify Khalidi's pages.

## Worked example and practice

**Invented example:** someone knows that “the morning visitor” arrived, but does not know that “the author of the unsigned letter” arrived. The visitor and the author could be one person. A difference between known descriptions is therefore not sufficient by itself for distinct objects. This identifies a possible inferential risk; it does not settle whether direct self-awareness in the assigned passage is knowledge by descriptions.
