# C01-M02-PRACTICAL — Reasons, relevance and near-cases

**Status:** study-ready within Course 1 Module 2; learner pilot pending. **Budget:** two focused hours already allocated within the module's eight hours, after opening Sessions 2–3. This is not two extra hours on top of the 120-hour course.

**Guiding question:** When does a reason bear on a conclusion, and when is a fallacy label a poor substitute for explaining a defect?

**Exact source:** [OpenStax 5.5, Informal Fallacies](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-philosophy/pages/5-5-informal-fallacies), complete online section, checked 2026-10-13. Its categories orient the task; the cases below are constructed for this course. No copy of the section is redistributed here.

**Route:** Read and annotate for about 30 minutes. Use about 60 minutes for the four cases below. Spend about 30 minutes comparing feedback, revising one answer and recording a fresh near-case. Save input, change, resulting argument map or outline, and two sentences on what the change shows and does not show. A paper version is equivalent.

1. “The speaker supports a school-library levy, but the speaker once returned a book late; therefore the levy is bad.” Identify the stated conclusion, reason, a charitable missing argument that could make conduct relevant, and why the given reason alone fails.
2. “One poorly indexed book was borrowed from this library, so all its books are poorly indexed.” State what the example might show and what broader inference remains unsupported. What further evidence would help?
3. “The proposed bus timetable reduces late arrivals in a small trial, but the trial did not include winter; therefore it will fail in winter.” Explain why a limitation is important without treating an unanswered question as proof of failure.
4. “Every source cited in this essay has a date and author. This source lacks a date. Therefore I must verify its provenance before relying on it.” Explain why the conclusion can be a reasonable procedural step even if one can attach a superficial fallacy label to a similar-looking pattern.

**Expected output:** Four short analyses with one reconstructed argument each, a charitable near-case, and a revised answer to one case. These support the module's existing argument and condition outputs; do not count them as extra gate items.

**Feedback:** A strong response names the inferential gap, relevance or sampling problem; identifies the strongest legitimate concern in a weak argument; and distinguishes “not yet established” from “false.” In case 4, the caution is a low-cost source-checking decision, not a proof that the source is false. More than one reasonable judgment may survive when premises are clarified. Record which clarification changes the verdict. See [feedback anchors](examiner-notes.md) for the opening module's general criteria.

**Independent changed case:** Without tutor help, assess “A draft policy has one ambiguous clause, so the entire policy must be rejected.” State a charitable interpretation, a concrete defect, and one nonfallacious revision. Save the untouched response and assistance declaration. If this case was already rehearsed, create a different policy or text case with the same outcome.
