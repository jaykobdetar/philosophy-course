# C01-M02-PRACTICAL — Reasons, relevance and near-cases

**Status:** study-ready within Course 1 Module 2; learner pilot pending. **Budget:** two focused hours already allocated within the module's eight hours, after opening Sessions 2–3. This is not two extra hours on top of the 120-hour course.

**Guiding question:** When does a reason bear on a conclusion, and when is a fallacy label a poor substitute for explaining a defect?

**Exact source:** [OpenStax 5.5, Informal Fallacies](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-philosophy/pages/5-5-informal-fallacies), complete online section, checked 2026-10-13. Its categories orient the task; the cases below are constructed for this course. No copy of the section is redistributed here.

**Route:** Read and annotate for about 30 minutes. Use about 45 minutes for the four cases below and 15 minutes for the independent changed case before feedback. Spend about 30 minutes comparing feedback, revising one answer and recording a fresh near-case. Save input, change, resulting argument map or outline, and two sentences on what the change shows and does not show. A paper version is equivalent.

1. “The speaker supports a school-library levy, but the speaker once returned a book late; therefore the levy is bad.” Identify the stated conclusion, reason, a charitable missing argument that could make conduct relevant, and why the given reason alone fails.
2. “One poorly indexed book was borrowed from this library, so all its books are poorly indexed.” State what the example might show and what broader inference remains unsupported. What further evidence would help?
3. “The proposed bus timetable reduces late arrivals in a small trial, but the trial did not include winter; therefore it will fail in winter.” Explain why a limitation is important without treating an unanswered question as proof of failure.
4. “Every source cited in this essay has a date and author. This source lacks a date. Therefore I must verify its provenance before relying on it.” Explain why the conclusion can be a reasonable procedural step even if one can attach a superficial fallacy label to a similar-looking pattern.

**Expected output:** Four short analyses with one reconstructed argument each, a charitable near-case, and a revised answer to one case. These support the module's existing argument and condition outputs; do not count them as extra gate items.

**Feedback:** A strong response names the inferential gap, relevance or sampling problem; identifies the strongest legitimate concern in a weak argument; and distinguishes “not yet established” from “false.” In case 4, the caution is a low-cost source-checking decision, not a proof that the source is false. More than one reasonable judgment may survive when premises are clarified. Record which clarification changes the verdict. See [feedback anchors](examiner-notes.md) for the opening module's general criteria.

**Independent changed case:** Without tutor help, assess “A draft policy has one ambiguous clause, so the entire policy must be rejected.” State a charitable interpretation, a concrete defect, and one nonfallacious revision. Save the untouched response and assistance declaration. If this case was already rehearsed, use the alternate invented case: “A supplier delivered one order late; therefore every future order from this supplier will be late.” Explain the evidential limitation, a charitable concern, and a proportionate conclusion. If both are exposed, an assessor must document another comparable prompt before use.

**Next:** Finish Module 2's remaining reconstructions, condition tests, and 200-word validity/soundness explanation. Do not let fallacy names replace those skills.


**Prerequisites and outcomes:** Complete Sessions 2–3. Reconstruct the relevant premise-to-conclusion step, distinguish a legitimate concern from an unsupported verdict, and create a nonfallacious near-case. This contributes to C01-O2/O3 and PC-31; no extra course hours are added.

**Teaching explanation and worked example, invented:** “This restaurant has one dirty table, therefore its kitchen is unsafe” moves from a visible local defect to a wider conclusion. The observation can justify asking about cleaning procedures without by itself proving the kitchen unsafe. A proportionate near-case says that several documented hygiene failures support an investigation. The needed amount and kind of evidence depend on the conclusion and the cost of a mistaken decision. Relevant caution and categorical proof are different inferential aims. In case 4, make a procedural policy premise explicit rather than pretending that descriptive facts alone deductively establish an obligation.

**Feedback and delayed retrieval:** Use the PC-31 section of [examiner notes](examiner-notes.md#pc-31-practical-reasoning) only after saving the independent answer. If a label replaces an explanation, rewrite the argument with its missing premise and test whether that premise is justified. Within Module 3's opening retrieval time, recall one difference between “not established” and “false” on a different subject. The last 30 minutes of this packet cover feedback, revision, and recording that retrieval plan.

Text-to-speech of the same OpenStax section and dictated answers are acceptable; verify any source quotation in the displayed text.
