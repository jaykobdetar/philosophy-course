# C01-M06 — Meno and inquiry

**Planned allocation: 10 focused hours (600 minutes), within the 120-hour baseline.**

**Guiding question:** How can inquiry begin when we do not yet know its answer?

**Prerequisites and outcomes:** Modules 1–5. Reconstruct the inquiry paradox; distinguish what a demonstration displays from what is inferred about its origin; compare true opinion and knowledge (O1–O4).

**Assigned reading:** Plato, *Meno*, Lee Perlman translation at [MIT OCW](https://ocw.mit.edu/courses/es-113-ancient-greek-philosophy-and-mathematics-spring-2016/dacf28712c74d94ae88a474376a61cf0_MITES_113S16_Meno.pdf): 70a–86c (PDF pages 1–34, stop before 86d), then 97a–100c (pages 53–60). Read main dialogue only; extensive translator footnotes are optional. Use visible Stephanus references. The omitted intervening discussion is not assigned, and a bridge summary is not evidence that you read it.

**Teaching explanation:** At 80d–e, inquiry seems trapped: if you already know, inquiry is unnecessary; if you do not, you cannot identify the target or recognize success. The pressure comes from treating knowing and not knowing as exhaustive states with no useful intermediate grasp. Distinguish knowing an answer from understanding a question, having a candidate, or possessing criteria for testing it. Challenging the dichotomy is a response to the paradox, not a full theory of knowledge.

In the geometry exchange an enslaved boy first answers confidently, becomes puzzled, then responds to guided questions. Socrates uses this to support recollection. The improvement under questioning is observable in the dialogue; the claim about an earlier origin of knowledge requires further inference. Questions can convey structure even without stating the final answer. The social asymmetry and scripted character of the exchange limit what it can establish about independent learning.

At 97a–100c, correct opinion can guide action successfully; knowledge is distinguished through an explanatory tie that gives stability. Do not assume this is already the modern justified-true-belief formula in Gettier's exact sense. Similar vocabulary can mask different questions about guidance, explanation, and retention.

**Worked example, invented:** A learner knows what a square is but guesses that doubling its side doubles its area. A two-unit side gives area four; a four-unit side gives sixteen. They can recognize the error without having known the correct side for area eight. A square built on the original diagonal has area eight. This supports the possibility of progress from partial understanding. It does not alone establish a prenatal source for the insight. A verbal description and the arithmetic are sufficient; drawing is optional.

**Practice and outputs:** Number the paradox's steps and name the step your response challenges. Describe the boy's initial answer, the production of puzzlement, and the later achievement, with locators. Make a two-column “episode shows / Socrates additionally infers” table. Write 400 words comparing knowledge and true opinion using the route-to-Larissa and explanatory-tie discussion. Add one objection concerning the role of questioning, and answer it without dismissing the episode as worthless.

**Independent changed case:** Invented mechanic knows what a functioning lock does but does not know why this lock sticks. She tests whether friction, a bent key, or a misaligned plate explains it; a test could rule out each candidate. In 200–250 words explain how this case bears on the paradox, and why finding a correct adjustment once might differ from understanding the mechanism. Criteria: distinguish partial grasp from answer knowledge, justify recognition of progress, and avoid inferring a complete epistemology from one success.

**Repair and retrieval:** If your reconstruction conflates no answer with no relevant understanding, separate these in a new two-column note. Retrieve the distinction between uncertainty about death and ethical commitment during revision. **Next:** Module 7 examines a more radical starting doubt.
