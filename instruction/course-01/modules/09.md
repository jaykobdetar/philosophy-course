# C01-M09 — Singer and obligation

**Planned allocation: 12 focused hours (720 minutes), within the 120-hour baseline.**

**Guiding question:** How far does the duty to prevent serious harm extend?

**Prerequisites and outcomes:** Modules 1–8. Reconstruct the strong and moderate principles, separate normative and empirical premises, and defend an objection and reply concerning demandingness, distance, or collective responsibility (O1–O5).

**Assigned reading:** Peter Singer, “Famine, Affluence, and Morality,” *Philosophy & Public Affairs* 1 (1972), pp. 229–243, whole article, [local scan](../../../collection/philosophy-study-collection/texts/introduction_logic/required/singer_famine_affluence_morality_1972.pdf). PDF page 1 is a cover; article pp. 229–243 occupy PDF pp. 2–16. The scan contains the original pagination and no later postscript. Its JSTOR notice is preserved; it is an existing private study copy, not a new rights clearance. [The stable journal link](https://www.jstor.org/stable/2265052) did not expose article content in this authoring check. Obtain lawful library access if the local copy is unavailable to you.

**Teaching explanation:** The argument joins an evaluative premise about serious suffering, a general preventive principle, and claims about what agents can prevent at a given cost. The strong principle concerns sacrificing something of comparable moral importance; the moderate formulation concerns preventing something very bad without sacrificing anything morally significant. Keep the exact difference visible when interpreting pp. 231 and 241. Both formulations can support substantial obligations, but they need not set the same stopping point.

The pond case elicits a judgment about prevention at low cost. Extending it to distant aid requires attention to capability, causal effectiveness, and competing moral costs. Distance by itself differs from the information and effectiveness that may vary with distance. Likewise, the presence of other potential helpers differs from their actually providing sufficient help. An objection about the long-run effectiveness of a method targets an empirical bridge; an objection that a duty is excessive can challenge a moral principle or its application.

The paper uses a particular historical emergency. Its historical statistics and empirical predictions are not current aid data. This course asks you to evaluate a stated argument, not to make a personal financial decision or repeat 1972 empirical claims as contemporary facts. Demandingness is more than personal dislike: explain what morally important commitment a demand would compromise and why it limits the principle.

**Worked example, invented:** A person can redirect an unused water supply to prevent a neighbour's crop failure at the cost of a decorative fountain. A second version diverts drinking water required by their dependent. Holding preventability constant while changing the sacrifice isolates the cost condition. It does not show that every actual aid programme works or that a dependent always overrides every stranger; those claims need argument.

**Practice and outputs:** Make two numbered reconstructions, one for each principle, with moral premises distinguished from effectiveness assumptions. Explain in 150 words what the pond case supports and what extra work the general argument requires. Write 600 words on one of demandingness, distance, or collective responsibility. Give the strongest reply available in the article and one reason for accepting or resisting it. In a ten-minute discussion or typed exchange, change one feature of a rescue case and identify the exact premise affected. Cite at least two original page numbers.

**Independent changed case:** Invented scheme: ten neighbours can each contribute one hour; ten hours will certainly repair a barrier before a dangerous flood. Nine have irrevocably committed, but the final hour is missing. You could provide it by missing a modest recreational event. Compare a second version in which the tenth hour is already guaranteed by someone else. In 250 words analyze the duty under both principles and identify what depends on actual contributions rather than the number of potential helpers. Criteria: preserve facts, distinguish principle from application, and identify a relevant objection without inventing uncertainty about the stipulated repair.

**Repair and retrieval:** If the two principles collapse into “give everything,” rewrite their stopping conditions separately. If an objection concerns effectiveness, say what evidence would adjudicate it. Retrieve one Gettier case's truth/evidence distinction as a five-minute contrast within revision. **Next:** Choose the bounded essay question in Module 10.
