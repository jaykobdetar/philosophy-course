# C01-M05 — Apology and the examined life

**Planned allocation: 8 focused hours (480 minutes), within the 120-hour baseline.**

**Guiding question:** What does Socrates claim to know, and why does he defend examination even at personal cost?

**Prerequisites and outcomes:** Module 4. Explain Socratic wisdom, distinguish historical report from dramatic presentation and argument, and assess an inference about death (O1, O2, O4, O5).

**Assigned reading:** Plato, *Apology*, complete 17a–42a in [Harvard CHS's marked adaptation of Jowett](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/primary-source/plato-the-apology-of-socrates-sb/), credited to Miriam Carlisle, Thomas E. Jenkins, Gregory Nagy, and Soo-Young Kim. Reread 20c–23c, 28b–30c, and 40c–41c. Cite the displayed adaptation, including its markers, rather than the unmarked local Jowett file.

**Teaching explanation:** The oracle story motivates an investigation of claimed wisdom. Socrates' advantage is not possession of every answer: the comparison concerns acknowledging a lack of knowledge where others claim it. Avoid the self-refuting slogan that he knows absolutely nothing. His defense also distinguishes risk of death from wrongdoing: uncertainty about death does not erase his claims about the importance of just conduct and care of the soul.

Plato's presentation is evidence for how this text depicts Socrates. It is not, without further corroboration, a verbatim transcript of the trial. Separate three questions: what happened historically; how the author arranges the speaker's defense; and whether the reasons work. At 40c–41c a hopeful consideration of death treats it as absence of consciousness or relocation of the soul. A reconstruction must ask whether the alternatives are exhaustive and why either would be good. Absence of unpleasant experience and absence of harm are different claims; losing possible goods may matter even without an experience of loss.

**Worked example, invented:** A researcher says, “I cannot yet explain this result; another researcher claims certainty without evidence.” This can support a comparative claim about intellectual caution without proving the cautious researcher correct about everything. Similarly, “I will not fake data to keep my job” ranks wrong action against personal loss; it does not show that unemployment is harmless.

**Practice and outputs:** Make a three-column note labelled historical claim, dramatic presentation, philosophical inference. Put one passage-based entry in each and state what further evidence each would need. Write 500 words explaining the relation between the oracle, inquiry, and wisdom; include two locators and one apparent tension. Reconstruct one death argument in numbered premises and give a 150-word objection to a specific step. Discuss whether acknowledged ignorance can coexist with an uncompromising ethical commitment.

**Independent changed case:** Invented adviser: “No one can prove that losing this competition is bad, so fearing defeat is irrational; therefore we should ignore every cost of entering.” In 200 words compare the inferential structure with one assigned *Apology* passage. State one similarity, one crucial difference, and what evidence could support the adviser. Criteria: do not infer goodness from lack of proof of badness; use the text to delimit the comparison rather than attaching Socrates' name to your position.

**Repair and retrieval:** If the explanation makes wisdom unlimited ignorance, mark exactly what is and is not claimed at 21b–23b. If the death objection attacks a missing premise, supply the charitable reconstruction first. Retrieve the unanimous-approval/explanation distinction for five minutes before feedback. **Next:** Module 6 asks how inquiry can start without knowledge.
