# C01-S03 — Validity, soundness, and counterexamples

**Guiding question:** Can an argument's form be good while a premise is false, or its conclusion true while its form fails?

**Exact source:** [OpenStax 5.2, Logical Statements](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-philosophy/pages/5-2-logical-statements), complete section, plus the validity and soundness examples in [5.3, Arguments](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-philosophy/pages/5-3-arguments). Online sections checked on 2026-10-13. Do not treat a fallacy name as an explanation.

**90-minute route:** Read for 20 minutes. Spend 20 minutes on the four cases below. Invent and explain two new examples in 20 minutes. Spend 20 minutes on a charitable argument for “all good explanations are simple” and a counterexample to that universal claim; finish with 10 minutes checking whether the counterexample really targets the claim.

1. Construct a **valid** argument with a false premise. Say why the false premise does not make the form invalid.
2. Construct an **invalid** argument with a true conclusion. Give a case where its premises hold and its conclusion fails while preserving the argument's form.
3. Explain why being a square is sufficient but not necessary for being a rectangle.
4. In a stipulated race where only paid entrants may win, explain why paying is necessary but not sufficient for winning.

**Output:** Four explained cases, two original cases, and the charitable argument/counterexample pair. Save as `C01-S03-conditions.md`. Two supplied cases plus two original ones may count toward Module 2's four condition tests if each has a reasoned explanation.

**Feedback:** Test a universal claim with a genuine counterexample; a merely disliked claim or odd example is not enough. Distinguish “if P then Q” from its converse. The tutor should ask you to repair a case before offering a different worked example. [Examiner notes](../examiner-notes.md#c01-s03) give checks.

**Independent check:** A library rule says every borrower must have a card, but having a card does not guarantee a loan. For a person who has a card, identify one conclusion you may and one you may not draw. Explain the direction of the condition without copying a practiced square example.
