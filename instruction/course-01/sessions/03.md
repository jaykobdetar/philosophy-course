# C01-S03 — Validity, soundness, and counterexamples

**Guiding question:** Can an argument's form be good while a premise is false, or its conclusion true while its form fails?

**Exact source:** [OpenStax 5.2, Logical Statements](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-philosophy/pages/5-2-logical-statements), complete section, plus [OpenStax 5.4, Types of Inferences](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-philosophy/pages/5-4-types-of-inferences), from **Deductive Reasoning** through **Testing Deductive Inferences**; stop before **Inductive Inferences**. The 5.4 section defines validity, shows invalid conditional forms and tests them with counterexamples. OpenStax does not define *soundness* in this bounded passage; use the short explanation below, checked against the locally preserved *forall x: Calgary* (Fall 2025), chapter 2 §2.4. Online sections and the local textbook were checked on 2026-10-13.

**Soundness in two steps:** First ask whether the conclusion must follow *if* the premises are true (validity). Then ask whether the premises are in fact true. A deductive argument is **sound** only when both conditions hold. A false premise can leave the form valid but make the argument unsound. A true conclusion alone does not make an invalid inference sound. These checks are separate from whether you like the conclusion.

**90-minute route:** Read for 20 minutes. Spend 20 minutes on the four cases below. Invent and explain two new examples in 20 minutes. Spend 15 minutes on a charitable argument for “all good explanations are simple” and a counterexample; spend 10 minutes on the independent library-card check, then 5 minutes checking one issue against feedback and saving the record. Extend and log time if the source or feedback needs more attention.

1. Construct a **valid** argument with a false premise. Say why the false premise does not make the form invalid.
2. Construct an **invalid** argument with a true conclusion. Give a case where its premises hold and its conclusion fails while preserving the argument's form.
3. Explain why being a square is sufficient but not necessary for being a rectangle.
4. In a stipulated race where only paid entrants may win, explain why paying is necessary but not sufficient for winning.
