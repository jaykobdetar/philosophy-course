# C11B-M02 — Du Bois: selfhood, education and political agency

**20 hours. Guiding question:** How can a description of divided self-understanding support an argument about institutions? Prerequisites: B1 method and Course 8 politics.

## Teaching

The opening question of chapter I treats the speaker as a problem before he has defined himself. The ensuing account of double consciousness concerns self-relation within a social world that evaluates him through racialized contempt. It should not be reduced to having two hobbies, speaking two languages, or possessing two disconnected personalities. The divided aims occur under conditions in which recognition and opportunity are unequally structured.

A good interpretation also asks what the desired resolution preserves. In the passage following the “seventh son” paragraph, Du Bois describes a better self without simply losing either inherited identification. That makes pure assimilation and permanent division inadequate as automatic readings. The text's aspiration combines participation with the preservation of contributions devalued by the surrounding world. Be careful: explaining this aspiration is different from accepting every historical or racial category used to express it.

Chapter III changes the form of argument. Du Bois acknowledges Washington's achievements while criticizing a program as he understands it. He questions whether economic progress can be secured when political rights, civic equality and higher education are sacrificed. The three paradoxes are dependency arguments: the proposed end appears to require resources or institutions that the program undercuts. Reconstruct each dependency explicitly. A temporal sequence—one event happened after another—is not yet a causal explanation; Du Bois himself distinguishes direct causation from contributing influence in the chapter.

The relation between chapters need not be that one “proves” the other. A description of constrained self-development can explain why education and political agency matter; the institutional argument still needs its own premises. Keep Du Bois's representation of Washington separate from a verified reconstruction from Washington's own writings, which are not assigned here. You may evaluate the internal force of Du Bois's criticism while reserving a verdict about its complete historical fairness.

## Worked practice — original dependency argument

An invented training program claims it can produce skilled tutors while eliminating every institution that trains tutors. If the program depends on graduates of those institutions, its means undercut its end. This is not a proof that no alternative training system could exist. A serious objection proposes an actual substitute mechanism; a reply asks whether the program supplies it. The logical structure helps read the educational paradox in chapter III without copying its content.

Produce B2, **700 words**, connecting one precisely located chapter-I paragraph with one chapter-III argument. Include a sentence identifying where description becomes an explanatory or normative inference, one serious objection, and a limitation of using Du Bois alone to represent Washington. Attach a two-column “claim / exact source” table with at least four entries. Revise any passage that says merely “Du Bois wanted equality” into the specific kind of equality or agency at issue.

## Independent changed-case B2-I

An invented scholarship fund offers job training on condition that recipients waive participation in its governance. The directors argue that earning power will eventually make political participation unnecessary. In 350–450 words, use one chapter-III dependency argument to assess that inference, then explain why the case does not simply reproduce Du Bois's historical situation. Identify one relevant chapter-I claim about opportunity or self-development. Thirty-five minutes, primary text and citation sheet only; no feedback or prewritten answer. Criteria: preserve the argument's conditional structure, avoid a blanket anti-training reading, and distinguish analogy from historical identity.
