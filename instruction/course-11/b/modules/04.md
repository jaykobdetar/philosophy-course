# C11B-M04 — Menkiti and Gyekye: person and community

**20 hours. Guiding question:** Does achieving moral personhood determine who is entitled to moral concern? Prerequisites: B1 conceptual method and B2–3 distinctions among selfhood, recognition and institutions.

## Outcomes and assigned reading

Reconstruct both positions charitably; distinguish descriptive humanity, moral achievement, social recognition and normative standing; test a single case under competing readings; identify a genuine disagreement without relying on individualist/collectivist labels.

Read Ifeanyi Menkiti, **“Person and Community in African Traditional Thought,” whole essay beginning p. 171**, in Richard A. Wright, ed., *African Philosophy: An Introduction*, **third edition, University Press of America 1984**. Read Kwame Gyekye, **“Person and Community in African Thought,” pp. 101–122**, in *Person and Community: Ghanaian Philosophical Studies I*, 1992. Both exact assignments remain access-dependent and were not inspected in this authoring run. The later SEP essay by Gyekye is not a substitute for the 1992 chapter. Do not import pagination from another anthology edition.

## Teaching

The word “person” can carry several burdens. It can identify a human being, acknowledge a socially constituted identity, praise mature moral conduct, or mark the standing in virtue of which someone has claims on others. These uses may connect, but they are not equivalent. A society's refusal to call a cruel adult a “real person” might be an ethical criticism without denying that the adult remains owed humane treatment. A philosophical argument must specify which sense carries each premise and conclusion.

Menkiti's account gives the community an important role in the achievement of personhood. Read for the stronger and weaker possible claims: community supplies the practices through which moral maturity develops; community recognition constitutes a status; or community wholly determines an individual's value. Do not automatically attribute the strongest version. Reconstruct his actual reasons and the cases he uses. Then ask what follows for children, outsiders and those unable to meet ordinary expectations.

Gyekye's discussion challenges an account that gives community an exhaustive or overriding role while retaining the importance of communal life. That makes “individualism defeats collectivism” an inadequate summary. Identify where he makes room for an individual's capacities, choice or claims and how those coexist with obligations. Compare the arguments at the same level: a thesis about formation does not directly contradict a thesis about rights unless an additional premise connects them.

The hardest cases reveal ambiguities. An adult can be socially useful without being morally admirable, or admirable while dissenting from prevailing expectations. A child may lack mature moral achievement while being owed care. A newcomer may contribute extensively before formal recognition. For each case ask four separate questions: Is this a human being? What ethical achievement is present? What social status is recognized? What treatment is owed? A proposed theory may answer these differently without contradiction, but it must explain the relationship.

## Worked practice — original case

An invented town praises a generous resident as “a true member” and condemns a wealthy exploiter as “no member at all.” The exploiter nonetheless retains protection from assault. Inferring that the town denies his biological humanity would confuse an evaluative expression with a descriptive classification. Inferring that no relation between virtue and membership exists would be equally hasty: the praise and criticism still regulate ideals of membership. This miniature teaches how to disambiguate a claim; it does not establish either assigned author's final theory.

Produce B4: two 300-word reconstructions with at least two locators each, followed by a 500-word analysis of a newcomer who cares for elders yet refuses a local initiation practice. Specify why the refusal occurs: she objects to exclusion of another group, not to all communal obligations. Determine which facts matter under each reading. Include a serious objection and a reply for each author, distinguishing your extensions from their explicit claims.

## Independent changed-case B4-I

In an invented community, a child with profound lifelong care needs cannot perform the public duties usually associated with mature personhood. A respected official infers that the child therefore has weaker claims to protection. In 400–500 words, assess the inference separately through Menkiti and Gyekye, identifying the sense of personhood doing the work. State whether a critic must challenge a definition, a connecting premise, or both. Forty minutes, both essays and citation sheet only; no AI/hints or prior case notes. Criteria: accurate paired reconstruction, no unsupported attribution of denied rights, and a reasoned account of the inference. Disagreeing with an author is compatible with competence.
