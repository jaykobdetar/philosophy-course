# C11B-M05 — Mills: institutions and critique

**16 hours. Guiding question:** How can a philosophical model explain institutional patterns without pretending that every participant signed an agreement? Prerequisites: Course 8's Mills chapter 1 and B1 claim types.

## Outcomes and reading

Read Charles W. Mills, ***The Racial Contract*, Cornell University Press 1997, chapter 2, complete**. Course 8's chapter 1 is available for reference, not a newly billed complete reading. ## Teaching

A philosophical model can illuminate a pattern by showing how apparently separate practices work together. Its usefulness depends on the relationships it reveals and the evidence it accommodates. A contract model therefore need not assert that every participant literally convened and consented. But abandoning literal consent does not make the model immune to criticism: ask what explanatory work the model performs, which mechanisms it identifies and what would count against its scope.

Distinguish **an institution's rule**, **a pattern of effects**, **an individual's intention**, and **a justification offered for the arrangement**. A rule can produce a patterned disadvantage without each administrator having a conscious intention to do so. The reverse also matters: the existence of unequal outcomes alone does not establish every proposed mechanism. Explanation needs a traceable connection among rules, history, practices and effects. In your chapter-2 reconstruction, name which of these the actual passage addresses.

Keep explanatory and normative claims distinct. Showing how a hierarchy is maintained does not automatically specify a remedy. Calling the hierarchy unjust requires standards or commitments, even if those are supplied elsewhere in the argument. A critique can expose a contradiction in an institution's declared ideals, question the ideals themselves, or explain why an apparently neutral theory overlooks relevant history. These are different argumentative tasks.

For a serious objection, choose the vulnerable link. Does the evidence support the model's breadth? Does a counterexample show that the model fails, or does it reveal that the claim needs a scope qualification? Does focusing on structure leave room for resistance and change? Do not substitute “some people are kind” for a challenge to an institutional explanation. Conversely, do not dismiss every individual variation as irrelevant without showing the level at which the model operates.

## Worked practice — original institution

An invented licensing board inherited a registry that excluded one neighborhood for decades. It now applies an apparently equal rule: only people already on the registry can train successors. Administrators insist they feel no hostility. Their sincerity does not settle whether the rule perpetuates the inherited exclusion; the mechanism runs through eligibility. Yet to establish a racialized pattern in a real institution, the analyst would need evidence about the registry, categories, history and consequences. The case illustrates a structural inference; it is not historical evidence for Mills.

Write B5, **700 words**, on one actual chapter-2 claim. Include a four-step reconstruction, two checked primary locators, one empirical premise requiring evidence beyond the course, and one serious objection. Use a claim ledger: textual claim / supporting passage / kind of inference / uncertainty. Your conclusion must stay within the evidence. The tutor checks whether the objection attacks the thesis actually reconstructed.

## Independent changed-case B5-I

In an invented city, an official school textbook presents a documented forced transfer of land from a racialized population as a voluntary exchange. The records of coercion are publicly accessible, and no rule bars that population from schools or archives. Pupils learn that current ownership therefore requires no further justification. A reformer proposes correcting the textbook and claims that this correction alone would restore both knowledge and the lost land. In 350–450 words, explain one relevant claim from Mills chapter 2, assess the reformer’s inference, and specify evidence needed to establish the textbook’s actual role in sustaining the arrangement. Identify one observation that would weaken that proposed explanation. Thirty-five minutes, chapter and citation sheet only; no AI/hints. Criteria: a source-grounded account of the mechanism, a defensible distinction between the effects of the proposed reform, and a genuine empirical defeater. Do not assume the invented history proves a general claim about real institutions.
