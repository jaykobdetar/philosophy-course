# C11A-M05 — Zhuangzi: story, perspective and skill

**24 hours. Guiding question:** What philosophical work can a story do without stating a single doctrine? Prerequisites: A1 interpretive method and A2–4 comparison within the tradition.

## Outcomes and assigned reading

Distinguish narrator, character and interpretive inference; compare two supported readings of a story; explain how skill and perspective challenge a generalization; state a productive cross-tradition connection and a limit.

Read **Zhuangzi chapters 1–3 and 6**, Brook Ziporyn, *Zhuangzi: The Essential Writings*, Hackett 2009. The accepted curriculum also permits another **identified complete scholarly translation**; verify complete coverage of these four chapters and record translator, publisher, year and locators before use. The 2009 assigned primary text was not inspected here. The “complete” requirement concerns assigned chapter coverage; the Hackett title itself is a selected edition. No freely circulating anonymous rendering is silently equivalent.

## Teaching

A story can expose a problem in a distinction without endorsing every sentence its characters speak. First list the speakers and the change between the opening and ending. Then ask what a reader is invited to reconsider. An entertaining reversal may undermine a confident judgment; it need not entail that all judgments are equally good. Identify exactly which scope the reversal reaches.

In chapter 1, attend to changes of scale and standpoint. A perspective can reveal limits in another perspective while remaining a perspective itself. To conclude that no judgment is possible would require another argument. In chapter 2, ask how assertions, disputants and transformations affect the claim being considered. A story about dreaming need not function as a modern argument that external reality does not exist. It may concern the stability of identity, the authority of a perspective, or how distinctions are made. These are rival interpretive possibilities to test, not interchangeable slogans.

Chapter 3's skilled practice raises a different question: can responsiveness surpass clumsy rule application while remaining disciplined? A learner might read a skill story as celebrating practice, another as loosening deliberate control. These readings can overlap, but their disagreement appears when a novice tries to imitate effortless conduct without training. Track details of attention, difficulty and adjustment. A successful act is not necessarily evidence that all explicit guidance is useless.

Chapter 6 changes the pressure again by attending to transformation and attitudes toward life and death. Do not erase loss or bodily change by declaring the text “just metaphor.” Nor should a character's attitude become a universal command to feel a particular emotion. Compare what the narrative portrays, what its contrasts suggest, and what you can responsibly defend as a philosophical implication.

A strong interpretation explains more than a favorite sentence. Use a constraint test: which detail would be puzzling if your reading were true? A rival that handles that detail better deserves a response. Cross-tradition comparison begins only after this work; shared vocabulary such as “skepticism” can otherwise obscure different targets.

## Worked practice — original story

A novice cartographer mocks a fisher's irregular river map. During a flood the official road map becomes unusable, while the fisher navigates by channels. One reading says all maps are arbitrary. Another says maps select features relative to a task and can be better or worse under changed conditions. The fisher's successful navigation favors the second reading because it explains why the reversal is informative. Yet the story does not prove that practical success is the only standard for every map. The strongest conclusion is narrower than the dramatic reversal invites.

Produce A5: a 900-word interpretation of one complete episode in chapters 1–3 or 6. Use a scene map, two rival readings, three details that discriminate between them, and one unresolved feature. Add a 200-word comparison with a thinker already studied: one shared question and one difference in purpose or warrant. Do not use an episode reserved for your final fresh form; record your chosen episode.

## Independent changed-case A5-I

In a new invented story, an experienced musician follows no written score during a difficult performance, but stops, listens, and rehearses slowly when encountering an unfamiliar instrument. A reader declares that this proves learning rules is always harmful. In 350–450 words, assess the inference by comparison with the skill episode in Zhuangzi chapter 3. Identify one detail in each that constrains your interpretation and one difference preventing straightforward equivalence. Forty minutes, assigned sources and locator sheet only; no model answers or assistance. Criteria: story-to-claim reasoning, specific evidence, and an explicit limit.
