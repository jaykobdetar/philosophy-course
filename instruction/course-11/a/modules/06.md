## Teaching

“Human nature is bad” can suggest an incurable stain to a modern reader. But a thinker who advocates learning and transformation cannot simply be read as denying every possibility of goodness. Separate the starting materials, their unregulated development, the capacities needed for learning, and achieved ethical conduct. The argument's explanatory burden is to show why improvement needs deliberate formation and why such formation is possible.

Chapter 1 supplies a setting for thinking about learning as a sustained activity involving models, accumulation and directed effort. Chapter 23 stages a dispute about nature and goodness. Build the argument from its distinctions rather than from the shock of the headline. If “nature” is restricted to what is unlearned, practices acquired through effort will not count as natural in that sense. If another author includes developmental tendencies under “nature,” a surface opposition can partly reflect how the category is drawn.

Partly does not mean entirely. Even after aligning definitions, the authors may disagree about what spontaneous tendencies contribute, how reliable they are, and how social practices make goodness possible. To test a “merely verbal” interpretation, ask whether the two accounts predict the same development under a specified intervention. To test a “total opposition” interpretation, ask whether both allow ordinary people to become good and both require cultivation. Shared conclusions can coexist with different explanations.

Translation work requires humility and specificity. Compare what an English term encourages a reader to infer. A rendering such as “deliberate effort” may make agency salient; a different rendering might suggest fabrication or artificiality. Do not claim that either is the actual wording of a named translator until you have checked it. More importantly, a difference in English associations is not proof of the correct Chinese meaning. Your conclusion should say what philosophical inference becomes easier or harder under the identified rendering and what textual evidence constrains it.

## Worked practice — original models

Model P says: a seed has a tendency to grow, but soil and care are necessary. Model Q says: timber can become furniture, but design and workmanship explain its useful form. Both allow successful development and both require intervention. They differ over how much the end is explained by the starting material's tendencies. This distinction shows why “both require education” cannot by itself dissolve a Mengzi–Xunzi dispute. The models are teaching analogies; do not attribute their exact language or predictions to either author.
