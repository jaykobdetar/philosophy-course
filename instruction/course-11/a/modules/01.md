# C11A-M01 — Context and reading practice

**10 hours. Guiding question:** How can a short saying support a philosophical interpretation without becoming an invented treatise? Prerequisites: Courses 1 and 6; distinguish paraphrase, premise and inference.

## Outcomes

Create a provisional context map; distinguish an author's words from a translator's gloss and your inference; begin six revisable term records; explain why comparison needs a shared question and a limit.

## Assigned reading and access

Read David B. Wong, SEP [“Chinese Ethics”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/), §1, “Characteristics of Chinese Ethics: Practical Focus and Closeness to Pre-theoretical Experience,” complete. Read Bryan W. Van Norden, SEP [“Mencius”](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mencius/), §1 opening paragraphs only, ending with the five themes and “ethical cultivation.” These bounded passages were inspected on 2026-10-20. Stop there; the remainder is not hidden required reading. No primary-text mastery is required yet.

## Teaching

Begin with the kind of evidence a text offers. A saying addressed to a particular questioner may recommend conduct in a specific situation. Turning it into “everyone must always do this” adds a scope premise. Conversely, refusing all general interpretation would make it impossible to explain why related sayings recur. Good reading moves between a local exchange and a provisional account of the larger pattern, testing each against the other.

Use three columns: **textual observation**, **interpretive claim**, **additional assumption**. “The speaker contrasts two ways of governing” is an observation. “The contrast concerns the motives produced in subjects” is an interpretation requiring support. “Only one method may ever be used” is a further assumption that the contrast may not warrant. This distinction makes disagreement tractable: two readers can agree about the wording yet disagree about its implications.

The required context introduces early Chinese ethical inquiry and the background to Mengzi. It does not establish a uniform “Chinese mindset.” Record historical claims with their source and uncertainty. Confucius precedes the Warring States thinkers; do not place every author at the same court or imagine every received text as a single author's finished manuscript. A context map should show a sequence and relationships, not invented conversations.

For each glossary entry, leave room for passage, translation, provisional sense, contrasting use and unresolved question. Start `ren` as a question about humane conduct or character, `li` about patterned propriety and ritual, `yi` about fitting or right action, `de` about a kind of excellence or influence, `dao` about a way, and `xing` about nature. These are orientation prompts, not final translations. The later modules must determine what each term does in an argument. Similar English labels can hide different roles.

## Worked practice — original invented miniature

A teacher says, “A good host remembers a guest's needs,” then later, “Do not exhaust the household to impress a guest.” A novice concludes that hospitality is inconsistent. A better reconstruction distinguishes attending to needs from competitive display. Its missing premise is that meeting a need need not require extravagance. A remaining difficulty is an unusually costly genuine need. The reconstruction earns its plausibility by explaining both remarks and naming the case that could defeat it; it does not claim the fictional teacher stated the missing premise.

Apply the three-column method to this miniature in 150 words. Then make A1: a one-page provisional context map and six-entry glossary with “not yet evidenced” in empty primary-passage fields. Add a 250-word account of how you will test an interpretation against a difficult passage. Success means the map states its limits and the glossary remains revisable.

## Independent changed-case A1-I

A fictional teacher says, “A student should imitate a skilled teacher,” and “A student who cannot question a teacher has not learned.” In 250–350 words, reconstruct a compatible interpretation and a genuinely conflicting interpretation. State what further textual evidence would decide between them. Add a 100-word comparison-method note explaining why calling this “Chinese obedience versus Western freedom” would overreach. Thirty minutes, source-open; no AI, hints, model answers or prewritten response. Declare exposure. The criteria are fidelity to the supplied remarks, explicit assumptions, and a testable distinction, not agreement with a preferred answer.

Rewrite that leap as a qualified inference. After two study days, spend ten minutes of the revision allocation reconstructing the miniature without notes, then check it.
