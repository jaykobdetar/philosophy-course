# C11A-M04 — The Mohist challenge

**16 hours. Guiding question:** Does caring for everyone require treating everyone identically? Prerequisites: A2 family/role reasoning and A3 extension.

## Outcomes and assigned reading

Reconstruct the case for inclusive concern; distinguish its scope from equal treatment and equal affection; formulate a serious Confucian objection; test the practical premises of a proposed reply.

Read **Mozi chapter 16, “Universal Love III,” complete**, in Ian Johnston, *The Mozi: A Complete Translation*, Chinese University Press/Columbia University Press, 2010. Other translators call the topic “Impartial Caring.” A chapter title alone does not establish the exact strength of the position. The assigned edition and chapter are fixed by the curriculum; its full passage was not inspected here. Obtain a lawful copy and record page boundaries. Do not substitute another chapter on the same theme or an anthology excerpt missing the replies.

## Teaching

Separate three propositions before evaluating any argument. **Inclusive concern** says no relevant person's good is simply excluded. **Identical treatment** says each receives the same action or resource. **No special relationships** says personal bonds should disappear. The first does not logically entail the other two. People with different needs can receive different treatment under a common standard; parents can act as reliable caregivers without taking outsiders to have no importance. Whether the chapter requires a stronger view must be established from its reasons and replies.

A reconstruction of the Mohist challenge should show why restricted concern is implicated in harm and why widening concern is proposed as a remedy. Identify the move from diagnosing disorder to recommending a norm. That move needs a claim about how agents would act under the norm and what counts as benefit. Do not confuse the desirable consequences of widespread adoption with a guarantee that unilateral adoption is costless. Nor should a modern label such as “utilitarian” replace the chapter's own account of the relevant goods.

An objection may challenge feasibility, motivation or moral justification. “People will not do it” attacks feasibility; “this does not give me a reason to do it” attacks justification; “training can elicit this conduct only by destroying a valuable relation” concerns its moral cost and mechanism. Put each objection against the premise it actually contests. A Confucian response might defend differentiated responsibilities as ethically constitutive or as a route to wider concern. Present this as a reasoned position grounded in assigned sayings, not as what every Confucian must say.

The chapter's replies matter. For each major objection, ask whether the response supplies an example, appeals to a standard, challenges an assumption, or changes the practical question. A proposal can be coherent yet under-supported empirically. A useful evaluation names the remaining uncertainty rather than issuing a blanket verdict.

## Worked practice — original case

Two households share a flood shelter. One adult can rescue a nearby child; another can reach a distant injured adult. Caring about both people is compatible with different actions by the rescuers because distance and competence differ. This shows why inclusive concern does not alone require identical treatment. It does not settle whether each should always favor their own household when claims and access are equal. That second question is where a dispute about partiality remains.

Create A4: a three-column matrix for **concern, treatment, relationship**, with one claimed textual requirement and locator in each column. Then write a 600-word dispute: four-step argument from Mozi 16; a plausible objection based on an assigned Analects passage; a reply; and one remaining empirical or normative premise. The tutor should test whether the objection targets the actual chapter rather than a maximal equal-affection doctrine you invented.

## Independent changed-case A4-I

A village relief organizer gives identical food parcels to every household, despite one household having four children and another having one adult. She says that because she loves everyone impartially, changing parcel sizes would betray Mozi. In 350–450 words, assess her inference using chapter 16 and one Analects passage. Then change the case: supplies are enough for everyone's basic needs, but a parent reserves a modest extra for their own child. State what further argument would be required to evaluate that act. Thirty-five minutes, primary texts and citation sheet only; no content help. Criteria: distinguish the three propositions, identify the relevant textual warrant, and preserve a genuine dispute.
