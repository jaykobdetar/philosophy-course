# Early Modern Philosophy

**Stage:** Core
**Requires:** [Introduction to Philosophy](introduction.md)
**Status:** outline

## What this unit is for

The seventeenth- and eighteenth-century arguments about knowledge, mind, and reality that set the agenda for modern philosophy, read as a conversation between rationalists and empiricists.

## Anchor readings

- Descartes, *Meditations on First Philosophy*, all six (AT VII)
- Elisabeth of Bohemia and Descartes, their 1643 correspondence on how mind and body interact (cite by letter date)
- Spinoza, *Ethics* Part I, selections (part and proposition, for example E1p11)
- Leibniz, *Discourse on Metaphysics* or *Monadology* (section)
- Locke, *An Essay Concerning Human Understanding*, selections: I.ii (innate ideas) and II.viii (primary and secondary qualities) (book, chapter, section)
- Berkeley, *A Treatise Concerning the Principles of Human Knowledge*, Part I, §§1–33
- Hume, *An Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding*, Sections II–VII and XII (section and paragraph)

## Mastery criteria

1. Reconstruct Descartes's route from methodical doubt to God and the external world, and explain the objection of the Cartesian Circle.
2. Explain Elisabeth's objection about mind–body interaction and assess Descartes's replies.
3. Compare rationalist and empiricist accounts of ideas and knowledge (Descartes, Spinoza, and Leibniz; Locke, Berkeley, and Hume).
4. Reconstruct Berkeley's argument for idealism and one objection to it.
5. Reconstruct Hume's arguments about causation and induction, and explain his "sceptical solution".
6. Situate each figure historically without anachronism.
