# Political Philosophy

**Stage:** Core
**Requires:** [Ethics](ethics.md)
**Status:** outline

## What this unit is for

What could make political authority legitimate, what justice requires of institutions, and the limits of state power, including critiques of the social contract tradition from within and outside it.

## Anchor readings

- Hobbes, *Leviathan*, chapters 13–15 and 17–18 (chapter and paragraph)
- Locke, *Second Treatise of Government*, chapters 2, 5, and 8–9 (section)
- Rousseau, *The Social Contract*, Books I–II, selections (book and chapter)
- Mill, *On Liberty*, chapters 1–2
- Mary Wollstonecraft, *A Vindication of the Rights of Woman*, selections
- W. E. B. Du Bois, *The Souls of Black Folk*, chapter 1
- Rawls, *A Theory of Justice*, revised edition, selections including §§3–4, 11–13, and 24–26
- Nozick, *Anarchy, State, and Utopia*, chapter 7, selections
- Susan Moller Okin, *Justice, Gender, and the Family*, selections
- Charles W. Mills, *The Racial Contract*, selections

## Mastery criteria

1. Reconstruct and compare Hobbes's, Locke's, and Rousseau's accounts of political authority and consent.
2. Explain Mill's harm principle and apply it to a contested case.
3. Reconstruct Rawls's argument from the original position for his two principles, including the difference principle.
4. Reconstruct Nozick's entitlement theory and his Wilt Chamberlain argument against patterned principles, and assess them.
5. Explain Wollstonecraft's, Okin's, or Mills's critique of the social contract tradition and evaluate it.
6. Defend a political principle against a serious objection.
