# Program guide

## Purpose and recommendation

This program is for an adult beginning formal philosophical study, with no college coursework assumed, who can protect five to seven focused hours each week. Its aim is sustained philosophical competence: reading difficult arguments accurately, reasoning with formal and informal tools, writing defensible positions, understanding historical and cultural contexts, and conducting a bounded independent inquiry. It is designed around writing, with conversation, interactive computer exercises, and optional audio as supports.

This handbook is the revised study plan replacing the earlier fifteen-unit outline. Keep the original collection as a source library; use the sequence, reading boundaries, and assessment rules here for the new program.

The recommended route combines a common foundation, a sustained global-tradition course, a selected area, an advanced seminar, and a research capstone. It is a complete self-study plan, not an accredited degree, a substitute for a whole undergraduate education, or a validated claim of equality with a university graduate. Philosophy majors differ substantially. The design borrows defensible features from several programs rather than treating a single university as the universal standard.

The biggest practical conclusion is that this is a multi-year education. Do not try to imitate a full-time undergraduate timetable in a few evening hours. Work on one main course at a time, maintain a small cumulative-review practice, and take the time required to revise your writing. Finish useful stages along the way rather than regarding everything before the capstone as unfinished preparation.

## Prerequisites and flexibility

The numbered sequence is a convenient default. The real dependencies are conceptual: argument analysis before most essays; ordinary-language logic before formalization; truth-functional and first-order semantics before technical language work; Descartes and Hume before the central Kant problems; ethical reasoning before advanced justice; contextual competence before cross-cultural comparison; and research practice before the capstone.

You may study history or ethics while still improving formal proofs. Full first-order proof proficiency is not a gate against all philosophical reading. Keep one main course at a time at this weekly budget; if you run logic alongside another subject, divide the same hours rather than doubling them. A learner may demonstrate prerequisites through fresh independent tasks instead of repeating already mastered material, but the assessment evidence must be recorded.

> • Breadth across history, value, and theoretical philosophy, with formal reasoning somewhere in the curriculum

> • A transition from introductory courses to demanding reading and advanced seminars
>
> • Repeated philosophical writing, criticism, and revision
>
> • Some opportunity for independent work; an extensive thesis is one route, not a requirement of every strong philosophy major

University course counts alone are a poor comparison. An eight-course major with junior independent papers and a senior thesis can entail more work than the number eight suggests. ## What completion means here

Keep a portfolio of work and an evidence record. Completion means that you have finished the required route, can meet its stated learning outcomes on independent work, have corrected serious misunderstandings, and can explain and defend the final project. It does not mean that an AI’s numerical score certifies university-level attainment.

Use two descriptions accurately:

> • Self-study completion: the work and readiness checks are complete, but assessment is principally self- and AI-assisted
>
> • Externally reviewed completion: a suitably qualified human has reviewed selected work against the stated criteria and documented strengths and remaining weaknesses

Neither description awards credit. A single human review cannot certify every topic, and several AI models agreeing does not create independent faculty assessment. The evidence record should say what was reviewed, by whom, and with what limits.

## Workload and pace

Every estimate below includes focused reading and rereading, notes, exercises, tutorials or self-dialogue, drafting, feedback review, revision, assessment, and cumulative review. The totals are planning estimates, not credit conversions or guarantees of quality. The first two courses provide a calibration period. Record actual time in fifteen-minute increments for a few modules, then adjust later estimates without cutting essential learning outcomes.

Run a pacing check during the first four to six weeks and again at the first dense module in a new stage. Record pages understood rather than merely scanned, time spent reconstructing, draft words that survive revision, and help needed. After two contrasting sessions, project the remaining assigned pages and writing separately. For planning sensitivity, forty dense pages at four pages per hour require ten reading hours before exercises or revision; at two pages per hour they require twenty. These are illustrative rates, not claims about you or universal norms. The advanced seminar now gives forty-four hours to core analysis because a twenty-eight-hour bundle left too little room for its four reconstructions and maps. If the core cannot be read responsibly within an estimate, extend the module and update the overall projection. Optional enrichment is the first thing to remove; required arguments are not silently cut to protect a tidy total.

Use the following six-hour weekly rhythm as a starting point:

> • 120 minutes close reading and source notes, in two sessions
>
> • 60 minutes argument reconstruction, logic, or a computer-based exercise
>
> • 100 minutes drafting or revising the current written task
>
> • 40 minutes discussion, oral explanation, or a tutor session
>
> • 25 minutes retrieval from earlier modules and correction of errors
>
> • 15 minutes planning, source checks, and recording the next step

At five hours, reduce the size of that week’s reading/task package and carry the remaining hour forward. At seven hours, add an hour of reading or revision. Do not remove revision, postpone all writing until the end, or count the same activity twice. Logic-heavy weeks move time from prose writing to problems; essay weeks move it from new reading to drafting.

Optional listening during night work is enrichment. Use familiar material or a lecturer’s overview, and only where listening is safe and compatible with work. Do not operate machinery, drive, or undertake safety-critical work while doing demanding philosophical exercises. Distracted audio does not count toward the focused workload and does not replace primary-text reading. Afterwards, an optional five-minute note can record one argument you remember and one question to revisit when rested. There is no required audio quota.

## The study cycle

**1. Orient.** Read a short historical/context note and the module question. Identify unfamiliar terms. A beginner may see one brief worked example before attempting a different task.

**2. Read.** First locate the question and conclusion; then reread the relevant passage, noting reasons and qualifications. Avoid transcribing the whole text.

**3. Reconstruct.** Put the argument into your own words, mark implied premises, and distinguish the author’s claim from your charitable repair.

**4. Test.** Try a counterexample, an alternative interpretation, a conflicting case, or a rival argument. Identify exactly which premise or inference is challenged.

**5. Write.** Make a bounded claim, support it with reasons and textual evidence, and address the strongest relevant objection.

**6. Discuss.** Explain the argument without reading a script. Ask for an objection that targets your actual claim, then revise if necessary.

**7. Check.** Consult a scholarly source and verify quotations, locators, and attribution against the primary text. Record whether the secondary source changed your reading.

**8. Revise.** Make substantive changes before polishing style. Explain the two most important changes in a short revision memo.

**9. Retrieve.** Later, reconstruct the key idea without notes and apply it to a fresh case. Reopen the text afterwards to correct errors.

This is a flexible teaching sequence. It does not require unnecessary struggle with an unexplained technical term or forbid a useful lecture before reading. Support is gradually reduced as you gain skill.

## Academic tools for beginners

Course 1 teaches these practices in context. A lack of prior college credits is not a reason to delay philosophical work.

> • Distinguish an argument from a report, illustration, explanation, or rhetorical question
>
> • Paraphrase a short passage accurately and separate it from a quotation
>
> • Find a claim’s supporting reason and identify an unstated assumption
>
> • Use necessary and sufficient conditions, validity, soundness, counterexamples, and charity
>
> • Write a paragraph with a specific claim, evidence, and explanation
>
> • Cite a passage precisely and maintain a bibliographic record
>
> • Read a scholarly article’s introduction, argumentative structure, and conclusion without assuming every detail is equally important
>
> • Explain the difference between empirical evidence and a philosophical premise

When difficulty persists, add a targeted bridge of six to twelve focused hours. For reading, work through one paragraph at a time with vocabulary glosses and three paraphrases. For writing, develop three 150-word claim–reason–objection paragraphs with feedback. For basic quantitative reasoning, practise percentages, conditional probability, and interpreting a small table before the science course’s probability module. These are extra hours when needed, not hidden prerequisites or admissions tests.

## Writing that develops across the program

Course 1 moves from 150-word paraphrases to 300–500-word reconstructions, then to a 1,000–1,200-word essay. Early core courses use 600–800-word interpretive exercises and 1,500–1,800-word revised essays. Middle courses add comparison and source evaluation, generally culminating in 1,800–2,200 words. Advanced work uses a 2,500–3,500-word seminar paper. The capstone is a 5,000–7,000-word independent research paper with a bounded original contribution, such as a refined objection, a justified distinction, or a more accurate interpretation. A longer thesis is an optional extension, not evidence of quality by length alone.

For each substantial paper, retain the proposal, outline, first draft, feedback, revision, source list, and a 200-word revision memo. Do not submit several near-identical essays merely to increase the count. One paper revised seriously is often more educational than three unexamined drafts, but revision is paired with later independent work to show transfer.

The core paragraph template is temporary scaffolding: “I argue that … because … . A serious objection is … . This matters because … . My reply is … .” Mature work need not sound formulaic. Interpretive and comparative essays may need a more complex structure, but clarity about the question, evidence, and inferential steps remains essential.

## Feedback and readiness

The criteria are stable; the expected complexity rises. Evaluate six dimensions:

1\. Question and thesis: a precise answer to a genuine problem, with scope appropriate to the assignment

2\. Interpretation and context: an accurate, charitable account of the relevant position, supported by exact passages and without anachronism

3\. Reasoning: explicit reasons that support the conclusion; important assumptions, distinctions, and counterexamples handled responsibly

4\. Objection and reply: a substantial challenge to the actual argument and a responsive answer or justified revision

5\. Evidence and research: traceable citations, verified claims, suitable sources, and a clear distinction between evidence and inference

6\. Organization and expression: a reader can follow the argument; terms remain consistent; prose does not hide gaps in reasoning

Use the descriptors “not yet demonstrated,” “developing with support,” “independently competent,” and “strong and transferable.” “Independently competent” means the work succeeds on the assignment’s essential tasks without substantive coaching, though it may contain minor errors and need stylistic revision. Serious source fabrication, a central misreading, an unsupported central inference, or inability to explain one’s own paper prevents readiness even if the prose is excellent.

A normal course has three sources of evidence: a revised paper, an independent short task or problem set on a fresh passage/case, and an unscripted explanation or accessible equivalent. A delayed retrieval/transfer task in a later course checks retention. For each essential outcome group in the course assessment blueprint, retain one satisfactory module artifact and one independently competent fresh task. The module artifact may be supported practice, with the assistance recorded; the independent task must be completed without content assistance. A paper supplies depth but cannot stand in for unaddressed outcome groups. This evidence rule is a local design choice, not an empirically validated cutoff or university grading standard.

Written tasks are open-text when interpretation matters, but closed to AI assistance. Short recall checks can be closed-note. Formal examinations should say exactly which sources and aids are permitted. Oral work may be recorded or documented with consent; a live text conversation or additional timed written questions can provide an accessibility alternative, with its limits noted. Anxiety, accent, typing speed, or disability should not be mistaken for philosophical weakness.

If an outcome is not ready, name the problem, assign two or three targeted exercises, and repeat only the relevant component with a fresh prompt. Keep prior attempts. No penalty is needed. Do not force a full course restart because one proof technique or textual interpretation needs work.

## Cumulative review and returning after a break

After a module, make one pivotal retrieval prompt by default. Add at most two more only for fragile concepts: an argument, distinction, or application. Revisit it in the next study cycle, a few weeks later, and in a later course. Use the weekly review slot for the oldest or least secure prompt, not every accumulated card. Merge redundant prompts and retire a prompt after a successful later-course transfer, keeping only occasional cumulative sampling. A failed recall gets correction against the source and a focused later retest; it does not create a growing catch-up debt. Adjust intervals to actual recall. These are practical heuristics, not a scientific formula for philosophy.

After a long break, spend one session reconstructing the last course’s central questions and reread your last revision memo. Attempt two representative tasks. Resume from the first missing skill or unfinished module. A forgotten label does not require repeating work you can still explain and apply.

## Interactive work on a computer

Use ordinary documents or an offline diagram tool for argument maps. Each node should contain a complete proposition, with arrows showing support or objection. The map is valuable only if you can explain the relation in prose. Use a spreadsheet for small decision tables and probability examples; a local text file works equally well. For logic, use the selected proof system’s exercises and a compatible proof checker, while also producing unaided solutions on paper or in a plain document.

No custom server, API key, coding environment, or new software installation is a prerequisite. Optional tools are replaceable. An interactive activity always has a low-technology equivalent so that tool setup does not become an unplanned part of the curriculum.

For every interactive activity, save a small four-part record: the input passage or case; what you changed; the resulting map, table, or proof; and a two-sentence explanation of what the change shows and what it does not show. Then check the result against the source or formal rule. A paper outline, hand-drawn table, or written proof is the low-technology equivalent. The activity is complete when the reasoning is inspectable, not when an app awards a point.

For a solo five-minute explanation, spend one minute stating the question and claim, one giving the strongest reason, one stating the strongest objection, one answering or narrowing the claim, and one naming an uncertainty and next check. You may record it privately if useful. The accessible written alternative is five short paragraphs with the same functions; do not treat speaking speed or performance polish as philosophical evidence.

## AI coaching and assessment limits

The learner writes the assessed arguments. An AI may ask questions, define a term, show a different worked example, diagnose a draft, suggest a passage to reread, or generate a clearly labelled practice problem. It may not write or rewrite the learner’s assessed paragraphs, invent quotations, provide a gate answer, or grade confidently when it cannot inspect the source.

For a tutoring session, supply the course, module, exact edition/passage, your attempt, the time available, and the requested kind of help. Ask the tutor to identify one or two consequential issues first, point to evidence, and let you attempt a repair before giving more assistance. For historical or global traditions, require attention to translation, context, interpretive disagreement, and the limits of comparison.

Keep a brief assistance declaration with assessed work: tools used, kinds of help, and what was done independently. Use a fresh prompt and, where feasible, a separate assessment session for independent tasks. This reduces immediate answer leakage but does not make the model independent or reliable. Automated proof checking verifies formal steps under a defined calculus; it does not establish that the English argument was faithfully formalized.

Source verification means opening the cited edition and checking that it supports the claim. A second AI repeating the citation is not verification. When uncertain, mark the claim unresolved and consult the text, an authoritative reference, or a human specialist. Never treat a fluent invented quotation as a memory aid.

## Human dialogue and external feedback

Peer discussion supplies perspectives and exposes ambiguities; subject expertise improves interpretation and assessment. They perform different jobs. A practical low-cost option is a reading partner or small group that meets periodically to exchange short papers and discuss the assigned question. A stronger option is occasional paid or volunteer feedback from a philosophy instructor, doctoral researcher, or subject specialist, with scope, confidentiality, fees, and qualifications checked first. Audited courses, university extension, public philosophy groups, and library services may help, but access and offerings must be checked locally.

A useful review request includes the actual source passage, assignment, rubric, your draft, and two questions about it. Ask the reviewer to identify the most consequential error, the strongest objection, and a concrete revision task. Suggested checkpoints are the end of the methods course, the first middle-stage essay, the advanced seminar, and the capstone proposal and final paper. These are recommendations, not appointments, promised availability, or purchases.

If no human review is available, proceed with clearly labelled self-study evidence and a skeptical verification routine. Preserve work so that later external review is possible. Do not present unreviewed completion as independently validated expertise.

Session 2: Read OpenStax 5.3 “Arguments” for twenty minutes. For twenty minutes, identify reasons and conclusions in four short arguments. Use these original examples: “Every borrower agreed to return the book; Mira is a borrower; so Mira agreed to return it.” “The lights are on, so somebody must be home.” “A rule applies to everyone only if it applies to the mayor; this rule does not apply to the mayor; so it does not apply to everyone.” “Many people like this painting, so it is a good painting.” Spend twenty minutes explaining which conclusions follow deductively and which need additional assumptions. Use twenty minutes to revise the first session’s paragraph around a single claim and supporting reason. Finish with ten minutes of recall and correction.

Session 3: Read OpenStax 5.2 “Logical Statements” and the validity/soundness examples in 5.3 for twenty minutes. Work for twenty minutes on these distinctions: a valid argument with a false premise; an invalid argument with a true conclusion; being a square as sufficient but not necessary for being a rectangle; paying an entry fee as necessary but not sufficient for winning a stipulated race in which only paid entrants are eligible to win. In twenty minutes, invent and explain your own examples. Take twenty minutes to write two versions of “All good explanations are simple”: one charitable argument supporting it and one counterexample. Finish with ten minutes checking whether the counterexample targets the stated claim.

Session 4: Read Euthyphro from 5d to the start of 8b in the [<u>marked Jowett copy</u>](https://persweb.wabash.edu/facstaff/helmang/phi110-1516F/phi110-handouts/Euthyphro.pdf) (PDF pages 4–7, up to but not including the 8b marker) for twenty minutes. Spend twenty minutes listing the first definitions and objections, using exact locators. Spend twenty minutes drawing a small argument map in a document or on paper. Use twenty minutes to write a 150-word explanation of why an example of piety may fail as a definition of piety. Finish with ten minutes of questions for the next tutorial. A tutor should diagnose at most two problems, not rewrite the paragraph.

If these tasks take longer, extend the opening period. The first six hours count within the modules below; they are not additional homework.

**1. Output: six argument reconstructions, four definition tests, and a 200-word explanation of validity versus soundness. PC activity: rearrange premises and inspect what the argument requires.

Dialogue: defend each horn in turn and distinguish what causes something to be loved from what makes it pious. Apology and the examined life, 8 hours.** Read the whole dialogue in the [<u>marked CHS version</u>](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/primary-source/plato-the-apology-of-socrates-sb/); reread the displayed 20c–23c, 28b–30c, and 40c–41c sections. Write 400 words comparing knowledge and true opinion. Do not describe omitted passages as read.

Dialogue: what survives each stage of doubt? Use exact textual evidence rather than a slogan about “I think, therefore I am.”

Output: reconstruct the stronger and weaker principles in the paper, separate the pond example from the general argument, and write 600 words on demandingness, distance, or collective responsibility. ## Readiness and remediation

Progress to ordinary core reading when you can attribute a passage correctly, give reasons rather than assertions, and compose an intelligible claim–reason paragraph on fresh material. Progress to advanced essay work only after the substantial essay and independent task show competent interpretation, reasoning, attribution, and organization. If a weakness remains, use the targeted bridge described earlier. Continue discussing philosophy while developing prose; do not confuse difficulty with academic conventions with inability to reason.

Optional enrichment: a lecture on Plato or Descartes after the relevant first reading, or another dialogue. No enrichment is required for assessment, and it is outside the 120 hours.

# Course 2 Formal logic

## Scope and preparation

150 focused hours. Begin after Course 1’s argument and paragraph modules, or after Course 1 in the default sequence. Ordinary language is the starting point; no prior symbolic logic or advanced mathematics is assumed. The aim is proficiency in truth-functional and first-order logic with identity, not a survey of every logic.

Use forall x Calgary, Fall 2025, Parts I–VII, chapters 1–41, with matching solutions. Preserve one version and its notation for the course. The official text’s sample teaching plan also includes chapters 45–47; this local course defers them to optional extension because the learner needs substantial independent practice and writing. Parts VIII–IX are not silently required.

Outcomes: distinguish truth from validity; symbolize and translate back; use truth tables and interpretations; produce natural-deduction proofs; respect quantifier and identity restrictions; explain what a formal result does and does not establish. Evidence must sample translation, semantics, proof, and explanation separately.

## Ordered modules

**1. Basic logical notions, 12 hours.** Part I, chapters 1–3. Explain validity, soundness, consistency, and equivalence using original examples. For each chapter, complete four varied exercises, check after attempting them, and record conceptual errors.

**2. Truth-functional syntax and symbolization, 18 hours.** Part II, chapters 4–8. Practise main connectives, brackets, inclusive disjunction, material conditional, and ambiguity. Complete at least twenty-four short symbolizations, including translation back into English. Compare two plausible formalizations of an ambiguous sentence.

**3. Truth-functional semantics, 18 hours.** Part III, chapters 9–15. Construct complete and abbreviated truth tables, classify sentences and arguments, and provide countervaluations. Complete at least sixteen problems spanning validity, equivalence, and consistency. Explain one countervaluation in prose.

**4. Natural deduction for truth-functional logic, 26 hours.** Part IV, chapters 16–22. Work through one example per new rule, then a partially completed proof, then independent mixed practice. Complete at least twenty proofs including conditional proof, indirect proof, and nested assumptions. For five proofs, write a strategy paragraph before using a checker.

**5. First-order language, 18 hours.** Part V, chapters 23–29. Domains, predicates, quantifiers, scope, identity, and multiple quantification. Complete twenty-four symbolizations with domain declarations. Explain the difference between “everyone admires someone” and “someone is admired by everyone” using a small diagram.

**6. Interpretations and countermodels, 18 hours.** Part VI, chapters 30–35. Build finite interpretations, assess quantified sentences, and distinguish inability to find a countermodel from proof of validity. Complete twelve interpretation/countermodel tasks and explain two in ordinary prose.

**7. First-order natural deduction, 24 hours.** Part VII, chapters 36–41. Complete sixteen mixed proofs covering quantifier rules, identity, and rule restrictions. Make an error card for each recurring misuse of a name, variable, or undischarged assumption. Use Carnap only in the same supported calculus; a plain written solution with the book’s rules is sufficient.

**8. Transfer and independent assessment, 16 hours.** Formalize one short philosophical argument and write 800–1,000 words about interpretive choices and limitations. Complete an unaided mixed assessment with translations, semantic tests, truth-functional proofs, quantified proofs, and a countermodel. Explain two proof strategies orally or in text, then complete a smaller fresh set after a delay. Assessment and delayed work are included in the sixteen hours.

## Assignment selection and checking

For a textbook exercise set, choose the first accessible example of each new problem type, then a middle and later item that changes the structure. Record chapter and item labels in the work log. The quotas above are minimum sampling guides, not an achievement score: add practice where errors remain and do not complete forty identical easy items instead of mixed tasks. Before an assessment, choose unseen textbook problems with published answers where available and keep the answers closed. For items without solutions, use a checked formal derivation or countermodel; do not accept unsupported AI approval.

Readiness requires independent success across all four families: faithful symbolization, semantic analysis, rule-correct proof, and conceptual explanation. A recurring quantifier-scope or assumption-discharge error requires remediation even if most easy items are correct. Correctly noticing and repairing a transcription slip is different from misunderstanding a rule. If proofs are difficult, add faded examples and a smaller fresh set; continue historical or ethical study while practising, but postpone advanced technical work that depends on the missing skill.

Optional extension: chapters 45–47 on normal forms, functional completeness, and equivalence proofs, then chapter 48 on soundness. Modal logic chapters 42–44 belong to a further course. Budget additional time explicitly if you choose them.

# Course 3 Ancient philosophy

## Scope and core readings

120 hours; prerequisite Course 1. This is a focused study of inquiry, justice, explanation, and the good life in selected Greek traditions, not the whole ancient world. The Classical Chinese option in Course 11 supplies sustained study of another ancient tradition. Every route includes the bounded early Buddhist encounter in Course 7 and Islamic/Jewish work in Course 4; the Africana option is predominantly modern and is not described as an ancient survey.

Read Plato, Republic I 327a–354c, II 357a–367e, IV 427d–445e, VI 509d–511e, VII 514a–521b, and IX 577c–580c; Phaedo 64a–69e and 72e–84b. Use Grube/Reeve’s Republic, Hackett 1992, and Grube/Cooper’s Five Dialogues for Phaedo; cite Stephanus. The no-purchase marked routes are [<u>Republic in Gutenberg number 55201</u>](https://www.gutenberg.org/files/55201/55201-h/55201-h.htm), chiefly Jowett’s 1888 third edition with Stephanus numbering incorporated from the 1908 edition, and [<u>CHS Phaedo</u>](https://www-current.chs.harvard.edu/primary-source/plato-phaedo-sb/), Jowett adapted by Gregory Nagy, Miriam Carlisle, and Soo-Young Kim. The local Gutenberg Republic number 1497 lacks those markers; use number 55201 for these bounded assignments. Cite the named version actually read. Read Aristotle, Categories 1–5; Physics II.1–3 and II.8; De Anima II.1–3; use the relevant translations in Jonathan Barnes, editor, The Complete Works of Aristotle, revised Oxford translation, Princeton 1984, with Bekker numbers. Read Epicurus, Letter to Menoeceus, complete, preserved in Diogenes Laertius X.122–135, using the collection’s C. D. Yonge translation; and Sextus Empiricus, Outlines of Pyrrhonism I.1–30, Julia Annas and Jonathan Barnes translation, Outlines of Scepticism, Cambridge 2000. ## Modules and assignments

**1. Historical orientation and method, 10 hours.** Revisit Apology 20c–23c and Meno 80d–86c. Consult OpenStax 4.2 “Classical Philosophy” for Greek context ([<u>https://openstax.org/books/introduction-philosophy/pages/4-2-classical-philosophy</u>](https://openstax.org/books/introduction-philosophy/pages/4-2-classical-philosophy)). Create a one-page chronology separating approximate author dates, dramatic settings, and composition where the sources actually establish them; mark unsupported dates uncertain. Use OpenStax for broad orientation, and verify exact quotations in a primary text. Its incidental description of the opening sentence of Aristotle’s Metaphysics is inaccurate; the primary opening is I.1, 980a21 ([<u>https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/metaphysics.1.i.html</u>](https://classics.mit.edu/Aristotle/metaphysics.1.i.html)). This quotation check is only needed if you use that remark, not an extra assigned book. Write 400 words on why a dialogue character’s assertion is not automatically Plato’s doctrine.

**2. The challenge of justice, 16 hours.** Republic I and II through 367e. Map Thrasymachus’ and Glaucon’s challenges separately. Output: a 600-word reconstruction of the strongest challenge and a written explanation of what a successful answer would need to show.

**3. Plato’s answer and its limits, 18 hours.** Republic IV 427d–445e and IX 577c–580c. Reconstruct the city–soul comparison and the relation between psychic order and justice. PC activity: identify every premise needed to move from an account of justice to the claim that being just benefits its possessor. This supplies the answer omitted from the old reading list.

**4. Knowledge and the soul, 18 hours.** Republic VI 509d–511e, VII 514a–521b; Phaedo 64a–69e, 72e–84b. Distinguish images from arguments. Write 700 words evaluating either recollection or affinity, including what the selected argument is intended to establish.

**5. Aristotle on explanation, 16 hours.** Categories 1–5 and Physics II.1–3, II.8. Classify the explanatory questions asked about one natural object and one artifact. Write a 500-word comparison of substance and attribute, then assess one argument about teleology without importing modern biological assumptions silently.

**6. Aristotle and Hellenistic alternatives, 14 hours.** De Anima II.1–3; Epicurus 122–135; Sextus I.1–30. Explain why “soul” need not mean an immaterial ghost. Compare Epicurus on death with Socrates’ argument in Apology, and identify what suspension of judgment is meant to do. These are bounded encounters, not complete courses in Hellenistic philosophy.

**7. Essay and revision, 20 hours.** Write 1,500–1,800 words on one question: “Does Republic IV answer Glaucon’s challenge?”; “Does the affinity argument support immortality?”; or “Does Aristotle’s soul account avoid the problems of the model it opposes?” Include outline, draft, feedback, revision, and memo.

**8. Independent assessment and later recall, 8 hours.** Interpret an unseen short passage from the assigned corpus with the source visible; explain one comparison without notes; answer a new objection to the essay; complete a delayed reconstruction in the next course. Ready means accurate textual work and relevant reasoning on new material, not memorizing every author’s dates.

If the city–soul argument or a technical term remains unclear, annotate the exact passage and compare two interpretations before retesting. Optional extensions include Presocratics, Stoicism, and the complete Republic; they are not hidden essentials.

# Course 4 Medieval philosophy and transmission

## Scope and core readings

100 hours; prerequisites Courses 1 and 3. This course studies selected arguments about time, intellect, revelation, causation, and existence across interconnected Latin, Arabic, and Jewish philosophical settings. It is a bridge with depth in a few problems, not a comprehensive medieval survey. Avoid narrating Islamic and Jewish thinkers merely as carriers of Greek ideas to Europe.

Core: Augustine, Confessions XI.14–31, Henry Chadwick translation, Oxford 1991; Anselm, Proslogion 2–4, Gaunilo’s complete On Behalf of the Fool, and Anselm’s reply sections 1–4, Thomas Williams translation, Proslogion with the Replies of Gaunilo and Anselm, Hackett 2001; Avicenna, “On the Soul,” complete excerpt in Muhammad Ali Khalidi, editor, Medieval Islamic Philosophical Writings, Cambridge 2005, pp. 27–58; al-Ghazali, The Rescuer from Error, the complete anthology excerpt, pp. 59–98, in the same anthology; Maimonides, Guide of the Perplexed I.50–60, Shlomo Pines translation, Chicago 1963; Aquinas, Summa Theologiae I, question 2, articles 1–3, and question 1, article 8, English Dominican Province translation. Read the editor’s introductions to the assigned Avicenna and al-Ghazali excerpts. The Avicenna and al-Ghazali assignments are complete anthology excerpts, not complete original treatises; the Avicenna selection comes from the Book of Salvation. Check the quoted page within the actual borrowed copy before citing it.

Outcomes: reconstruct distinct arguments for existence and distinguish their premises; explain the flying-person thought experiment without treating it as a complete proof of Cartesian dualism; interpret negative theology; compare reason/revelation relationships with contextual care; use a translation responsibly.

## Modules and assignments

**1. Context and transmission, 10 hours.** Read the anthology’s general introduction and source introductions. Map the languages, institutions, translation movements, and intellectual settings actually supported by these introductions. Label the scope as the Arabic/Islamic context supplied by this source; mark missing Latin/Jewish contextual details as questions rather than filling them from an AI’s recollection. Output: a sourced 500-word explanation of why a simple “faith versus reason” storyline is inadequate.

**2. Time and experience, 12 hours.** Augustine XI.14–31. Reconstruct the puzzle of past, present, and future. PC activity: distinguish a claim about temporal reality from a claim about measuring time. Write a 400-word objection and possible response.

**3. Anselm and Gaunilo, 14 hours.** Assigned Proslogion and replies. Produce parallel reconstructions, separating the original argument, parody, and response. Explain whether the parody targets the strongest reading. Do not simply label an argument “ontological” and assume that settles its validity.

**4. Avicenna on self and soul, 14 hours.** Assigned On the Soul excerpt. Describe the thought experiment’s assumptions, conclusion, and limits; compare Aristotle and Descartes cautiously. Write a 600-word interpretive exercise grounded in the actual translation.

**5. Al-Ghazali and philosophical inquiry, 12 hours.** Assigned anthology excerpt of Rescuer. Identify distinctions among mathematical, logical, physical, metaphysical, political, and ethical investigations where the text makes them. Write a 500-word analysis of an argument against philosophers, distinguishing autobiography, classification, and demonstration.

**6. Maimonides and Aquinas, 14 hours.** Guide I.50–60; Summa I q.1 a.8 and q.2 aa.1–3. Compare negative predication with one of Aquinas’s arguments. Map the objection–response form of a Summa article. Do not assimilate all theological arguments to one generic proof.

**7. Essay and revision, 18 hours.** Write 1,500–1,800 words: “What, exactly, does Avicenna’s thought experiment establish?”; “Does Gaunilo answer Anselm?”; or “Can negative theology make informative claims?” Include one competing interpretation and a justified textual decision.

**8. Assessment and delayed check, 6 hours.** Analyze a fresh assigned passage, compare two methods of argument, explain an unanticipated objection, and later reconstruct the essay’s key inference. Remediate contextual misattributions by returning to the source introduction and passage before repeating the task.

Optional extension: Averroes’ Decisive Treatise or further work in Islamic philosophy, Jewish philosophy, scholasticism, or medieval women’s thought. These require a separate reading plan and are not necessary for this bounded course.

# Course 5 Early modern philosophy

## Scope and core readings

120 hours; prerequisites Courses 1 and 3, with Course 4 recommended in sequence. Study arguments about knowledge, causation, substance, and mind in their seventeenth- and eighteenth-century contexts. “Rationalist” and “empiricist” are useful retrospective labels with limits.

Read Descartes, Meditations I–VI, in Cottingham/Stoothoff/Murdoch volume II; Elisabeth–Descartes correspondence dated 6 May, 21 May, 10 June, 28 June, and 1 July 1643, Lisa Shapiro, The Correspondence between Princess Elisabeth of Bohemia and René Descartes, Chicago 2007; Spinoza, Ethics I, definitions and axioms, propositions 1–15 with demonstrations, and Appendix, Edwin Curley, A Spinoza Reader, Princeton 1994; Leibniz, Monadology §§1–39 and §§78–90, Ariew and Garber, Philosophical Essays, Hackett 1989; Locke, Essay Concerning Human Understanding I.ii.1–9 and II.viii.1–26, Peter Nidditch edition, Oxford 1975; Berkeley, Principles of Human Knowledge, Introduction §§1–25 and Part I §§1–33, Dancy edition, Oxford 1998; Hume, Enquiry Concerning Human Understanding sections II–VII and XII, Tom Beauchamp edition, Oxford 1999. Use stable locators, not a modernized aid’s page numbers, when quoting the scholarly text.

Outcomes: reconstruct Descartes’ main transitions and the circle worry; explain Elisabeth’s interaction objection; compare rival substance and ideas accounts; evaluate Berkeley’s idealism and Hume’s induction argument; distinguish historical interpretation from a contemporary repair.

## Modules and assignments

**1. Doubt, the thinker, and representation, 12 hours.** Reread Meditations I–II and read III. Trace what each stage has established. Output: an annotated argument map and 500-word account of one contested inference.

**2. God, error, and the world, 16 hours.** Meditations IV–VI. Write 700 words explaining the strongest version of the circle objection and a possible response. A tutorial asks what depends on clear and distinct perception and what depends on divine reliability.

**3. Embodiment and interaction, 12 hours.** Read the five dated letters. Create a correspondence map showing objection, reply, reformulation, and unresolved issue. Output: 500 words on whether Descartes answers Elisabeth’s question rather than a different question.

**4. Substance and explanation, 16 hours.** Spinoza’s assigned passages and Leibniz’s Monadology. Reconstruct one argument for a substantive metaphysical claim from each. Compare explanatory aims before evaluating their conclusions.

**5. Ideas and qualities, 14 hours.** Locke and Berkeley. Use an ordinary object to distinguish primary/secondary qualities and ideas/objects. Write 600 words assessing a premise in Berkeley’s argument, with a charitable Locke comparison.

**6. Hume’s challenge, 20 hours.** Enquiry II–VII and XII. Separate the skeptical argument about induction, the “skeptical solution,” the account of necessary connection, and mitigated skepticism. PC activity: map where a proposed defense of induction risks circularity.

**7. Essay and revision, 22 hours.** Write 1,800–2,000 words on the Cartesian circle, Elisabeth’s objection, Berkeley’s anti-abstraction argument, or Hume’s account of induction. Use at least two exact passages and one checked scholarly interpretation, selected through the reference protocol below.

**8. Assessment and delayed check, 8 hours.** Independent passage interpretation plus a comparison not rehearsed in the essay, followed by a brief defense and delayed reconstruction. If comparison becomes a list of slogans, return to a shared question and state each author’s actual premise and conclusion.

Optional enrichment: the remainder of the Ethics or Monadology and lectures on the period. Do not let optional whole-book ambitions displace revision.

# Course 6 Ethics

## Scope and core readings

120 hours; prerequisite Course 1. This course develops normative argument, a limited introduction to metaethics, and careful application. It does not claim to exhaust moral philosophy.

Read Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics I.1–10, II.1–9, and VI.1–5, Terence Irwin, third edition, Hackett 2019; Kant, Groundwork, sections I–II, Ak 4:393–445, Mary Gregor and Jens Timmermann, revised edition, Cambridge 2012; Mill, Utilitarianism chapters II and IV, George Sher edition, second edition, Hackett 2001; Hume, Treatise 3.1.1, Selby-Bigge/Nidditch edition, Oxford 1978; A. J. ## Modules and assignments

**1. Moral questions and reasoning, 10 hours.** Revisit Singer’s argument and read OpenStax 8.1–8.3 for vocabulary. Separate a factual dispute, a normative claim, and a metaethical question. Write a 400-word case analysis with assumptions explicit.

**2. Character and flourishing, 18 hours.** Aristotle I.1–10, II.1–9, VI.1–5. Reconstruct the function argument and explain practical wisdom. Create an example in which applying the mean requires judgment rather than arithmetic.

**3. Moral cultivation across contexts, 12 hours.** Mengzi 2A6 and 6A1–10. Explain the sprouts analogy and one rival interpretation of “human nature.” Write 600 words comparing an aspect of moral development with Aristotle while stating a limit of the comparison.

**4. Duty and autonomy, 18 hours.** Groundwork I–II. Reconstruct a universal-law test and distinguish treating a person as a means from merely as a means. Test the argument on a fresh maxim before reading a commentator’s response.

**6. Moral language and applied argument, 16 hours.** Hume 3.1.1, Ayer VI, Thomson’s article. Explain the is/ought passage and emotivism without assuming Hume and Ayer have the same project. Analyze one Thomson thought experiment, distinguishing the conclusion it supports from a broader conclusion it does not establish.

**7. Essay and revision, 22 hours.** Write 1,800–2,000 words defending a bounded verdict on one core argument or applying two theories to a precisely specified case. Prompts: “Can Kant’s universal-law test identify the relevant wrong?”; “Does Mill’s proof justify utility as the ultimate end?”; “Does Thomson’s argument establish the conclusion commonly attributed to it?”

**8. Assessment and delayed check, 8 hours.** Independently distinguish three theory types, interpret a new passage, and assess a changed case. Disagreement with a moral conclusion is not a grading error; unsupported interpretation or inference is. Remedy a theory-confusion by reconstructing its premises before revisiting applications.

Optional extension: contractualism, care ethics, error theory, moral realism, animal ethics, or environmental ethics. Social Philosophy below offers a complete further route rather than a vague instruction to “read more.”

# Course 7 Knowledge and reality

## Scope and core readings

140 hours; prerequisites Course 1, the semantic and symbolization parts of Course 2, and Course 5. The combined course deliberately concentrates on knowledge, testimony, personal identity, and responsibility. It does not claim full coverage of epistemology and metaphysics. Modal ontology, grounding, time, and advanced skepticism are extension areas.

Read Gettier’s complete 1963 article again; Alvin Goldman, “What Is Justified Belief?”, in Pappas, Justification and Knowledge, 1979, pp. 1–23; Hume, Enquiry section X, Part I; Thomas Reid, An Inquiry into the Human Mind on the Principles of Common Sense, chapter VI, section 24, Derek Brookes critical edition, Edinburgh 1997; Miranda Fricker, Epistemic Injustice, Oxford 2007, chapter 1; Locke, Essay II.xxvii, complete; the Buddhist Anattalakkhaṇa Sutta, SN 22.59, complete, Bhikkhu Sujato translation, SuttaCentral ([<u>https://suttacentral.net/sn22.59/en/sujato</u>](https://suttacentral.net/sn22.59/en/sujato)); Derek Parfit, “Personal Identity,” Philosophical Review 80, 1971, pp. 3–27; Harry Frankfurt, “Alternate Possibilities and Moral Responsibility,” 1969, pp. 829–839; Peter van Inwagen, “The Incompatibility of Free Will and Determinism,” 1975, pp. 185–199. Required context is the Internet Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry “Buddha,” sections 1.b “Sources,” 3.b “The Four Noble Truths or Realities,” 3.c “Ontology of Suffering: the Five Aggregates,” and 3.d “Arguments for the Doctrine of Non-self” ([<u>https://iep.utm.edu/buddha/</u>](https://iep.utm.edu/buddha/)). Read these bounded sections within Module 5, not the whole entry; one early Buddhist discourse in a Theravāda/Pāli canonical collection is not a complete account of Buddhist or Indian philosophy or a direct stenographic transcript.

Outcomes: distinguish knowledge from true belief and evaluate a reliabilist proposal; compare competing testimony principles; explain an epistemic injustice; distinguish psychological continuity, identity, and the Buddhist analysis without conflating them; reconstruct a free-will argument; defend a focused position.

## Modules and assignments

**1. Knowledge and justification, 16 hours.** Gettier and Goldman. Create a map distinguishing an analysis of knowledge from an account of justified belief. Write 600 words evaluating one reliability condition using a new case.

**2. Testimony and trust, 16 hours.** Hume X Part I and Reid VI.24. Explain the disagreement over the grounds of trusting others, avoiding the claim that all testimony is about miracles. PC activity: vary source reliability, dependence among witnesses, and background evidence in a case matrix. State what the comparison does not decide.

**3. Epistemic injustice, 14 hours.** Fricker chapter 1. Reconstruct testimonial injustice and compare it with ordinary error or deception. Write a 600-word case analysis with a counterexample to an overbroad definition. Hermeneutical injustice is an optional later topic, not assessed from an unread chapter.

**4. Persons and continuity, 18 hours.** Locke II.xxvii and Parfit’s article. Map body, memory, psychological continuity, and numerical identity. Explain the role of fission without assuming that imaginative possibility alone settles metaphysical possibility.

**5. A different inquiry into self, 16 hours.** SN 22.59 and the four specified IEP “Buddha” sections. Identify the five aggregates, the argument’s structure, and the practical purpose of the discourse. Mark one claim directly supported by Sujato’s SN 22.59 and one stronger interpretive claim made by the IEP guide. The guide is a scholarly interpretation, not a neutral answer key; disagreement with it is not automatically a textual error. Write 700 words comparing a narrowly specified question with Locke while explaining why the projects are not interchangeable. Do not recast “not-self” automatically as a Western bundle theory.

**6. Freedom and responsibility, 22 hours.** Frankfurt and van Inwagen. Reconstruct the principle of alternate possibilities, a Frankfurt case, and the consequence argument. Diagram which assumptions differ, then write 600 words on whether an objection attacks determinism, alternate possibilities, or a transfer principle.

**7. Essay and revision, 28 hours.** Write 1,800–2,200 words on testimony, reliabilism, identity, or responsibility. Include one verified secondary interpretation and one serious rival argument. State clearly whether your paper is interpretive, conceptual, or normative.

**8. Assessment and delayed check, 10 hours.** Interpret a new assigned passage, construct a fresh counterexample, and answer an unscripted objection. If two positions are being conflated, make a question–premise–conclusion comparison before retesting. Retention is checked in science or the selected area course.

# Course 8 Political philosophy

## Scope and core readings

140 hours; prerequisites Courses 1 and 6. Study legitimacy, liberty, distribution, gender, race, and the limitations of idealized social contracts. The course includes conflicting traditions and distinguishes interpreting a theory from endorsing its politics.

Read Hobbes, Leviathan chapters 13–15 and 17, Richard Tuck edition, Cambridge 1996; Locke, Second Treatise chapter II §§4–15, chapter V §§25–51, and chapter VIII §§95–99, Peter Laslett edition, Cambridge 1988; Mill, On Liberty chapters I–II, any complete reliable English edition; Mary Wollstonecraft, A Vindication of the Rights of Woman chapter II, Miriam Brody edition, Penguin 1992, or a complete public-domain text; W. E. B. Du Bois, The Souls of Black Folk, chapter I “Of Our Spiritual Strivings,” 1903; Rawls, A Theory of Justice, revised edition, Harvard 1999, §§3–4, 11–13, and 24–26; Nozick, Anarchy, State, and Utopia, Basic Books 1974, pp. 150–164, from the entitlement principles through the Wilt Chamberlain discussion; Charles Mills, The Racial Contract, Cornell 1997, introduction and chapter 1, pp. 1–40. Read the works in context; a critique of a tradition is not just an extra objection after its “real” theory.

Outcomes: explain legitimacy and consent arguments; apply and criticize the harm principle; reconstruct the original position and entitlement objections; interpret gender and race critiques as philosophical arguments; distinguish ideal principles from historical exclusion and defend an institutional principle with limits.

## Modules and assignments

**1. Authority and method, 12 hours.** Hobbes 13–15. Separate prudential motivation, moral obligation, and institutional authority. Write 500 words on what the state-of-nature argument assumes.

**2. Consent and property, 18 hours.** Hobbes 17; Locke’s assigned passages. Map the labor/property argument and the consent claim. Compare a specific premise rather than summarizing “Hobbes versus Locke” in general.

**3. Liberty and expression, 16 hours.** Mill I–II. Apply the harm principle to two carefully described cases, then explain the argument for hearing contrary opinions. PC activity: distinguish a claim’s truth, the right to express it, and the justification for a particular restriction.

**4. Equal standing and education, 18 hours.** Wollstonecraft II and Du Bois I. Write 700 words reconstructing one author’s account of a barrier to equal agency or recognition. Discuss interpretive risks in treating autobiographical, literary, and historical forms as mere illustrations.

**5. Fairness and entitlement, 20 hours.** Rawls and Nozick. Reconstruct the original-position argument and the Wilt Chamberlain objection; identify where principles are selected and where institutions are evaluated. Build a small numerical distribution example, explaining why arithmetic alone cannot decide justice.

**6. Contract and exclusion, 20 hours.** Mills introduction and chapter 1. Compare a descriptive claim about actual institutions with a normative claim about legitimate ones. Write 700 words on whether Mills revises, rejects, or repurposes the social-contract framework, with evidence.

**7. Essay and revision, 28 hours.** Write 1,800–2,200 words on a bounded issue: a consent argument, the limits of Mill’s speech argument, Rawls versus Nozick on a specified distribution, or Mills’s challenge to ideal theory. Represent the strongest relevant opposing view before defending a conclusion.

**8. Assessment and delayed check, 8 hours.** Analyze a new political case, identify which factual questions need evidence, and defend one principle under a changed circumstance. Remediate a factual/normative conflation before reassessing. Political agreement with a tutor is never a readiness criterion.

Optional extension: Rousseau’s Social Contract I–II, feminist critiques of the family, democratic theory, migration, and global justice. The Social Philosophy area option develops several of these themes in depth.

# Course 9 Kant and selected nineteenth century responses

## Scope and core readings

140 hours; prerequisites Courses 5 and 6, with Course 7 useful. This course studies Kant’s answer to a problem inherited from Hume, followed by three selected responses concerning recognition, social life, and genealogy. It is intentionally not a complete history of nineteenth-century philosophy.

Read Kant, Prolegomena to Any Future Metaphysics, Preamble §§1–5, §§6–30, and §§57–60, Gary Hatfield translation, revised edition, Cambridge 2004; Critique of Pure Reason, B Preface Bxvi–Bxxii and Second Analogy A189/B232–A211/B256, Guyer and Wood translation, Cambridge 1998. Read Hegel, Phenomenology of Spirit §§178–196, A. V. Miller translation, Oxford 1977, with the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry “Georg Wilhelm Friedrich Hegel,” section 3.1.1 “Phenomenology of Spirit,” only the passage from the paragraph beginning “Something of Hegel’s phenomenological method” through the paragraph ending “But this is only worked out in the text gradually,” inclusive ([<u>https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hegel/</u>](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/hegel/)); Marx, “Estranged Labour” in the 1844 manuscripts, complete section, and “Theses on Feuerbach,” all eleven theses, in Early Writings, Rodney Livingstone and Gregor Benton translation, Penguin 1975; Nietzsche, On the Genealogy of Morality, First Essay §§1–17, Carol Diethe translation, edited Keith Ansell-Pearson, revised student edition, Cambridge 2007.

Outcomes: distinguish analytic/synthetic and a priori/a posteriori; explain the problem of synthetic a priori cognition; reconstruct a bounded argument in the Second Analogy; interpret recognition, alienation, and genealogy in context; compare a selected post-Kantian project without flattening methodological differences.

## Modules and assignments

**1. Kant’s question and vocabulary, 12 hours.** Prolegomena §§1–5 and B Preface selection. Build a concept sheet with examples and nonexamples. Explain why “a priori” does not simply mean “innate” or “obvious.”

**2. Space and experience, 18 hours.** Prolegomena §§6–13. Reconstruct one argument concerning intuition; distinguish transcendental conditions from psychological reports. Write 600 words testing a possible objection.

**3. Objectivity and causation, 26 hours.** Prolegomena §§14–30; Second Analogy A189/B232–A211/B256. Distinguish a succession of perceptions from perception of an objective succession. Create a paired-case diagram, then explain which premise establishes a necessary rule. The exact passage supports the course’s answer-to-Hume outcome.

**4. Limits of metaphysics, 12 hours.** Prolegomena §§57–60. Explain the distinction between the legitimate use of concepts and overextension. Write 500 words on what the critical project permits and limits; do not infer that Kant simply rejects all metaphysics.

**5. Recognition, 18 hours.** Hegel §§178–196 and the bounded SEP section 3.1.1 passage specified above. Trace dependence and reversal in lordship and bondage. Write a 700-word interpretation distinguishing the argument’s place in the work from a later political application.

**6. Alienation and genealogy, 22 hours.** Marx’s two texts and Nietzsche First Essay. Write two 500-word reconstructions, then compare what counts as a philosophical explanation. A genealogy is not automatically a refutation merely because it explains a belief’s origin.

**7. Essay and revision, 24 hours.** Write 1,800–2,200 words on Kant’s Second Analogy, Hegelian recognition, Marxian alienation, or a specified Nietzschean genealogical claim. Use the exact primary passage and a checked specialist commentary; acknowledge a competing interpretation.

**8. Assessment and delayed check, 8 hours.** Interpret an unfamiliar short passage from the set and compare two methods of inquiry. If the course becomes terminology recall, return to a single argument and state what would follow if its premise were false.

Optional extensions include Kierkegaard, Schopenhauer, later German idealism, and the rest of the Critique. The area option on pragmatism and phenomenology provides a specified later route.

# Course 10 Philosophy of science

## Scope and core readings

120 hours; prerequisites Course 1 and the induction module of Course 5; Course 2’s basic logic is helpful but full first-order proof proficiency is not required. The course combines classic arguments with the social conditions of inquiry and a real scientific case. No laboratory degree is assumed.

Read Hume, Enquiry IV–V again; Karl Popper, Conjectures and Refutations, chapter 1 “Science: Conjectures and Refutations,” Routledge 1963, whole chapter; Carl Hempel, Philosophy of Natural Science, Prentice-Hall 1966, chapters 2 and 5; Thomas Kuhn, The Structure of Scientific Revolutions, fourth edition, Chicago 2012, chapters II, VI, VII, and IX; Bas van Fraassen, The Scientific Image, Oxford 1980, chapter 2, sections 1–3; Helen Longino, Science as Social Knowledge, Princeton 1990, chapter 4 “Values and Objectivity.” A verified scientific case packet comprises an official plain-language summary, one primary research abstract or methods/results excerpt, and an explicit description of the uncertainty involved. The default packet uses the IPCC Sixth Assessment Synthesis Report, Summary for Policymakers, 2023, Introduction including footnote 4, sections A.1 and A.2, and Figure SPM.1 ([<u>https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/summary-for-policymakers/</u>](https://www.ipcc.ch/report/ar6/syr/summary-for-policymakers/)). Pair it with only the publicly available abstract of Nathan Gillett and colleagues, “Constraining human contributions to observed warming since the pre-industrial period,” Nature Climate Change 11 (2021), pp. 207–212, [<u>https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-020-00965-9</u>](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41558-020-00965-9) . The abstract is a bounded original-study excerpt, not the whole paper. Compare the kinds of claims, stated method, scope, and uncertainty with the synthesis; do not claim this one article directly underlies Figure SPM.1 without tracing that relationship. The purpose is philosophical analysis of evidence, confidence, and institutions, not independent climate-science adjudication.

Outcomes: distinguish deduction, induction, and confirmation; evaluate falsifiability as a criterion; explain a model of scientific explanation; compare realism with empirical adequacy; analyze values and criticism in inquiry; produce a source-grounded case analysis without pretending philosophical reasoning replaces scientific expertise.

## Modules and assignments

**1. Induction and confirmation, 14 hours.** Hume IV–V and Hempel chapter 2. Reconstruct the induction problem and a hypothetico-deductive testing scheme. PC activity: distinguish P(evidence given hypothesis) from P(hypothesis given evidence) using a small invented frequency table. A six-hour probability bridge is available if needed and is additional to the course estimate.

**2. Testing and falsifiability, 14 hours.** Popper chapter 1. Explain the role of risky predictions and auxiliary assumptions. Write 600 words on why one apparently contrary observation does not automatically dictate abandonment of a theory.

**3. Scientific explanation, 14 hours.** Hempel chapter 5. Reconstruct an explanation schema and test it with an original case. Identify an explanatory direction or relevance problem, then explain whether it defeats the whole approach or a specific formulation.

**4. Change in science, 18 hours.** Kuhn II, VI, VII, IX. Distinguish normal science, anomaly, crisis, and revolution without turning them into a simple inevitable cycle. Write 700 words on a selected claim about theory change.

**5. Realism and empirical adequacy, 16 hours.** Van Fraassen chapter 2 §§1–3. State what accepting a theory commits one to under each view. Compare an inference to the best explanation with an empiricist reply using a carefully bounded example.

**6. Objectivity and scientific communities, 14 hours.** Longino chapter 4 and the complete bounded case packet. Read the SPM’s footnote 4 before using confidence and likelihood terms. In Gillett’s abstract, identify the stated method and claim, then state what cannot be evaluated without the full paper. Identify evidence, assumptions, value judgments, uncertainty language, and routes for criticism. Do not infer that values make every conclusion arbitrary or that consensus makes criticism unnecessary.

**7. Case paper and revision, 22 hours.** Write 1,800–2,200 words on one philosophical issue in the packet, or on an equivalently documented scientific case chosen from public primary sources. Use at least one course argument and distinguish what the scientific source actually shows from your interpretation. A case packet for an alternative must be bounded and accessible before the paper begins.

**8. Assessment and delayed check, 8 hours.** Analyze a new claim about testing or uncertainty, reconstruct one argument without notes, and defend the case paper under a changed assumption. If source comprehension is weak, consult a domain introduction rather than invent an answer; the tutor must flag unresolved scientific facts.

Optional extension: Kuhn chapter X on changes of world view, Bayesian epistemology, philosophy of biology or physics, feminist philosophy of science, causal inference, and replication. These are specializations, not competencies conferred by finishing this introduction.

# Course 11 Sustained study of a global tradition

Choose one complete option. The recommended route is Classical Chinese Philosophy because it develops a conversation already begun in ethics and offers contrasting methods of philosophical inquiry. Africana Philosophy is an equally legitimate alternative, especially after political philosophy. Neither option claims to represent every tradition outside Europe. Indian thought is encountered in Course 7 and Islamic/Jewish thought in Course 4; further sustained study remains an honest extension opportunity.

## Option A Classical Chinese philosophy

140 hours; prerequisites Courses 1 and 6. The default complete-text route uses Slingerland, Confucius Analects With Selections from Traditional Commentaries (Hackett 2003); Van Norden, Mengzi With Selections from Traditional Commentaries (Hackett 2008); Ian Johnston, The Mozi A Complete Translation (Chinese University Press and Columbia University Press 2010); Ziporyn, Zhuangzi The Essential Writings (Hackett 2009); and Hutton, Xunzi The Complete Text (Princeton 2014). Borrow one or two at a time as the course proceeds. The 2023 Readings in Classical Chinese Philosophy anthology is a useful alternative only where its actual contents cover every assigned passage; its inclusion of an author does not guarantee the whole range.

Core passages: Analects Books 1–4, 7, 12, 13, and 15, Edward Slingerland translation; Mengzi 1A1, 1A7, 2A2, 2A6, 3A5, 4A17, 6A1–10, 6B2, 7A1, 7A15, and 7B24, Bryan Van Norden translation; Mozi chapter 16, “Universal Love III” in Johnston (also rendered “Impartial Caring III”), complete; Zhuangzi chapters 1–3 and 6, Brook Ziporyn translation in The Essential Writings, Hackett 2009, or another identified complete scholarly translation; Xunzi chapters 1 and 23, Eric Hutton translation, The Complete Text, Princeton 2014. The finite common context is SEP “Chinese Ethics,” section 1 “Characteristics of Chinese Ethics: Practical Focus and Closeness to Pre-theoretical Experience” ([<u>https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/</u>](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/ethics-chinese/)), plus SEP “Mencius,” section 1 “Life and Confucian Background,” only its opening paragraphs through the sentence listing five Analects themes and ending “ethical cultivation” ([<u>https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mencius/</u>](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/mencius/)). Whole translator introductions are optional. Consult passage notes where needed while reading each primary text; these belong to that author’s module. Stable book/chapter locators control the assignment; transliterations and English renderings vary.

Outcomes: reconstruct debates over cultivation, partiality, order, and knowledge; explain key terms in context without equating them automatically with familiar English categories; compare thinkers within the tradition; evaluate the philosophical effect of a translation choice; produce a narrow, source-grounded interpretive argument. This English-language course does not establish Chinese philological competence. A translation-sensitive judgment must compare two identified renderings of a short passage or use a translator’s explicit discussion; it evaluates the philosophical consequences of those renderings, not independently certifies fidelity to the Chinese. Use a lawful short comparison or commentary rather than buying a second complete book only for the exercise.

**1. Context and reading practice, 10 hours.** Read the two bounded SEP context selections above. Create a provisional Warring States context map and begin a glossary of ren, li, yi, de, dao, and xing. Add a primary passage and alternative rendering as each term is encountered in Modules 2–6; the ten-hour opening module does not require mastering all six in advance. Do not treat a glossary entry as a final definition.

**2. Analects, 22 hours.** Read the specified books. Choose four connected sayings and reconstruct an account of cultivation or governing. Write 700 words explaining how an aphoristic text can support a philosophical interpretation without forcing it into an imaginary continuous treatise.

**3. Mengzi, 20 hours.** Read the assigned passages. Examine the sprouts, human nature, and the extension of concern. Write 700 words on one argument and a textual difficulty. Revisit Aristotle only after representing Mengzi in his own terms.

**4. Mohist challenge, 16 hours.** Read Mozi 16. Reconstruct the argument for impartial care and a likely Confucian objection. PC activity: distinguish impartial concern, identical treatment, and absence of personal relationships. Identify which claim the text actually requires.

**5. Zhuangzi, 24 hours.** Read chapters 1–3 and 6. Trace the role of story, perspective, skill, and transformation. Compare two interpretations of one passage and explain their evidence. Do not assume that a story is either a literal doctrine or philosophically empty literature.

**6. Xunzi, 18 hours.** Read chapters 1 and 23. Reconstruct a disagreement with Mengzi, including the work done by “nature,” deliberate effort, ritual, and learning. Write 800 words that distinguishes disagreement from a translation-generated appearance of disagreement.

**7. Essay and revision, 22 hours.** Write 2,000–2,400 words: “Is the Mengzi–Xunzi disagreement primarily about motivation, development, or the meaning of nature?”; “Does Mozi’s argument require abandoning special relationships?”; or “What does a selected Zhuangzi passage justify about knowledge or perspective?” Include one specialist secondary interpretation and a translation note.

**8. Assessment and delayed check, 8 hours.** Independently interpret a fresh assigned passage, compare two thinkers without using a European thinker as the default measure, and defend a translation-sensitive claim. A subsequent cross-tradition comparison must state both a productive connection and a limit.

Readiness depends on accurate intra-tradition interpretation, evidence, and awareness of comparison limits. If the course becomes a list of equivalents such as “ren equals virtue,” return to contrasting passages and revise the glossary. Optional extensions include the Laozi, Han Feizi, Neo-Confucianism, and modern Chinese thought; completion does not imply coverage of them.

## Option B Africana philosophy

140 hours; prerequisites Courses 1 and 8. This is a focused course on selfhood, freedom, personhood, and philosophical method in selected African and African-diasporic texts. “Africana” identifies an interconnected field, not one unified doctrine or an undifferentiated continent.

Core: Du Bois, The Souls of Black Folk, chapters I and III; Frantz Fanon, Black Skin, White Masks, chapter 5, “The Lived Experience of the Black Man,” Richard Philcox translation, Grove 2008; Kwasi Wiredu, “How Not to Compare African Thought with Western Thought,” in Richard A. Wright, editor, African Philosophy An Introduction, third edition, University Press of America 1984, whole essay; Ifeanyi Menkiti, “Person and Community in African Traditional Thought,” in Richard Wright, editor, African Philosophy An Introduction, third edition, 1984, whole essay beginning p. 171; Kwame Gyekye, “Person and Community in African Thought,” in Person and Community Ghanaian Philosophical Studies I, 1992, pp. 101–122; Charles Mills, The Racial Contract, chapter 2, Cornell 1997. Required context is Du Bois’s short “The Forethought” to Souls, plus the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy entry “Frantz Fanon”: opening biographical paragraphs before the Contents, then section 1 “The Problem of Blackness” through the paragraph beginning “In the end, Fanon is a unique thinker,” inclusive ([<u>https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/frantz-fanon/</u>](https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/frantz-fanon/)). Do not assign the remainder of the entry or whole collection introductions as hidden extra reading. Wiredu and Menkiti are available in the same 1984 anthology; borrowing that edition avoids mixing pagination from later reprints.

Outcomes: distinguish phenomenological, historical, conceptual, and normative claims; reconstruct the person/community debate; interpret racialization and double consciousness in context; assess methodological risks in cross-cultural comparison; defend a bounded argument without treating an author as spokesperson for all African thought.

**1. Context and method, 14 hours.** Read Wiredu’s essay, Du Bois’s “The Forethought,” and the bounded Fanon context reading named above. Write 600 words on a bad comparison and how to repair it. Identify the language, region, historical setting, and kind of claim relevant to each author.

**2. Du Bois on self and social life, 20 hours.** Souls I and III. Interpret double consciousness and the education/politics dispute. Map the roles of narrative, history, and argument. Output: a 700-word reading with exact textual support.

**3. Fanon on racialized experience, 24 hours.** Read chapter 5 in full, returning to the finite SEP context passage as needed. Distinguish the lived description from the claims about social structures it supports. Compare a narrow point with Du Bois while preserving historical differences.

**4. Personhood and community, 20 hours.** Menkiti and Gyekye in full. Reconstruct their positions and identify where personhood is descriptive, normative, acquired, or presupposed. PC activity: test a single case under each interpretation rather than attach blanket “individualist” and “collectivist” labels.

**5. Institutions and critique, 16 hours.** Mills chapter 2, with Course 8’s chapter 1 available for reference. Write 700 words on an explanatory or normative claim and a serious objection. Separate what the evidence establishes from what remains a philosophical inference.

**6. Comparative-method workshop, 14 hours.** Return to Wiredu. Audit one of your earlier comparisons for conceptual translation, selective evidence, and overgeneralization. Revise the argument rather than just adding a disclaimer.

**7. Essay and revision, 24 hours.** Write 2,000–2,400 words on the Menkiti–Gyekye debate, Du Bois and Fanon on a specified problem, or a precise Mills argument. Include a checked specialist source and a method note explaining the limits of your scope.

**8. Assessment and delayed check, 8 hours.** Interpret an unseen assigned passage and answer a new objection. Readiness requires contextual accuracy and a genuine argument, not recognition of author names. Remedy unsupported generalizations by narrowing the claim and locating direct evidence.

Optional extensions include Hountondji, African feminist philosophy, Akan philosophy, decolonial thought, and philosophy in other African languages. These are substantive further studies, not competencies acquired by association.

# Course 12 A selected philosophical area

Choose one area using the compatibility map near the front of the handbook. The recommended route is Mind and Language, followed by the AI seminar in Course 13. The other area choices are fully specified below; Africana plus Aesthetics or Pragmatism requires an explicitly additional 140-hour prerequisite before an offered advanced seminar. They trade some breadth for a different specialization; no choice is universally required by university philosophy programs. You do not need to decide before reaching this stage.

## Option A Mind and language

140 hours; prerequisites Courses 1, 2’s first-order language/semantics, 5, and 7. This is a bounded combined introduction, not two complete specialist courses.

Core: Frege, “On Sense and Reference,” complete, Max Black translation in Translations from the Philosophical Writings of Gottlob Frege, third edition, Blackwell 1980; Russell, “On Denoting,” Mind 14, 1905, pp. 479–493; Kripke, Naming and Necessity, Harvard 1980, Lecture I; Ryle, The Concept of Mind, 1949, chapter I; Putnam, “The Nature of Mental States,” 1967, complete, reprinted in Mind, Language and Reality, Cambridge 1975, pp. 429–440; Turing, “Computing Machinery and Intelligence,” Mind 59, 1950, pp. 433–460; Searle, “Minds, Brains, and Programs,” Behavioral and Brain Sciences 3, 1980, pp. 417–424, excluding the separate commentaries; Nagel, “What Is It Like to Be a Bat?”, 1974, pp. 435–450; Jackson, “Epiphenomenal Qualia,” 1982, pp. 127–136. The specified English Frege translation must be obtained; the collected German original is not an English reading substitute.

Outcomes: explain reference/sense and descriptions; assess a naming argument; distinguish behaviorist, identity, and functionalist strategies at introductory level; reconstruct machine-intelligence and consciousness arguments; identify the limits of a thought experiment.

**1. Meaning and reference, 20 hours.** Frege and Russell. Analyze an informative identity and a definite description. Produce a formalization with a prose account of what it clarifies and what it leaves unresolved.

**2. Names and necessity, 16 hours.** Kripke Lecture I. Distinguish rigid designation, epistemic certainty, and necessity. Write 700 words on one argument against a descriptive account; do not assess Lecture III claims that have not been assigned.

**3. Mind and explanation, 18 hours.** Ryle I and Putnam. Compare category-error and functionalist strategies. Construct an original multiple-realization example and explain why it matters.

**4. Intelligence and understanding, 20 hours.** Turing and Searle. Separate an operational test from a theory of consciousness. Reconstruct the Chinese Room and the systems reply as presented in the article. Write a 700-word objection and response.

**5. Experience and physicalism, 20 hours.** Nagel and Jackson. Identify the epistemic and metaphysical premises separately. Compare an ability/knowledge reply from a checked scholarly overview; do not present that overview as an assigned primary Lewis paper.

**6. Integrative workshop, 10 hours.** Select one connection between language, understanding, and consciousness. Produce a 1,000-word debate map with two rival views and one unresolved question. This is preparation for the essay, not an additional final paper.

**7. Essay and revision, 26 hours.** Write 2,000–2,400 words evaluating a single argument from the course. Keep interpretive fidelity separate from your proposed improvement. Use one verified secondary source and a new case.

**8. Assessment and delayed check, 10 hours.** Interpret a new passage, solve a new reference/description problem, and defend the paper against a changed case. If formalism obscures meaning, translate every symbol back into English before retesting.

## Option B Aesthetics and philosophy of art

120 hours; prerequisites Courses 1, 6, and Course 9’s Kant vocabulary. Core: Hume, “Of the Standard of Taste,” complete; Kant, Critique of the Power of Judgment §§1–22, Guyer and Matthews translation, Cambridge 2000; Morris Weitz, “The Role of Theory in Aesthetics,” 1956, pp. 27–35; Arthur Danto, “The Artworld,” 1964, pp. 571–584; Kendall Walton, “Categories of Art,” 1970, pp. 334–367; Plato, Republic X 595a–608b. Use actual accessible artworks whose maker, date, medium, and viewing context can be checked. Reproductions and recordings are imperfect evidence, and their limits should be stated.

Outcomes: reconstruct theories of taste and art; distinguish evaluation from classification; explain the role of categories and context; apply a theory without mistaking an example for proof; defend an aesthetic judgment with reasons.

**1. Art, imitation, and evidence, 12 hours.** Republic X 595a–608b. Analyze the argument against imitation and its target; choose two works for later comparison and record reliable metadata.

**2. Hume on taste, 16 hours.** Whole essay. Reconstruct the standard and the qualified judge. Write 600 words on whether the account is circular or culturally narrow.

**3. Kant on beauty, 22 hours.** §§1–22. Explain the four moments with distinct examples, then test one claim about disinterest or universality. Avoid equating “disinterested” with uninterested.

**4. Defining art, 16 hours.** Weitz and Danto. Compare open-concept and artworld strategies. PC activity: classify a difficult example under both and explain what evidence is needed.

**5. Categories and perception, 16 hours.** Walton. Reinterpret one work under two categories, distinguishing what actually changes in experience from what the theory predicts.

**6. Case essay and revision, 28 hours.** Write 2,000–2,400 words defending a theory-guided judgment or classification of a specified work, addressing a serious rival reading. Include provenance and reproduction limitations.

**7. Assessment and delayed check, 10 hours.** Analyze an unfamiliar work with sufficient contextual information, reconstruct one course argument, and respond to a new objection. Revise if a verdict depends on missing facts. Readiness concerns justified interpretation, not sharing the tutor’s taste.

## Option C Pragmatism phenomenology and existential inquiry

140 hours; prerequisites Courses 1, 5, and 9. This option gives sustained contact with methods that a purely analytic survey can miss. It is a focused comparison of inquiry and lived experience, not a complete history of “continental philosophy.”

Core: William James, Pragmatism, lectures II and VI, Harvard critical edition 1975 or complete 1907 text; John Dewey, “The Reflex Arc Concept in Psychology,” Psychological Review 3, 1896, pp. 357–370; Edmund Husserl, The Idea of Phenomenology, lectures I–II, Lee Hardy translation, Kluwer 1999; Maurice Merleau-Ponty, Phenomenology of Perception, Preface, Donald Landes translation, Routledge 2012; Sartre, Existentialism Is a Humanism, lecture text, Carol Macomber translation, Yale 2007; Simone de Beauvoir, The Ethics of Ambiguity, Part I, Bernard Frechtman translation, 1948; Kierkegaard, Fear and Trembling, Problema I, Alexander Jech translation, Hackett 2024. The collected Hollander anthology does not contain that required Kierkegaard section and cannot serve as its substitute.

Outcomes: explain a pragmatist method and a theory of truth without reducing them to expediency; describe phenomenological method and its limits; distinguish phenomenological description from empirical psychology; evaluate responsibility, freedom, and ambiguity; compare philosophical methods fairly.

**1. Pragmatist method, 18 hours.** James II and VI. Apply the pragmatic maxim to a carefully bounded dispute, then test a common caricature. Write 700 words on what James’s truth claim does and does not assert.

**2. Organism and environment, 14 hours.** Dewey’s article. Map the critique of a stimulus–response sequence and explain its philosophical significance without treating the article as contemporary neuroscience.

**3. Phenomenological reduction, 20 hours.** Husserl I–II. Write a 600-word explanation of the methodological move and a separate question about its justification. Keep description and argument visibly distinct.

**4. Embodied experience, 18 hours.** Merleau-Ponty Preface. Perform a short, safe description of ordinary perception, then distinguish your observation from claims attributed to the text. No simulation establishes a universal psychological law.

**5. Freedom and ambiguity, 22 hours.** Sartre’s lecture and Beauvoir Part I. Compare a precise claim about freedom, situation, or responsibility, with textual evidence and an objection.

**6. Faith and the ethical, 16 hours.** Kierkegaard Problema I and its editorial context. Explain the pseudonymous presentation and teleological suspension claim. Distinguish understanding the argument from endorsing its conclusion.

**7. Essay and revision, 24 hours.** Write 2,000–2,400 words on one methodological dispute or a bounded comparison between two assigned texts. Use genre-appropriate criteria: faithful description, interpretive evidence, and the inferential work done by the example.

**8. Assessment and delayed check, 8 hours.** Interpret a new passage and explain what would count as a successful objection within that method. Remedy caricatures by reconstructing the author’s question before comparing conclusions.

## Option D Social philosophy and applied justice

140 hours; prerequisites Courses 6–8 and preferably Course 11B. Core: Susan Moller Okin, Justice, Gender, and the Family, Basic Books 1989, chapters 1 and 8; bell hooks, Feminist Theory From Margin to Center, second edition, South End Press 2000, chapters 1–2; Iris Marion Young, “Five Faces of Oppression,” chapter 2 of Justice and the Politics of Difference, Princeton 1990; Elizabeth Anderson, “What Is the Point of Equality?”, Ethics 109, 1999, pp. 287–337; Peter Singer, Animal Liberation, 1975, chapter 1 “All Animals Are Equal,” in a clearly identified edition; Aldo Leopold, “The Land Ethic,” in A Sand County Almanac, 1949, complete; Charles Mills, “Ideal Theory as Ideology,” Hypatia 20, 2005, pp. 165–183. The two applied encounters are bounded tests of moral and political frameworks, not full courses in animal or environmental philosophy.

Outcomes: reconstruct a structural critique; distinguish distributive from relational concerns; analyze the public/private boundary; evaluate an extension of moral concern; use a concrete case without assuming away empirical complexity.

**1. Family and the scope of justice, 18 hours.** Okin 1 and 8. Identify the argument about the family and background assumptions of a theory of justice. Write a 700-word analysis of one claim.

**2. Feminist method and difference, 18 hours.** hooks 1–2. Reconstruct the critique of the movement and its audience; explain what follows for political method. Avoid treating any one author as all feminist philosophy.

**3. Structural injustice, 18 hours.** Young chapter 2. Apply two distinct forms of oppression to a documented case and explain the limits of the evidence. A fictional teaching case is acceptable if clearly labelled.

**4. Equality and relationships, 22 hours.** Anderson’s article. Map the objection to luck egalitarianism and the alternative. Write 800 words defending or challenging one inference with an original case.

**5. Beyond human institutions, 20 hours.** Singer’s chapter 1 and Leopold’s essay. Compare the unit of moral concern, the role of consequences, and conflicts within each approach. Do not make the approaches agree by removing their controversial commitments.

**6. Method and idealization, 12 hours.** Mills’s article. Audit an earlier case analysis for assumptions that conceal exclusion or historical conditions.

**7. Essay and revision, 24 hours.** Write 2,000–2,400 words on a single course dispute, including a documented or explicitly hypothetical case and the strongest objection. Separate empirical uncertainty from normative disagreement.

**8. Assessment and delayed check, 8 hours.** Analyze a changed case and interpret an unseen assigned passage. Readiness requires a defensible argument and careful scope, not adopting a prescribed political position.

# Course 13 Advanced seminar and research apprenticeship

160 hours. Complete Course 11 and one Course 12 option first. The packet-specific prerequisites determine intellectual readiness: Packet A needs Mind and Language, Packet C needs Social Philosophy, while Packet B builds on Chinese Philosophy and Ethics and accepts any completed area course. Choose one of the three defined seminar packets below. The default is AI Language and Consciousness after Mind and Language. Comparative Moral Cultivation follows the Chinese option; Social Knowledge and Justice follows the Africana option and Social Philosophy. Aesthetics or Pragmatism can serve as the selected area while the learner specializes further in the completed global tradition. Additional advanced specialisms are extensions, not promised unfinished course options.

This course supplies the research skills that the capstone assumes: constructing a manageable question, distinguishing an author’s argument from your improvement, locating and assessing scholarship, building a debate map, defending an interpretation, and revising a substantial paper. It is not merely an extra reading list.

## Packet A AI language and consciousness

Prerequisite: Course 12A. Core readings: revisit the exact Turing and Searle passages already assigned; Emily Bender, Timnit Gebru, Angelina McMillan-Major, and Shmargaret Shmitchell, “On the Dangers of Stochastic Parrots: Can Language Models Be Too Big?”, FAccT 2021, pp. 610–623; David Chalmers, “Could a Large Language Model Be Conscious?”, Boston Review, 9 August 2023, complete essay based on the 2022 NeurIPS talk; Patrick Butlin and colleagues, Consciousness in Artificial Intelligence Insights from the Science of Consciousness, arXiv 2308.08708, version 3, 22 August 2023, sections 1, 2.1–2.5, and 4.1. Record the version and actual PDF pages read; this is a substantial technical selection of about forty report pages, not a brief article. Use the report’s Glossary for terms encountered, especially computational functionalism, access/phenomenal consciousness, recurrence, and global workspace. No programming or architecture implementation is required. During the forty-four-hour analysis phase, choose one theory contrast for close reconstruction rather than try to make every technical model the subject of your paper. If two initial reading sessions reveal unfamiliar terminology still preventing reconstruction, spend the next session on the report’s own definitions and diagrams and extend the logged module estimate if needed. These are sources for argument, not established answers about any particular current system.

Central question: what, if anything, do linguistic behavior, architecture, and functional organization justify about understanding or consciousness? Distinguish performance, meaning, understanding, access consciousness, phenomenal consciousness, and moral status. Do not infer one from another without an argument. Use Bender and colleagues for their actual claims about language-model risks and meaning, not as a comprehensive theory of consciousness.

Default paper prompt: “Does the argument I select establish a necessary condition for consciousness, or only a reason for uncertainty?” Alternative: “What would count as evidence against the systems reply in a specified model of understanding?” A tool’s fluent answer is an object of analysis, not empirical proof of its internal state. Any informal demonstration must be labelled illustrative, with no claim that a few prompts constitute a scientific experiment.

## Packet B Comparative moral cultivation

Prerequisite: Course 11A and Course 6. Core primary passages: Aristotle, Nicomachean Ethics III.1–5 and X.6–9; Mengzi 2A2, 6A1–10, and 7A1–21; Xunzi chapters 19, 22, and 23. Chapter 22 (“Correct Naming,” Hutton pp. 236–247) is included because the modern agency debate relies on its account of mind, approval, and desire. Retain the complete translations already selected. Read Bryan W. Van Norden, “Mengzi and Xunzi: Two Views of Human Agency,” International Philosophical Quarterly 32.2 (1992), pp. 161–184, and David E. Soles, “The Nature and Grounds of Xunzi’s Disagreement with Mencius,” Asian Philosophy 9.2 (1999), pp. 123–133, DOI 10.1080/09552369908575494. These offer different diagnoses of the disagreement; Soles does not claim there is no substantive difference. Treat their disagreement as something to establish through reading, not an invented opposition.

Central question: what relation between dispositions, deliberate effort, habits, and social practices explains ethical cultivation? First establish what each text argues in its own setting. Then identify a precise common problem and a genuine limit of comparison. A three-author historical summary is not yet a seminar argument.

Default paper prompt: “Does my proposed interpretation make the Mengzi–Xunzi disagreement substantive rather than merely verbal?” Alternative: “Can Aristotle’s account clarify one feature of cultivation without distorting the Chinese text?” Include a short translation note and one counterexample that tests your actual conclusion.

## Packet C Social knowledge and justice

Prerequisite: Course 11B and Course 12D. Core: Charles Mills, “White Ignorance,” in Shannon Sullivan and Nancy Tuana, editors, Race and Epistemologies of Ignorance, SUNY 2007, pp. 13–38; Miranda Fricker, Epistemic Injustice chapters 1 and 7; revisit Young’s “Five Faces of Oppression” and Anderson’s “What Is the Point of Equality?”; add Kristie Dotson, “Tracking Epistemic Violence, Tracking Practices of Silencing,” Hypatia 26, 2011, pp. 236–257. Exact editions and legal reading routes are listed in the ledger.

Central question: when does a pattern of credibility, interpretation, or exclusion constitute an epistemic wrong, and what follows for institutions? Distinguish interpersonal and structural claims, diagnosis and remedy, and philosophical argument from empirical evidence about the case.

Default paper prompt: “Does one specified framework explain an exclusion case that another misses, and why?” Alternative: “Does a proposed remedy answer the identified epistemic wrong without creating a different injustice?” Use a documented case with restrained factual claims or a clearly hypothetical case. Do not make allegations about identifiable people as an exercise.

## Seminar sequence for every packet

**1. Entry and question selection, 10 hours.** Explain two relevant arguments without notes; choose a packet; state three possible questions and narrow one. Read the core sources’ abstracts/introduction/conclusion for orientation. Output: a 400-word question note and an explicit list of scope exclusions.

**2. Primary-text and core-scholarship analysis, 44 hours.** Read the packet in full at its stated scope. Produce four 500-word reconstructions and two argument maps. Identify disagreements over definitions, evidence, inference, and purpose. These short tasks become material for the paper rather than separate polished publications.

**3. Research methods and current debate check, 22 hours.** Use the reference protocol below. Find four candidate scholarly sources, read their abstracts and introductions, then select two for full reading within a combined target of about forty to sixty pages. At least one should offer a serious challenge; at least one should be relatively recent at the time you take the course. If the search does not yield a defensible recent source, document that result instead of choosing a weak one to satisfy a date rule. Save citations, stable links, a short reason for selection, and notes distinguishing what was read from what was only indexed.

**4. Debate map and prospectus, 16 hours.** Write a 1,000-word map of three positions and a 600-word prospectus with a provisional thesis, objection, method, and feasible evidence base. The goal is a live question within the chosen literature, not proof that no one has ever had your idea.

**5. Seminar paper draft, 24 hours.** Write 2,500–3,500 words. Cite exact passages and explain the contribution of each secondary source. Mark uncertainties and unresolved objections rather than burying them. No AI-generated assessed prose.

**6. Critical response and revision, 24 hours.** Seek one substantial reader response if feasible; otherwise use a source-bound critical AI read and label its limits. Write a 500-word response to the strongest objection, revise the paper, and explain the main changes. A disagreeing review is not something to average away.

**7. Independent defense and transfer, 10 hours.** Give a ten-minute explanation and answer fifteen to twenty minutes of new questions, or use an accessible text equivalent. Complete an independent 800-word analysis of a fresh related case with sources visible but no live assistance.

**8. Capstone readiness portfolio, 10 hours.** Audit citations, select representative work, identify remaining weaknesses, and propose a capstone question. A later short transfer check is included. Remediate the weakest prerequisite before committing to the project.

Readiness for the capstone requires a manageable question, accurate source use, a defensible argument with a serious objection, evidence of independent performance, and a feasible access plan. A high word count or a large bibliography cannot compensate for a central error.

# Course 14 Independent research capstone

180 focused hours; complete the common courses, global tradition, selected area, and advanced seminar, or document equivalent demonstrated prerequisites. Begin only when the research apprenticeship has produced a viable question and source access. The default product is a 5,000–7,000-word research paper, not a dissertation-length survey. It should make a modest, identifiable contribution: clarify a distinction, improve an objection, defend an interpretation, apply an argument to a carefully delimited new case, or show why a tempting comparison fails.

This local capstone length and workload are design choices. Universities use varied senior seminars, independent projects, essays, and theses. A longer thesis can be a valuable future project but is not automatically a better finish for this learner’s goals.

## Milestones

**1. Proposal and feasibility, 16 hours.** Write 800–1,000 words containing the question, significance, provisional claim, method, strongest anticipated objection, source needs, and exclusions. State why the question can be answered within the paper. The proposal is ready only when essential sources can actually be read.

**2. Literature and source dossier, 26 hours.** Build an annotated bibliography of the sources that genuinely bear on the question. A likely working set is eight to twelve relevant items, including sources already studied in the advanced seminar; relevance governs the count. Read the portions that bear on the question, not automatically eight to twelve whole books. Plan roughly forty to seventy pages of additional close reading in this phase, plus rechecking earlier notes and writing annotations. If the argument requires more new reading, enlarge the logged budget or narrow the question rather than pretend that a whole source has been read. For each, record a complete citation, legal access route, exact relevant pages/sections, the claim used, and whether you read the whole item or only a defined portion. Include foundational arguments, a serious competing view, and current scholarship where relevant. Verify every quotation.

**3. Argument plan and trial section, 20 hours.** Make a detailed outline and write a 1,000-word central section. Test the main inference before writing the entire paper. If it fails, revise the question rather than hide the failure in background exposition.

**4. Full first draft, 40 hours.** Write the complete paper in your own words. Separate exposition from defense, and make the contribution visible. Include citations and a provisional bibliography; do not postpone all sourcing to the end.

**5. Critical review, 16 hours.** Request a focused human review if available and chosen; otherwise arrange a skeptical source-bound review and label it provisional. Ask about the most serious interpretive error, the weakest inference, and the strongest objection. Write a response plan explaining which suggestions you will accept and why.

**6. Substantive revision, 32 hours.** Reorganize and rewrite where needed. Resolve citation issues, check every significant attribution, and remove claims beyond the evidence. Keep an explicit limitations section where appropriate. A revision memo explains the three most important changes.

**7. Presentation and defense, 12 hours.** Prepare a ten-to-fifteen-minute explanation and answer twenty-to-thirty minutes of unscripted questions or an equivalent accessible format. Include a challenge not rehearsed during drafting and an explanation of what would change your conclusion. Revise again if the defense exposes a substantive error.

**8. Final portfolio and delayed synthesis, 18 hours.** Submit the revised paper, bibliography, assistance declaration, revision memo, and selected earlier artifacts. Write a 1,000-word reflection comparing an early and late argument, identifying actual development and remaining gaps. After a pause, explain the project and apply its central distinction to a fresh case. These activities, including the final source audit, are within the 180-hour estimate.

## Final portfolio

Include the early baseline paragraph; Course 1 essay and revision; representative independent logic work; one historical interpretation; one ethics/political paper; one knowledge/science paper; one context-sensitive global-tradition paper, with translation analysis where relevant; the advanced seminar paper; the capstone; and concise records of independent tasks and dialogue. Select evidence rather than collect every note into an unreadable archive.

Use the shared criteria, with stronger expectations for research scope and handling alternatives. Every essential dimension must be independently competent. An unresolved central source error, invalid pivotal inference, inability to explain the paper, or unavailable essential evidence requires further work. A competent capstone may conclude that the initial claim was too strong. Recognizing a limit can be a substantive achievement.

If the project is too broad, narrow the question before adding hours. If the sources are inaccessible, change to a defensible adjacent question while preserving the learning goal, with the change documented. If a qualified reviewer is unavailable, finish the work and label the assessment honestly. Never claim publication-level originality, external certification, or equivalence to a particular university thesis without the evidence to support it.

## Optional longer thesis route

After this capstone, a learner who wants deeper specialist work may expand to 8,000–12,000 words with additional sources, external supervision if possible, and a new workload estimate based on actual capstone pace. This is a separate extension decision. It is not silently added to the required 180 hours and is not necessary for completing the recommended route.

# Reference and research protocol

A course that asks for one checked scholarly interpretation includes a small research exercise, not an unspecified additional reading list. Allocate two to four hours within the stated module or essay budget: locate a relevant scholarly reference, read the section actually needed, verify its primary-text citation, and record how it affects your interpretation. You are not expected to read an entire encyclopedia entry or monograph because it appears in a bibliography.

Use the Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy for orientation and bibliographies, PhilPapers for bibliographic discovery, publisher/journal pages and university repositories to verify publications, and your library catalogue for access. Search engines locate sources; search snippets are not substitutes for reading them. No API key or custom retrieval software is required. A source can be bibliographically verified while its full text is inaccessible; record that distinction.

For a substantial research task:

1\. Write the question in one sentence and list two or three relevant concepts and author names

2\. Read the relevant section of a scholarly reference entry, not automatically the entire entry

3\. Trace a cited primary passage and one significant scholarly disagreement

4\. Verify title, author, publication venue, date, and page/section scope at the publisher, journal, institutional repository, or the text itself

5\. Read the actual argument before citing it as support; distinguish a quotation, paraphrase, and your own inference

6\. Follow one backward citation and one later citation or recent related paper to test whether the discussion has changed

7\. Keep only sources that do argumentative work, and record dissenting evidence rather than search only for support

A source note contains: full citation; stable link or catalogue identifier; edition/translation; pages actually read; question addressed; argument in your own words; a quotation only if necessary; your objection; and how you may use it. Verify every quotation against the actual edition. If a text’s standard numbers are absent, cite its actual book/chapter and printed page instead of inventing a crosswalk. Exact wording from a modernized adaptation must be attributed to that adaptation, not presented as an unaltered translation.

For changing fields such as AI, the advanced seminar’s current-debate search is intentionally part of the learning task. The core packet supplies a complete starting argument structure; the later search teaches independent research. Recheck the field when you reach it, potentially years after this curriculum’s 2026 evidence snapshot.

## Reusable assignment forms

Argument reconstruction: state the question, quote or identify the exact passage, number the explicit premises and conclusion, bracket supplied premises, explain each inferential link, note a rival interpretation, and give one objection. End by distinguishing the author’s argument from any improvement you propose.

Short interpretation: state the passage’s role, explain the key term in context, support the reading with two precise textual observations, consider one plausible alternative, and justify your preference. Criticism follows accurate interpretation.

Essay proposal: question, provisional answer, main supporting argument, strongest objection, primary passages, and one explicit exclusion. Keep it short enough to test before drafting.

Feedback memo: identify the most consequential strength; the two most consequential problems; the passages where they arise; and the next revision task. A style edit is not a substitute for diagnosing an invalid inference or central misreading.

Independent-task record: prompt, allowed sources, conditions and time used, assistance declaration, outcome-level evidence, errors requiring repair, and the next fresh task. Scores without evidence are not useful records.

Source/edition record: author and title, edition and translator, actual file or book used, stable reference system, differences from the recommended edition, and unresolved access/citation issues.

## Tutor instructions

Begin each session by asking what course/module is active, which exact passage or task is in view, how much rested focused time is available, and what the learner has already attempted. Use the current course outcomes and declared source edition. Do not assume unseen text is available or that a source claim is correct because another AI stated it.

Teach one consequential issue at a time. Offer a brief orientation or a different worked example when needed, then ask the learner to perform the next step. Ask for the reason linking premises to conclusions, the best objection, or evidence for an interpretation. Distinguish factual error, contested interpretation, philosophical disagreement, and unclear prose. Never grade a position by whether it agrees with your own.

For a draft, diagnose before rewriting. Do not generate replacement assessed paragraphs. For a proof, identify the rule or scope problem without supplying the whole solution immediately. For a historical/global text, attend to genre, translator choices, intellectual context, and comparison limits. Do not require that all philosophy imitate one analytic essay format.

For assessment, state permitted materials, use a fresh task, request evidence for each criterion, and record uncertainty. Hold out solutions until the independent attempt. Escalate unresolved source or interpretation disputes to the text, a reliable specialist source, or a qualified human. Do not call a changed role name, a fresh chat, or model agreement independent certification.

End with the learner’s two-sentence account of what changed, the one artifact saved, and the next concrete step. Do not add surprise required readings, expand the course indefinitely, or use optional enrichment as an unstated gate.

## Pedagogical evidence used with limits

> • Writing and interpretation: philosophy-department guides and the APA’s statements emphasize precise questions, charitable interpretation, reasoned argument, discussion, and expression. They support the selected outcomes, while the detailed developmental ladder is this program’s design \[E01–E05\]
>
> • Argument visualization: Cullen and colleagues’ 2018 quasi-experimental study supports careful argument-analysis practice with feedback. It bundled intensive teaching and at least ten weekly hours of practice in a selective-college seminar. Its results cannot be promised for this five-to-seven-hour self-study program \[E06\]
>
> • Retrieval and spacing: Karpicke and Blunt’s science-text experiments and Cepeda and colleagues’ spacing study justify revisiting material and attempting recall before correction. They do not establish that flashcards alone teach philosophical interpretation or prescribe one fixed schedule \[E07–E08\]
>
> • Worked examples and revision: research on fading examples and writing feedback supports scaffolded practice and substantive revision, with important differences in population and subject matter. The particular paragraph-to-capstone sequence remains a local choice \[E09–E10\]
>
> • AI: different studies find different results depending on tutor design and setting. High-school mathematics and bounded undergraduate physics experiments do not validate AI assessment of a philosophy curriculum. Therefore assistance declarations, fresh tasks, source checks, and optional human review are central \[E11–E12\]
>
> • Global and methodological breadth: professional guidance supports serious engagement with diverse traditions and cautions against breadth without depth or treating traditions as ornamental additions. It does not require the exact Chinese/Africana choices made here \[E02, E13\]

## What changed from the earlier fifteen unit plan

The previous material supplied a useful introduction, roles, rubrics, and reading anchors, but fourteen units were still outlines. The new route replaces those placeholders with finite readings, ordered modules, writing tasks, assessment evidence, and explicit hours. It also changes the structure rather than preserving a number for its own sake.

> • Preserved: close primary reading, argument reconstruction, counterexamples, repeated writing and revision, oral explanation, source verification, and cumulative review
>
> • Added: beginner academic skills, required science, a sustained global tradition, Indian and Islamic/Jewish encounters in the common courses, explicit research training, access checks, and a fully specified first teaching sequence
>
> • Corrected: Republic’s missing justice answer, Kant’s missing causation locator, overbroad medieval and nineteenth-century claims, unsupported logic chapter boundaries, and confusion between a collected aid and the assigned primary text
>
> • Rebalanced: combined knowledge/reality and mind/language courses state their limits; aesthetics, social philosophy, and pragmatist/phenomenological work become real choices rather than every specialist area becoming compulsory
>
> • Replaced: rigid numerical AI mastery thresholds with criterion-level evidence; a mandatory long thesis with a bounded research capstone; and “always struggle first” with scaffolded practice followed by increasing independence
>
> • Disclosed: multi-year workload, unavailable editions, translation differences, limits of AI grading, uncertain transfer of educational findings, and the difference between self-study completion and external review

The research and review record at the end summarizes the completed critique rounds, model roles, substantive corrections, and limits. No number of review passes proves correctness; material problems must be checked against the relevant evidence.

# Assessment blueprints and operational rubric

## Course specific evidence

The substantial paper assesses sustained argument, interpretation, attribution, organization, and revision. The independent task samples an essential outcome outside the paper’s narrow topic. The conversation checks responsiveness and the ability to explain one’s own reasoning. These are parts of the already budgeted assessment, not extra examinations. At the start of the final assessment module, make a small evidence grid with one row per outcome group in the blueprint below and columns for module artifact, independent fresh task, and any needed repair. Use the named module outputs as the first evidence. Reserve a source-open written session of about ninety minutes for three or four short fresh prompts spanning the remaining groups, plus a twenty-minute explanation or accessible equivalent; formal logic uses its longer mixed-problem session. If the paper already supplies independent evidence for a group, do not duplicate a large essay. Use the rest of the course’s assessment hours for feedback, repair, citation checking, and a fifteen-to-thirty-minute delayed sample of the least secure group. These times are within the existing module allocation. Keep the assessment prompts separate from coached practice. A row without both kinds of evidence remains unresolved; do not infer coverage from a total score.

> • Course 1: independently reconstruct a short passage, classify an inference with explanation, supply a counterexample, and answer an objection to the essay. Accurate reconstruction and attribution are essential; polished style cannot compensate for confusing the author with an opponent
>
> • Course 2: sample English-to-symbol translation, interpretation of a formula, truth-table/countermodel reasoning, a truth-functional proof, a first-order proof, and explanation of a rule restriction. Each family must be demonstrated independently; formal correctness alone does not establish faithful symbolization
>
> • Course 3: interpret a Republic passage relevant to justice, distinguish an Aristotelian explanatory claim, and explain one Hellenistic argument or practice. The paper supplies depth in one of these; independent tasks cover the remaining outcomes
>
> • Course 4: reconstruct one existence argument, interpret a soul/time/negative-theology passage not central to the paper, and explain a contextual difference between two authors. Do not assess detailed doctrines outside the assigned excerpts
>
> • Course 5: reconstruct a Cartesian transition, explain an interaction or substance argument, and analyze an induction/ideas claim. Select the independent tasks from topics the paper did not cover, with exact passages available
>
> • Course 6: analyze a changed case under two normative approaches, explain the selected metaethical distinction, and interpret a primary passage. An application needs an explicit theory-to-verdict inference, not a label
>
> • Course 7: evaluate a new knowledge or testimony case, distinguish two accounts of self/identity, and reconstruct an argument about responsibility. The paper plus independent tasks must supply evidence for all three clusters
>
> • Course 8: analyze an authority/liberty case, explain a distributive argument, and interpret a structural critique. Require the learner to identify any empirical premise that needs outside evidence
>
> • Course 9: explain Kant’s objective-succession argument, interpret one selected post-Kantian passage, and compare methods without confusing genealogy, social explanation, and transcendental argument. If the paper is on Kant, the independent passage is post-Kantian, and conversely
>
> • Course 10: distinguish confirmation from deduction, analyze a falsification or explanation case, compare realism/empirical adequacy, and identify a value/uncertainty issue in the scientific packet. The case paper and short tasks jointly cover these outcomes
>
> • Course 11A: interpret a fresh passage from one of the five assigned thinkers, reconstruct a dispute with another, justify one translation-sensitive claim, and explain a limit of cross-tradition comparison
>
> • Course 11B: interpret a fresh Du Bois/Fanon passage, reconstruct the person/community debate, and diagnose a methodological mistake in comparison. The paper cannot substitute for understanding the other core debate

> • Course 12A: analyze a reference/description example, explain functionalist reasoning, and assess a machine-intelligence or consciousness argument outside the paper topic
>
> • Course 12B: distinguish classification and evaluation, reconstruct a taste/category argument, and analyze an unfamiliar artwork whose relevant context is supplied
>
> • Course 12C: explain two different methods in their own terms, interpret a fresh passage, and show what a relevant objection would target
>
> • Course 12D: distinguish structural, relational, and distributive claims, evaluate an applied case, and identify a limitation of idealization
>
> • Course 13: evaluate the research question and source dossier, assess the seminar paper, test a new related case, and ask why a rival interpretation or source was rejected
>
> • Course 14: check proposal, research integrity, sustained argument, substantive revision, unscripted response, and delayed transfer. Completion requires the integrated evidence, not simply submission of a long document

## Observable rubric anchors

Question and claim. Developing work names a broad topic or a claim that shifts. Competent work answers the actual question with a specific, appropriately qualified thesis. Strong work explains why the precise disputed issue matters and anticipates a relevant limit. Repair: turn a topic into one question, then write a one-sentence answer and one explicit exclusion.

Interpretation and context. Developing work captures part of the view but confuses a term, speaker, premise, or historical setting. Competent work reconstructs the relevant position fairly using identifiable passages. Strong work handles a genuine ambiguity or rival interpretation through evidence. Repair: annotate the exact passage and compare two readings before evaluating either.

Reasoning. Developing work offers relevant reasons but leaves an important inferential gap. Competent work explains how the reasons support the conclusion, identifies crucial assumptions, and qualifies the conclusion appropriately. Strong work tests the pivotal assumption and explains why the argument survives a serious alternative. Repair: make the missing premise explicit and test it with a counterexample.

Objection and reply. Developing work states disagreement or attacks a weaker view. Competent work presents a serious challenge to the actual argument and answers it directly, including by revising the claim. Strong work shows how the objection changes or deepens the defended view and acknowledges residual difficulty. Repair: identify exactly which premise, inference, interpretation, or scope claim is challenged.

Evidence and attribution. Developing work gives vague references or blurs paraphrase and inference. Competent work makes claims traceable to sources actually read, identifies editions and assistance, and distinguishes quotation from interpretation. Strong work explains why the chosen sources are appropriate and what their limits are. Fabrication or unattributed borrowing is not merely “developing”; it requires correction before assessment can proceed.

Organization and expression. Developing work has a recognizable argument but unclear paragraph roles, terminology, or transitions. Competent work lets a reader follow the reasoning in ordinary language. Strong work makes difficult material precise and economical without hiding uncertainty. Repair: reverse-outline each paragraph’s function, remove irrelevant material, and repair one recurrent ambiguity at a time.

Revision. Developing work changes wording while leaving the central problem untouched. Competent work makes substantive, reasoned changes and explains their effect. Strong work prioritizes criticism well, justifies accepting or rejecting advice, and identifies what remains unresolved. A new independent task is still needed to show that the learning transfers beyond the revised artifact.

Dialogue. Developing work repeats prepared claims or misses the question. Competent work listens, answers directly, gives reasons, and can revise under an objection. Strong work identifies the issue beneath a question and draws a useful distinction. Assess philosophical responsiveness rather than confidence, fluency, speed, accent, or performance style.

Formal work. Developing work succeeds with hints but makes recurring syntax, scope, semantics, or rule errors. Competent work solves new varied problems independently and explains the strategy and restrictions. Strong work transfers between English, formal notation, proof, and countermodel, explaining where a formalization is contestable. A proof checker validates only the calculus it implements.

Not every dimension applies to every small assignment. A précis should not fail for lacking an objection if none was requested. Apply the dimensions relevant to the task, and raise complexity gradually rather than mechanically changing a numerical cutoff.

## Outcome evidence grids

Use these grids to fill the course record; they are the promised mapping, not an instruction to invent another exam. “Module evidence” means the learner’s named output, including its revision and assistance declaration. It can count as satisfactory supported practice. “Fresh sample” means a new, uncoached question, case, or passage selection completed independently; it need not be a text never encountered. Use a different case or inferential task from the module exercise. The quoted minute budgets are planning allocations within the course’s final assessment module, not strict speed grades. Adjust time for access needs without changing the intellectual task.

For every grid, allow the assigned primary texts and a citation sheet; prohibit AI content help, answer keys, and prewritten responses during the fresh samples. Logic permits only the agreed rule sheet, with checker feedback withheld until submission. Use the already assigned twenty-minute dialogue or accessible typed exchange to clarify a response, not to introduce untaught content. If a grid row already uses that dialogue, count it once rather than add another. Courses 13–14 use their own stated defense timings. A fifteen-to-thirty-minute delayed task samples the least secure group on a new case after roughly four to six weeks or two later modules. For Course 1, the broader six-hour consolidation package already includes that delayed task and corrections. Record actual minutes against the prior course. A failed group gets a targeted fresh retest within remaining assessment time; additional remediation is recorded as extra time if the estimate is exceeded.

These grids sample the central outcome groups and reading methods. They do not claim twice-tested mastery of every sentence, author, or topic in a survey. To finish, each row needs a satisfactory module artifact and an independently competent fresh sample; a narrow essay cannot erase a missing row. Source integrity, accurate interpretation, and relevant reasoning are required across the evidence.

The capstone’s evidence grid is its milestone sequence rather than another exam. Record relevant sections and reviewer observations for each group. A missing group requires work on that milestone rather than a generic extra test. The 180 hours already include preparation, defense, source audit, and delayed synthesis. Any additional reading required by a changed question is logged explicitly rather than hidden in this estimate.

# Reading access and edition crosswalk

The curriculum specifies what to read; the existing study collection supplies many, but not all, of those readings. A catalogue record verifies that a publication exists. A readable copy establishes access. An older translation, partial anthology, modernized aid, and preferred scholarly edition are different things. The distinctions below are part of the plan, not optional fine print.

Use the existing collection’s CAT identifiers to locate files in its index. No original collection files have been changed. Preserve notices and keep copyrighted study copies private. Online availability does not grant redistribution rights, and public-domain status can differ by jurisdiction. The plan does not require purchases, enrollment, account creation, or uploading your work.

## How to acquire only what you need

For the next course, search a local library catalogue by exact title, translator, and edition; check authorized electronic access; then ask about interlibrary loan or lawful chapter supply if eligible. Availability, fees, and loan duration vary. A borrowed anthology or collected edition can cover several assignments, but check the actual table of contents and passage extent before relying on it. A preview or catalogue listing is not the full reading.

Obtain the next one or two modules’ sources before beginning them. There is no reason to buy a shelf of books now. The initial course can start with the existing collection and free guides. If an indispensable text cannot be obtained, pause that module or choose a fully specified available route; do not silently replace the argument with an AI summary. The tutor may propose a pedagogically defensible substitution, but it must be explicitly documented with revised tasks and outcomes rather than called the original assignment.

The named scholarly edition is the default reference. Existing older full translations can support a first reading and may support assessed work when the relevant passage is clear and properly cited. Quote only the edition actually in hand. For a translation-sensitive dispute or a task depending on scholarly locators absent from the old copy, borrow the specified edition before that assessment. Compare translations when their difference affects the argument; do not make a modern edition a prestige requirement without a pedagogical reason.
